Description

StellarEvolutionTracks is a proposed supplement to Gravity from the Ground Up. The Chapter 8 program Star and the Chapter 20 program Neutron each compute one snapshot. This program puts time on the first of those snapshots, and a family of models on the second. Four modes share one command line: a single track, a Hertzsprung–Russell diagram with isochrones, a white-dwarf cooling curve, and a neutron-star mass–radius sequence.

The spine of the lesson is one sentence:

Mass sets the clock; composition sets the brightening; an equation of state that softens sets a maximum mass — and only one of those two maxima needs general relativity.

What this program is not. It is not MESA. In tracks two ODEs are integrated — central hydrogen depletion, then helium-core growth — and everything else on the track is a fit, a calibrated closure, or a rule. The remnant printed in the corner is a classification from the initial mass, except for a few low-mass stars whose own core hits this model’s cap. Modes wdcool and nsmr are separate calculations you start yourself; the program does not evolve a giant into a white dwarf or a core into a neutron star. Tracks stop at helium ignition. There is no mass loss, no AGB, no binary.
Integrated, fitted, or prescribed. When a beat writes an equation, the caption says which of those three it is. A one-zone star cannot derive its own core weighting, fuel reservoir, or radius growth; those three numbers are closures, not discoveries. Do not read a smooth curve as a solved interior.
Reading this page. Equations are rendered by MathJax from a public CDN. Without a network connection the raw \(\LaTeX\) remains visible as text. The program itself needs no internet access.

Before the first run

Unpack the program zip so you have a folder named StellarEvolutionTracks that contains main.py. Open a terminal in that folder. You need Python 3.10 or newer with NumPy and Matplotlib. If you do not already have that environment, follow the project-wide GFTGUX Python Setup Guide. Do not install packages into a system Python (PEP 668). Then:

python main.py --version
python main.py --mode tracks --mass 1.0 --no_postms

Every command on this page assumes that working directory. A run always opens a figure unless you pass --no_plot, which also requires --csvdir.

Beat 0 · The Sun is not a constant

A star in hydrostatic and thermal equilibrium would not change if its composition were fixed. The Sun’s is not. Four hydrogen nuclei become one helium nucleus, the mean molecular weight \(\mu\) in the core rises, and the luminosity follows.

python main.py --mode tracks --mass 1.0 --no_postms

Look at the luminosity history and the central-hydrogen panel. Read only the ZAMS, TAMS, and MS lifetime lines of the summary. You should see a star that starts near \(0.72\,L_\odot\) and ends near \(2.2\,L_\odot\) after about 9.7 Gyr. The yellow marker on the HR panel is the Sun today. Before reading on, write one sentence that explains why burning fuel makes the star brighter rather than fainter.

Mean molecular weights, fully ionised — derived
\[ \frac{1}{\mu}=2X+\tfrac34 Y+\tfrac12 Z,\qquad \mu_e=\frac{2}{1+X},\qquad Y=1-X-Z. \]

Solar \(X=0.70\), \(Z=0.02\) gives \(\mu\approx0.62\). A helium core with \(X=0\) has \(\mu\approx1.34\). That factor is the engine. Beats 1 and 2 turn it into a clock and three knobs.

Beat 1 · Mass sets the main-sequence lifetime

A heavier star has more fuel and a much higher burn rate. The burn rate wins. Put four masses on one diagram and read \(t_{\rm MS}\) from the printed table.

python main.py --mode hr --masses 1,2,5,10 --no_postms
python main.py --mode tracks --mass 10 --no_postms

The first figure is four short tracks leaving the dashed ZAMS. Use the second command only for its ZAMS line. Compare the Sun’s lifetime to the ten-solar-mass lifetime, and the two ZAMS luminosities. Homology says why \(L\) climbs so steeply, without solving the interior.

Homology, opacity \(\kappa=\kappa_0\rho^a T^b\), burning \(\varepsilon=\varepsilon_0\rho T^\nu\) — derived
\[ R\propto \mu^{\,p}M^{\,q},\qquad p=\frac{\nu-4+b}{D},\quad q=\frac{\nu-1+a+b}{D},\quad D=\nu+3+b+3a, \] \[ L\propto \mu^{\,4-b}M^{\,3-a-b}R^{\,b+3a}. \]

Electron scattering (\(a=b=0\)) collapses to \(L\propto\mu^4 M^3\), independent of the burning law. The summary prints the exponents for the opacity you chose. Homology places a power law, not a star. The program’s default ZAMS is an empirical piecewise fit, normalised so the zero-age Sun sits at \(0.72\,L_\odot\), \(0.89\,R_\odot\) — not at the present-day Sun, which has already brightened. Switch the anchor off with --homology when you want the pure power law on the same solar normalisation.

Beat 2 · Three closures a one-zone model cannot derive

The Sun in Beat 0 arrived with three numbers you did not choose. Turn each one. The first command is the reference.

python main.py --mode tracks --mass 1.0 --no_postms
python main.py --mode tracks --mass 1.0 --no_postms --core_weight 0
python main.py --mode tracks --mass 1.0 --no_postms --qc 0.10
python main.py --mode tracks --mass 1.0 --no_postms --expansion 1.0

Read TAMS and MS lifetime each time. One of those runs never reaches the terminal-age main sequence before the 15 Gyr stop; the summary will say so instead of printing a TAMS line. --core_weight decides how much the rising core \(\mu\) counts toward \(L\). --qc is the fraction of the star that ever burns; it moves the lifetime and not the terminal luminosity. --expansion is how much the radius is allowed to grow. Homology with electron scattering predicts no growth at all for a solar-type star; detailed models grow by about 70 per cent, which is why the default factor is 1.7.

Nuclear-burning equation — integrated
\[ \frac{dX_c}{dt}=-\frac{L(t)}{0.007\,c^2\,q_c M},\qquad L(t)=L_{\rm ZAMS}\left(\frac{\mu_{\rm eff}(t)}{\mu_{\rm eff}(0)}\right)^{4-b+(b+3a)p}. \]
The three closures
\[ \mu_{\rm eff}=\mu_{\rm env}^{\,1-w}\,\mu_{\rm core}^{\,w}, \qquad R(t)=R_{\rm ZAMS}\bigl[1+(f_{\rm exp}-1)\,s^{3/2}\bigr], \qquad s=1-X_c/X_0. \]

Defaults \(w=0.36\), \(q_c=0.15\) (rising slowly above \(1.2\,M_\odot\)), \(f_{\rm exp}=1.7\) are a joint solar calibration, not a fit to the figure. Below about \(0.35\,M_\odot\) the star is fully convective and a fixed \(q_c\) understates the fuel; the program warns there.

Beat 3 · Leaving the main sequence

When the centre is out of hydrogen the star does not stop. Hydrogen burns in a shell around a growing helium core. Drop --no_postms and let four masses finish.

python main.py --mode tracks --mass 1.0
python main.py --mode tracks --mass 1.5
python main.py --mode tracks --mass 2.0
python main.py --mode tracks --mass 5.0

On the HR panel the phases are coloured separately and the track ends at a star: helium ignition. For the Sun the post-main-sequence climb takes a fraction of the main-sequence time and several orders of magnitude in \(L\). Compare the Luminosity at end and Core mass at end lines of the three lighter stars. Then look at 5 solar masses: the regime is a Hertzsprung-gap crossing, the luminosity does not climb, and the crossing is a Kelvin–Helmholtz time, not a core-growth integration.

Below about 2 solar masses the helium core is degenerate. Degeneracy decouples the core from the envelope, so the luminosity is a function of core mass almost alone. That is why those three tips coincide, and why the tip of the red-giant branch is a standard candle in nature. The particular sixth-power law used here is a teaching closure, tuned to put the solar tip near \(2500\,L_\odot\). It is not the Paczyński AGB relation.

Core growth — integrated; giant luminosity — prescribed + closure
\[ \frac{dM_c}{dt}=\frac{L(t)}{0.007\,c^2\,X_{\rm env}}, \qquad L=\bigl(L_{\rm TAMS}^4+L_{\rm CMLR}^4\bigr)^{1/4}, \qquad \frac{L_{\rm CMLR}}{L_\odot}=2.3\times10^{5}\left(\frac{M_c}{M_\odot}\right)^{6}. \]
Hertzsprung-gap rule — prescribed; Hayashi line — fit
\[ L=L_{\rm TAMS},\quad \Delta t_{\rm gap}=t_{\rm KH}=\frac{GM^2}{R_{\rm TAMS}L_{\rm TAMS}}, \] \[ T_{\rm eff}=6560\,\mathrm{K}\;\bigl(L/L_\odot\bigr)^{-0.092}\bigl(M/M_\odot\bigr)^{0.10}. \]

A fourth closure, --core_efficiency (default 0.75), sets how large the helium core is at TAMS relative to the hydrogen reservoir that fed it. Away from the solar calibration, treat timescales as good to a factor of order unity and luminosities to tens of per cent. The shapes and the ordering of the timescales are the result.

Beat 4 · A cluster on one diagram

The stars of a cluster are born together. Today the heavy ones have left the main sequence and the light ones have not. Where the main sequence ends is the age.

python main.py --mode hr --isochrones 0.1,1,5,10
python main.py --mode hr --masses 0.6,0.8,1.0,1.2,1.5,2.0,3.0,5.0,8.0 --isochrones 0.1,1,5,10 --t_max 60

Each isochrone joins the points at which different masses have reached the same age. Find where it bends off the ZAMS: that is the turn-off. The first run cannot date a 10 Gyr cluster because the default 0.5 and 0.8 solar-mass tracks are still on the main sequence at the default --t_max of 15 Gyr. The second command lets those tracks finish. An isochrone here is linear interpolation in age along the tracks you asked for, not a modern library. The horizontal high-mass branches are Beat 3’s constant-luminosity rule, and the change of morphology at \(2\,M_\odot\) is a switch between two prescriptions.

Beat 5 · Where the track stops

Every track ends at helium ignition, or earlier for a low-mass star. The last lines of the summary say what the program will claim about the remnant. Read them before you believe the picture.

python main.py --mode tracks --mass 1.0
python main.py --mode tracks --mass 12
python main.py --mode tracks --mass 0.6 --t_max 200

The Sun is classified as a carbon–oxygen white dwarf near \(0.50\,M_\odot\); the 12-solar-mass star as a \(1.4\,M_\odot\) neutron star. In both cases the summary tells you the remnant is a classification from the initial mass. The third command is the exception: a \(0.6\,M_\odot\) star can grow a core to this model’s cap before helium ignition, and the summary then says the remnant came from the track. Hand the remnant on yourself — --mode wdcool --wd_mass 0.5 is roughly the Sun’s white dwarf; Beats 8–10 take a \(1.4\,M_\odot\) neutron star. Nothing between helium ignition and the remnant is computed.

Beat 6 · A star the size of the Earth

A white dwarf has no fuel. Electron degeneracy holds it up, and the heavier one is the smaller one.

python main.py --mode wdcool --wd_mass 0.6
python main.py --mode wdcool --wd_mass 1.2
python main.py --mode wdcool --wd_mass 1.4

The upper-left panel is the mass–radius curve. A dotted line marks the radius of the Earth; a dashed line marks the Chandrasekhar mass, \(1.456\,M_\odot\) at the default \(\mu_e=2\). Read the Radius line of each summary and ignore the other panels until Beat 7. Find the mass at which the star is the size of the Earth. Watch the radius as you approach the dashed line. Then change composition with --mu_e 1.0 and watch both the radius and the limiting mass move.

Degenerate electrons + Newtonian structure — derived / integrated
\[ P=A_e\bigl[x(2x^2-3)\sqrt{1+x^2}+3\,\mathrm{asinh}\,x\bigr], \qquad \rho=\mu_e m_u\,\frac{8\pi}{3}\Bigl(\frac{m_ec}{h}\Bigr)^{3}x^{3}, \] \[ \frac{dm}{dr}=4\pi r^{2}\rho, \qquad \frac{dx}{dr}=-\frac{Gm\rho}{r^{2}}\Big/\frac{dP}{dx}. \]

As the electrons become relativistic the pressure softens from \(P\propto\rho^{5/3}\) to \(P\propto\rho^{4/3}\), and no equilibrium exists above \(M_{\rm Ch}=5.825\,\mu_e^{-2}\,M_\odot\). That constant is the \(n=3\) Lane–Emden mass, not a fit. The curve on the figure approaches it from below as central density goes to infinity. Coulomb corrections, finite temperature, and GR are all absent: this is the zero-temperature Chandrasekhar model.

Beat 7 · A white dwarf fades

With no nuclear fuel a white dwarf can only cool. The light is the stored heat of the ions. Cool one, then change what it is made of and what it is wrapped in.

python main.py --mode wdcool --wd_mass 0.6
python main.py --mode wdcool --wd_mass 1.0
python main.py --mode wdcool --wd_mass 0.6 --A_ion 4
python main.py --mode wdcool --wd_mass 0.6 --Z_env 0.02

The luminosity panel is flat at early times and then falls along a slope of \(-7/5\) on log–log axes. Read the Cooling age lines. Changing the mass moves the luminosity and does not move the core-temperature history. Changing \(A_{\rm ion}\) moves the heat capacity. Changing the envelope metals moves the Mestel coefficient, and moves it a lot. The clock is the envelope together with the ions; the core \(\mu_e\) that set the radius in Beat 6 does not enter the cooling law.

Mestel cooling — derived / integrated
\[ U=\frac{3}{2}\frac{k}{A m_u}M T_c, \qquad L=C\,M\,T_c^{7/2}, \qquad \frac{dT_c}{dt}=-\frac{2}{3}\frac{A m_u}{k}\,C\,T_c^{7/2}. \]

The late-time limit of that ODE is \(L\propto t^{-7/5}\). The early flattening is the memory of a finite starting temperature, not a numerical artefact. These are Mestel-model ages, not modern cooling ages: neutrinos, crystallisation, and convective coupling are all missing. The coefficient \(C\) is matched at the layer where the electrons become degenerate, and that layer is taken to be envelope material. That is why envelope \(X\) and \(Z\) set the clock and core \(\mu_e\) does not.

Beat 8 · An ideal neutron gas

Do to neutrons what Beat 6 did to electrons, but integrate the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff equations instead of Newton. This is the historical Oppenheimer–Volkoff calculation. It is a benchmark that is known to fail.

python main.py --mode nsmr --eos neutron

Three panels: mass against radius, mass against central density, compactness and surface redshift. The summary reports a maximum mass of \(0.71\,M_\odot\) at about 9.3 km. Compare that with the dashed observational line near \(2\,M_\odot\). The arithmetic is not the problem. A cold ideal neutron gas is not what neutron stars are made of. The factor-of-three shortfall is the cleanest demonstration in the program that nuclear repulsion, not degeneracy alone, holds a neutron star up.

Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff — integrated
\[ \frac{dm}{dr}=\frac{4\pi r^{2}\epsilon}{c^{2}}, \qquad \frac{dP}{dr}=-\frac{G\bigl(\epsilon/c^{2}+P/c^{2}\bigr)\bigl(m+4\pi r^{3}P/c^{2}\bigr)}{r^{2}\bigl(1-2Gm/rc^{2}\bigr)}. \]

Beat 9 · How stiff must nuclear matter be?

Beat 8 ended with an equation of state that is too soft. The default polytrope option stands in, crudely, with one stiffness --p_nuc and one index --gamma.

python main.py --mode nsmr
python main.py --mode nsmr --p_nuc 0.02
python main.py --mode nsmr --p_nuc 0.08
python main.py --mode nsmr --gamma 3.0 --p_nuc 0.04

Defaults \(\Gamma=2.5\), \(p_{\rm nuc}=0.04\) clear the \(2\,M_\odot\) line. Softening drops below it; stiffening rises above it and the notes may drop a model at the highest density. The last command flags *** ACAUSAL ***: the sound speed on the branch exceeds \(c\). A maximum mass obtained with an acausal equation of state cannot be trusted, however large it is. The index is refused outside \(4/3<\Gamma\le 5\): below \(4/3\) there is no stable polytrope, and at or below \(6/5\) there is no finite radius.

Stiffness at nuclear saturation — closure, not a nuclear EOS
\[ p_{\rm nuc}=\frac{P(\rho_{\rm nuc})}{\rho_{\rm nuc}c^{2}}, \qquad \rho_{\rm nuc}=2.7\times10^{17}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}. \]

Beat 10 · Newton versus Einstein

The white dwarf’s maximum mass and the neutron star’s are not the same kind of limit. Run the ideal neutron gas both ways.

python main.py --mode nsmr --eos neutron
python main.py --mode nsmr --eos neutron --newtonian
python main.py --mode nsmr --eos neutron --newtonian --rho_hi 1e21

The Newtonian run reports no turning point. It prints a largest sampled mass, not a maximum mass, and it withholds redshift and the Buchdahl bound, which are general-relativistic results. Raise the density and the sequence still does not turn over; the models walk inside their own Schwarzschild radii, a configuration Newtonian gravity has no way to refuse. The TOV sequence turns over at a modest compactness \(GM/Rc^{2}\approx0.11\), because pressure itself gravitates.

Newtonian gravity still has a Chandrasekhar-like limit for this gas, approached only as central density goes to infinity, and every model near it is already inside its horizon. The program does not apply a turning-point test to Newtonian sequences. A Newtonian polytrope is unstable for \(\Gamma\le 4/3\); the ideal neutron gas stays above that at every density, so Newton’s sequence climbs without turning over. General relativity raises the critical index. That is the difference the two figures are for.

On a relativistic mass–radius panel two reference curves are drawn: the horizon \(R=2GM/c^{2}\), and the Buchdahl bound \(R\ge 9GM/4c^{2}\) for static spherical isotropic fluid with density not increasing outward. Neither curve is drawn on a Newtonian run, where neither belongs.

Output

Every mode prints a summary and opens a Matplotlib window. A notes on this run block appears when the model has been pushed outside the range it can be trusted — an unfinished track, a mass outside the reliable band, a sequence with no turning point, an acausal equation of state. Read it.

With --outdir the same figure is saved as a timestamped PNG plus a same-stem .provenance.txt. With --csvdir the numerical table is written; the commented header records the version and the parameters the selected mode actually used. CSV output does not turn the figure off. --no_plot --csvdir PATH is the data-only combination.

On a track figure, phases are coloured separately and the remnant in the corner box is a label, not a point the curve reaches. If the star was still burning hydrogen at --t_max, the last symbol is an integration stop, never a TAMS. On an nsmr figure the horizon and Buchdahl lines, and any maximum-mass star, are drawn only for a TOV sequence that earned them.

Experiments

EXP-1 · First run

Read the Sun

Run --mode tracks --mass 1.0. Record \(L_{\rm ZAMS}\), \(t_{\rm MS}\), \(L_{\rm TAMS}\), and \(L\) at the tip. What fraction of the main-sequence life is over at 4.57 Gyr? What exactly do the last two summary lines claim about the remnant?

EXP-2 · Beginner

Four clocks

Run --mode hr --masses 1,2,5,10 --no_postms. From the printed \(t_{\rm MS}\) values, estimate the exponent in \(t_{\rm MS}\propto M^{-n}\). Why is it not exactly \(n=2\) even for electron-scattering homology?

EXP-3 · Beginner

The rim is a turn-off

Run --mode hr --isochrones 0.1,1,5,10 --t_max 60. Point to the 5 Gyr turn-off. Which two default tracks would have been missing that turn-off at --t_max 15?

EXP-4 · Beginner

Earth-sized, then smaller

Run --mode wdcool at 0.6, 1.0 and 1.3 solar masses. Tabulate radius in Earth radii. What happens as you approach the dashed line, and what does --mu_e 1.0 do to that line?

EXP-5 · Intermediate

Why the ideal neutron gas fails

Run --mode nsmr --eos neutron, then --mode nsmr. Record both maximum masses. Oppenheimer and Volkoff made no arithmetical error. What assumption is wrong, and in what sense is the polytrope still not a neutron star?

EXP-6 · Intermediate

Three closures, three effects

From Beat 2’s four commands, assign each knob to the quantity it moves: terminal luminosity, lifetime, or terminal effective temperature. Then set --homology and say which closure homology refuses to provide.

EXP-7 · Intermediate

The Mestel slope

Write a default cooling run to CSV. On log–log axes, fit \(\log L\) against \(\log t\) over the last decade in time, then over the whole curve. Which fit is close to \(-7/5\), and what is the star doing in the flat early part?

EXP-8 · Intermediate

Support is not heat

Hold --wd_mass 0.6 --mu_e 2 and change --A_ion among 4, 12, 14 and 16. Which number moves, radius or cooling age? Then change only --mu_e and say which number moves now.

EXP-9 · Advanced

Handoff, not evolution

For \(1\,M_\odot\), list the phases skipped between helium ignition and the white dwarf, then hand \(0.50\,M_\odot\) to wdcool. For \(12\,M_\odot\), ask whether each nsmr equation of state contains a stable \(1.4\,M_\odot\) model. Mark every number you use as integrated, prescribed, or skipped.

EXP-10 · Advanced

Where Newton walks through a horizon

Compare --eos neutron with and without --newtonian. At what compactness does the TOV sequence turn over? What does the Newtonian sequence do instead when you raise --rho_hi? Why does pressure that gravitates produce a turning point at modest compactness?

Domain of Validity

  • Accepted stellar masses are 0.08–120 \(M_\odot\). The closures are a teaching model from about 0.35–15 \(M_\odot\). Below 0.35 the star is fully convective; above 15, mass loss and radiation pressure dominate. The program warns outside the band and will still run.
  • Tracks stop at helium ignition. Core helium burning, the AGB, dredge-up, mass loss and envelope ejection are absent. The remnant is a label except on the low-mass branch described in Beat 5.
  • Absolute ages and luminosities are approximate. Use the program for scalings, turn-offs, and limiting masses.
  • White dwarfs are the zero-temperature Chandrasekhar model plus classical Mestel cooling. Coulomb corrections, GR, neutrinos, crystallisation and convective coupling are absent. Cooling starts at \(3\times10^{7}\,\mathrm{K}\) because that is where neutrinos would have already dropped out.
  • Neither neutron-star equation of state is nuclear physics. The Fermi gas is the historical OV benchmark. The polytrope is a tunable stiffness. Rotation, crusts, hyperons and quark matter are absent.
  • Isochrones are interpolants through the tracks you asked for. The morphology change at \(2\,M_\odot\) is a switch of prescription.
  • Binarity is omitted on purpose. Most helium white dwarfs of the mass Beat 5 can produce are understood, in nature, as stripped by a companion.

Related Programs

  • Star — one snapshot of stellar structure. Prerequisite for the language of Beats 0–3. This program supplies the time that snapshot does not have.
  • Neutron — one central density. Mode nsmr is the family of those models and the turning point.
  • GravitationalWaveSources — takes a compact remnant as an inspiral source. Neighbour, after Beat 9.
  • Binary — two-body orbits. Background for the binary evolution this program refuses to pretend to compute.
