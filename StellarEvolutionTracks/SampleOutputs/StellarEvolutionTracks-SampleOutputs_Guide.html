Purpose. The Help page teaches StellarEvolutionTracks as a sequence of eleven beats: each is a short argument, a command whose figure is the evidence for it, and only then the equation the figure has earned. This guide shows the figure and the printed summary for each of those commands, as version 1.7.0 produces them, so that you can check a run of your own against a known-good result. It is a companion to the beats and not a substitute for them: run the command first, look at the panel the beat names, and only then compare. The four modes (a single evolutionary track, an HR diagram with schematic isochrones, white-dwarf structure and cooling, neutron-star mass–radius sequences) are separate teaching calculations, not one continuous evolution from a main-sequence star to its remnant, and the beats say where each one hands over to the next.

1. The Sun on the main sequence (Beats 0 and 2)

python main.py --mode tracks --mass 1.0 --no_postms --outdir ./figures --dpi 150

Figure 1. A one-solar-mass track from the zero-age main sequence to the terminal-age main sequence. --no_postms stops it there, so the figure holds only the hydrogen-burning story.

What it shows

  • Upper left: the path in the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram. The black dot is the zero-age point, the open square the terminal-age point, and the yellow dot the Sun today, which the track passes close to. The effective-temperature axis runs from hot on the left to cool on the right.
  • Upper right and lower left: luminosity and radius against age. Both climb steadily; a star that is using up its fuel gets brighter, not dimmer (the one-substitution argument of Beat 0).
  • Lower right: the central hydrogen fraction falling as the helium core grows.
  • The dotted vertical line marks the terminal-age main sequence.

Headline values

  • ZAMS: 0.72 L☉, 0.89 R☉, 5636 K.
  • Main-sequence lifetime: 9.73 Gyr; TAMS luminosity 2.198 L☉ at 5716 K, a brightening by a factor of about 3.1.
  • Helium core at the TAMS: 0.1123 M☉.
  • Homology exponents printed for the default (electron-scattering) opacity: L ~ mu^4.000 M^3.000.
  • The summary ends with “Post-MS: not computed” and says the remnant is a classification from the initial mass, not an integrated result.

2. Mass sets the main-sequence lifetime (Beat 1)

python main.py --mode hr --masses 1,2,5,10 --no_postms --outdir ./figures --dpi 150

Figure 2. Four main-sequence tracks on one diagram. Each starts on the dashed zero-age main sequence, with its mass in solar masses printed beside the starting point, and drifts upward as the star brightens.

What it shows

  • The zero-age main sequence as a dashed line and the four tracks leaving it at very different luminosities.
  • How steeply luminosity climbs with mass: the 10 M☉ track starts more than three orders of magnitude above the Sun's (a factor of about 4400).
  • The Sun's present position, for orientation.

Headline values

  • Main-sequence lifetimes printed in the summary table: 9.73 Gyr at 1 M☉, 1.361 Gyr at 2 M☉, 0.1685 Gyr at 5 M☉, 0.03469 Gyr at 10 M☉.
  • The Sun's lifetime divided by the ten-solar-mass lifetime: about 280.
  • ZAMS luminosity at 10 M☉ (from python main.py --mode tracks --mass 10 --no_postms): 3220 L☉, against the Sun's 0.72 L☉: a factor of about 4472 for a factor of ten in mass.
  • With --opacity kramers the printed exponents change to L ~ mu^7.769 M^5.462; the star reaches 6.293 L☉ at the end of the main sequence and lives 6.976 Gyr.

The figure is the picture of the first command; the second command in the Help is used only for its printed summary. The homology exponents are derived; the zero-age anchors this figure starts from are empirical fits, which the Help tags accordingly.

3. The Sun leaves the main sequence (Beat 3)

python main.py --mode tracks --mass 1.0 --outdir ./figures --dpi 150

Figure 3. The same one-solar-mass star with the post-main-sequence phase switched on: main sequence, subgiant branch and red-giant branch to helium ignition.

What it shows

  • The phases are coloured separately on the HR diagram (main sequence, subgiant or Hertzsprung gap, red-giant branch) and the track ends at a star symbol, helium ignition.
  • After the terminal-age point the luminosity rises by about three orders of magnitude in roughly a fifth of the time the star spent on the main sequence.
  • The radius grows from about 1.5 to about 160 solar radii; the helium core climbs from 0.112 to 0.47 M☉.
  • The program integrates central-hydrogen depletion and shell-burning core growth and uses empirical anchors and calibrated closures for the rest; it stops at helium ignition, so the eventual white-dwarf entry is a classification from the initial mass, not a computed endpoint.

Headline values

  • ZAMS: 0.72 L☉, 0.89 R☉, 5636 K.
  • Main-sequence lifetime 9.73 Gyr; TAMS luminosity 2.198 L☉; helium core at the TAMS 0.1123 M☉.
  • Post-main-sequence regime: degenerate red-giant branch; duration 1.988 Gyr, total age at the end 11.72 Gyr.
  • Core mass and luminosity at the endpoint: 0.4700 M☉ and 2479.2 L☉.
  • Schematic remnant: carbon-oxygen white dwarf, about 0.503 M☉, not integrated.

4. A 5 M☉ star crosses the Hertzsprung gap (Beat 3)

python main.py --mode tracks --mass 5.0 --outdir ./figures --dpi 150

Figure 4. A five-solar-mass track. After the terminal-age main sequence the model crosses to the Hayashi-line temperature at constant luminosity; there is no red-giant branch in this model.

What it shows

  • A horizontal line on the HR diagram from the terminal-age point to helium ignition: the luminosity does not change during the crossing.
  • A main sequence of well under a fifth of a billion years, against nearly ten billion for the Sun, and a crossing thousands of times shorter still.
  • The vertical spike at the end of the radius history: the star swells by a large factor in a very short time.
  • CNO burning (ν = 16) is selected automatically above 1.2 M☉, as the title of the figure says.

Headline values

  • Main-sequence lifetime 0.1685 Gyr; TAMS luminosity 868.9 L☉.
  • Post-main-sequence regime: Hertzsprung-gap crossing; duration 0.0002388 Gyr (about 0.24 Myr).
  • Luminosity at the end 868.94 L☉, equal to the TAMS luminosity: a prescription, not a result.
  • Schematic remnant: carbon-oxygen white dwarf, about 0.939 M☉.

The abrupt change of morphology at 2 M☉ (compare the previous figure) is a switch between two prescriptions, and the Help says so; it is not a discovery about stars.

5. A cluster on one diagram (Beat 4)

python main.py --mode hr --masses 0.6,0.8,1.0,1.2,1.5,2.0,3.0,5.0,8.0 --isochrones 0.1,1,5,10 --t_max 60 --outdir ./figures --dpi 150

Figure 5. Nine mass-dependent tracks with isochrones at 0.1, 1, 5 and 10 Gyr. The enlarged 60 Gyr limit lets every track, including the 0.6 M☉ one, reach the terminal-age main sequence.

What it shows

  • The zero-age main sequence and tracks from 0.6 to 8 M☉, each labelled with its mass at the zero-age point.
  • Each isochrone, with its own colour, line style and diamond markers, joins the points at which stars of different masses have reached the same age. Where it bends away from the zero-age main sequence is the turn-off.
  • The turn-off moving down and to the right, toward lower masses, as the age grows.
  • The nearly horizontal high-mass branches, which are the constant-luminosity prescription of Beat 3.

Headline values

  • Nine tracks; main-sequence lifetimes from 45.05 Gyr at 0.6 M☉ to 0.05769 Gyr at 8 M☉.
  • Turn-off masses in this run: 6.285, 2.289, 1.252 and 0.991 M☉ at 0.1, 1, 5 and 10 Gyr.
  • With the default masses and the default 15 Gyr limit (the Help's first command, python main.py --mode hr --isochrones 0.1,1,5,10) the printed turn-offs are 6.285, 2.289 and 1.260 M☉, and for 10 Gyr “age outside t_MS(M) range”, because the lowest-mass tracks are still on the main sequence at 15 Gyr.

The isochrones are interpolants through a handful of simplified tracks, not a modern isochrone library. The small change of the 5 Gyr turn-off between the two runs (1.260 against 1.252) is what Beat 4 asks you to interpret: the turn-off mass is read from the table of main-sequence lifetimes, and adding masses to that table changes the interpolation slightly.

6. Where the track stops (Beat 5)

python main.py --mode tracks --mass 0.6 --t_max 200 --outdir ./figures --dpi 150

Figure 6. A 0.6 M☉ star needs a large --t_max to get anywhere. Its own post-main-sequence integration reaches the core-mass cap of this schematic model before helium ignition.

What it shows

  • Unlike the tracks of the previous figures, this one does not end at a helium-ignition star symbol; the annotation says it ends without helium ignition and names the remnant as this track's own endpoint.
  • A very long main sequence, followed by a post-main-sequence phase longer than the Sun's whole main sequence.

Headline values

  • Main-sequence lifetime 45.05 Gyr; post-main-sequence duration 14.11 Gyr; total age at the end 59.15 Gyr.
  • Core mass at the end: 0.4200 M☉, which is the model's core-mass cap.
  • Schematic remnant: helium white dwarf, about 0.420 M☉, and the summary states that it comes from the track's own integration and not from the generic mass-only classification.

The program links stages; it does not evolve through them. Nothing between helium ignition and the remnant is computed, and Beat 5 sends you to --mode wdcool and --mode nsmr to take the remnant on yourself. The low-mass exception above is itself a bookkeeping endpoint, not a computed envelope ejection, and the real helium white dwarfs of these masses are generally understood to be products of binary interaction, which the program does not simulate.

7. A white dwarf: structure and Mestel cooling (Beats 6 and 7)

python main.py --mode wdcool --wd_mass 0.6 --outdir ./figures --dpi 150

Figure 7. A 0.6 M☉ carbon–oxygen white-dwarf model combining a cold Chandrasekhar structure calculation with classical Mestel cooling. Beat 6 uses the upper-left panel; Beat 7 uses the other three.

What it shows

  • Upper left (Beat 6): the cold white-dwarf mass–radius relation, with the star marking this model, a dotted line at the radius of the Earth and a dashed vertical line at the Chandrasekhar limit. More massive white dwarfs are smaller, and the radius drops steeply as the mass approaches the limit.
  • Upper right (Beat 7): the cooling luminosity, flat at early times and then falling along the dashed −7/5 line of the asymptotic Mestel law on log–log axes.
  • Lower left: the core and surface temperatures against cooling age.
  • Lower right: the cooling path far below the main sequence on the HR diagram.

Headline values

  • Requested and recovered mass: 0.6000 M☉; Chandrasekhar limit for μe = 2: 1.4563 M☉.
  • Radius: 8840.9 km = 1.388 Earth radii = 0.01271 R☉.
  • Central density 3.2070 × 109 kg m−3; mean density 4.1217 × 108 kg m−3; surface gravity 1.0187 × 106 m s−2.
  • Core temperature 3.000 × 107 K → 3.000 × 106 K; effective temperature 25254 K → 3368 K.
  • Cooling age: 5.6235 Gyr by RK4 and 5.6235 Gyr from the exact formula; relative difference 1.560 × 10−7.
  • Mestel coefficient C = 1.2840 × 10−31 (SI), computed from the envelope's composition.

The cooling age does not depend on the white-dwarf mass in this model (the 0.6 and 1.0 M☉ rows agree, though the luminosities differ), because both the luminosity and the stored heat are proportional to the mass. What moves the clock is the ionic heat capacity (--A_ion) and the envelope through the Mestel coefficient (--Z_env). The core μe sets the structure only: changing --mu_e alters the radius but leaves the cooling age as it was.

This is a strong demonstration of ideal degeneracy and the Mestel power law, but the quoted ages are model ages. Real white-dwarf cooling also involves neutrino losses, crystallisation, and convective coupling, none of which is modelled, and the Mestel matching layer is taken to be envelope material, an idealisation the Help states and quantifies.

8. An ideal neutron gas (Beat 8)

python main.py --mode nsmr --eos neutron --outdir ./figures --dpi 150

Figure 8. The historical Oppenheimer–Volkoff calculation: an ideal degenerate neutron gas integrated through the full general-relativistic TOV equations, sampled over 40 central densities.

What it shows

  • Left: the mass–radius relation, with the stable branch (solid), the unstable branch (dashed), a star at the maximum mass, and a dashed red line at the roughly 2 M☉ benchmark set by the heaviest precisely measured pulsars. The two steep lines are the horizon and the Buchdahl bound (Beat 10).
  • Middle: mass against central density, with the turning point starred. Beyond it the mass falls and the models are unstable.
  • Right: compactness and surface redshift along the sequence, against the Buchdahl bound.
  • The maximum mass falls far short of the benchmark line, which is the point of the beat: the calculation is right, the assumption of an ideal gas is not.

Headline values

  • Maximum mass: 0.7100 M☉, at a radius of 9.313 km.
  • Central density there: 3.3304 × 1018 kg m−3.
  • Compactness GM/Rc2: 0.1126; surface redshift: 0.1360.
  • Sound speed: 0.3653 c at the maximum, which is also its peak on the branch (causal).

What supports a real neutron star is the pressure of strongly interacting nuclear matter, of which degeneracy is only one contribution. The polytrope option of the next two figures stands in for that, crudely, with one adjustable stiffness.

9. How stiff must nuclear matter be? (Beat 9)

python main.py --mode nsmr --outdir ./figures --dpi 150

Figure 9. A relativistic TOV sequence using the program's default toy stiff polytrope, Γ = 2.5 and pnuc = 0.04, sampled over 40 central densities.

What it shows

  • The same three panels as Figure 8, now with a maximum mass above the 2 M☉ benchmark line.
  • The turning point of mass against central density, which identifies the maximum mass for this cold, non-rotating, one-parameter sequence.
  • How the stiffness of an equation of state sets the scale of neutron-star masses and radii, and how the stable branch bends back toward smaller radii at the highest densities.

Headline values

  • Toy polytrope: Γ = 2.5 and pnuc/(ρnucc2) = 0.04.
  • Maximum mass: 2.1754 M☉.
  • Radius and central density there: 11.692 km and 1.5013 × 1018 kg m−3.
  • Compactness GM/Rc2: 0.2747; surface redshift: 0.4898.
  • Sound speed: 0.8364 c at the maximum, which is also its peak on the branch; causal throughout.

Stiffening the equation of state raises the maximum mass and enlarges the radius. At --p_nuc 0.02 the maximum falls below the benchmark line; at --p_nuc 0.08 it rises well above it, and the notes block reports that one model at the highest central density was dropped because the star fell inside its own horizon, so read the notes. The last row is the subject of the next figure.

The TOV equations are integrated, but the chosen Γ-law is a tunable illustration, normalised at one density, and not a realistic nuclear equation of state. The polytrope index is restricted to 4/3 < Γ ≤ 5, and --gamma 1.3 and --gamma 1.2 are refused with a message that says why.

10. An acausal equation of state (Beat 9)

python main.py --mode nsmr --gamma 3.0 --p_nuc 0.04 --outdir ./figures --dpi 150

Figure 10. The same calculation with Γ = 3.0. The annotation on the middle panel and the summary both flag the sound speed as exceeding the speed of light.

What it shows

  • The maximum mass is larger than for the default, but the equation of state that produces it is not physically admissible: the sound speed reaches more than the speed of light.
  • The annotation on the middle panel reads “acausal!” and the terminal line ends with *** ACAUSAL ***.
  • Several of the highest-density models were dropped because the star fell inside its own horizon; the notes block lists each one.

Headline values

  • Maximum mass: 2.6736 M☉, at 12.349 km.
  • Sound speed: 1.0717 c at the maximum, which is also its peak on the branch, above 1 and therefore flagged ACAUSAL.
  • Models kept: 36 of 40.

The lesson of Beat 9 is that a maximum mass obtained with an acausal equation of state cannot be trusted, however large it is. Causality is a separate requirement from stability and from the existence of a surface; the polytrope range 4/3 < Γ ≤ 5 exists for those last two, not for causality.

11. Newton versus Einstein (Beat 10)

python main.py --mode nsmr --eos neutron --newtonian --outdir ./figures --dpi 150

Figure 11. The ideal neutron gas of Figure 8 integrated with Newtonian gravity. The figure's own title calls it a controlled failure, for comparison with the TOV result.

What it shows

  • No turning point: the mass is still rising at the highest central density sampled, so the program reports a largest sampled mass and not a maximum mass.
  • No redshift and no Buchdahl bound, which are results of general relativity and are withheld for a Newtonian run.
  • Right: GM/Rc2 against mass, with the line at 0.1 where Newtonian gravity is in trouble. The sequence runs well past it.

Headline values

  • Largest sampled mass: 3.5423 M☉ (not a maximum), at 6.950 km and a central density of 5.0000 × 1019 kg m−3.
  • GM/Rc2 there: 0.7526; the notes block counts the models inside their own horizon.
  • The program applies its turning-point test only to TOV sequences and says it does not classify the radial stability of a Newtonian one.

Beat 10: raise the density

The Newtonian neutron gas does approach a limiting mass of the white-dwarf kind, about 5.73 M☉, but only as the central density goes to infinity, and every model near it lies far inside its own horizon. That limit belongs to the Newtonian equation and is not the mass of any star that could exist. What Newtonian gravity lacks is the neutron star's own maximum, the turning point at modest compactness that comes from pressure gravitating in general relativity.

Notes for evaluation

  • The Help separates integrated differential equations (ODE), algebraically derived results (DERIVED), empirical fits (FIT), calibrated closures (CLOSURE) and adopted rules (PRESCRIPTION) throughout, and each equation carries exactly one of the five tags. Several exercises turn on telling them apart.
  • The stellar tracks stop at helium ignition, or earlier for a low-mass star (Figure 6); they do not include core-helium burning, the asymptotic giant branch, mass loss or envelope ejection.
  • The compact-object modes are separate calculations. The program does not evolve a plotted progenitor continuously into a white dwarf or a neutron star; Beat 5 shows what it does and does not claim.
  • Every mode prints a detailed terminal summary and a notes block when the chosen parameters expose a model limitation. Read the notes block: in Figures 9 and 10 it is where dropped models and the acausality flag are explained.
  • --outdir PATH saves a timestamped PNG and a same-stem provenance text file in addition to displaying the figure, and --csvdir PATH writes timestamped, self-documenting numerical tables; --no_plot --csvdir PATH supports data-only investigations. The exercises that ask you to interpolate or fit read those CSV files.
  • A run of your own may differ from these figures in the last digit or two if your Python, NumPy or Matplotlib versions differ; the provenance file beside each figure records the versions used.

Image provenance and compression. All eleven figures were regenerated on 2026-09-20 from the supplied version 1.7.0 source files, build a28c8d1a30e0, with the commands shown above (plus --outdir and --dpi 150) and the program's default plotting settings. After generation, each PNG was converted without changing its dimensions to a 128-colour indexed palette for compact embedding. No plotted data, labels, annotations or layout were changed. The tables were built from the printed summaries of the same build, by a script that reads the numbers from the program's output rather than retyping them.

Known issues that touch these samples

The release notes for version 1.7.0 list the open defects (Part B, KB-1 to KB-5). Three of them concern the neutron-star mode as these figures use it (KB-1, KB-2 and KB-5), so they are stated here in the terms that matter for the figures above.

  • Polytrope radii depend on a surface cutoff. A polytrope's density falls to zero only slowly near its surface, and the program stops the integration when the density has fallen to 10−8 of its central value. For the default index this makes no visible difference. For soft indices (Γ below about 1.5) combined with a large --p_nuc, the reported radius is too small: in the cases measured for the audit, tightening the cutoff from 10−8 to 10−10 lengthened the radius by 0.9 to 9 per cent, well outside the 0.1–0.6 per cent that the Help's Beat 9 quotes for the warning. The warning fires only for Γ below 1.4, so a run at Γ = 1.40 (where the effect reached 7 per cent) prints none. The cutoff is not exposed on the command line. The maximum-mass models behind the headline values of Figures 8–10 (the ideal neutron gas, the default polytrope, and Γ = 3.0) are not affected, nor is the pnuc = 0.08 run in the Beat 9 table: the same tightening changes their radii by less than 0.0001 per cent (computed for each of the four).
  • The causality flag of a sequence whose maximum lies at the lowest density sampled. If you set --rho_lo so high that the mass is already falling at the first model, the program correctly says that no turning point was found, but it can still print a causality verdict computed from that single model. None of the figures above is such a run.
  • Cosmetic: on stiff sequences the legend of the mass–radius panel can cover the star that marks the maximum mass (it is partly hidden in Figure 9 and hidden in Figure 10). The maximum mass is still printed in the legend and in the summary.

The white-dwarf, track and HR modes are unaffected by these three. The other two open items (KB-3, an unvalidated array pairing in a public helper function; KB-4, a documentation sentence about Γ just above 4/3) do not touch any figure here.
