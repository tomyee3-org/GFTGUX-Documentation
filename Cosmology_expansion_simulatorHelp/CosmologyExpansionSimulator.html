Description

Cosmology_expansion_simulator integrates the Friedmann equation for a homogeneous, isotropic (FLRW) universe filled with matter, radiation, spatial curvature, and a dark-energy fluid, and tracks the resulting scale factor \(a(t)\), Hubble parameter \(H(t)\), fractional energy-density budget \(\Omega_i(a)\), and deceleration parameter \(q(t)\) from deep in the radiation era to a chosen point in the future. It answers, numerically and for any cosmology you specify, the question every introductory cosmology course eventually asks: how old is the universe, and how does the answer depend on what the universe is made of?

One program covers three calculations, selected with --mode: a single expansion history (evolve), an overlay of several named or custom cosmologies with an age comparison (compare), and a systematic scan of the age of the universe across one cosmological parameter (age) — reproducing, among other things, the historical "age crisis": the Einstein-de Sitter model (flat, matter-only, \(\Omega_{m0}=1\)) could be too young for the oldest globular clusters. A lower matter density makes the universe older either by dropping flatness (an open, low-density, matter-only universe, no \(\Lambda\) at all, is old enough) or, if flatness is imposed instead, by adding a compensating dark-energy component to make up the difference. Either route by itself does not require dark energy — only the flatness-preserving route does, and only because flatness was assumed going in. What actually made dark energy hard to avoid was a separate line of evidence — the 1998 discovery, from Type Ia supernovae, that the expansion is accelerating (\(q_0<0\)), which no matter-only model, open or flat, can produce. This program can demonstrate both points side by side (EXP-2 and EXP-6).

Exact equations; conditional model; numerical results. This program evaluates the FLRW background equations for homogeneous, isotropic, separately conserved components and solves the resulting expansion history numerically. Given the stated energy content, the first Friedmann equation, the second (acceleration) Friedmann equation, and the fluid continuity equation are exact GR consequences of the FLRW model — not fitted. But three different claims are easy to blur together, and only the first is defensible without qualification: (1) the FLRW equations are exact consequences of General Relativity under the model's assumptions; (2) the chosen energy content and the CPL dark-energy ansatz are an exact description of nature (they are not — see below); (3) this program's finite-precision numerical output is exact (it is not — it carries ordinary RK4 truncation, root-finding, and quadrature error, typically small but never zero; see "How accurate is this, then?" below). The one deliberately phenomenological ingredient is the dark-energy equation of state \(w(a)=w_0+w_a(1-a)\) (the Chevallier–Polarski–Linder, or CPL, parametrization): nobody knows what dark energy is, so its equation of state is measured, not derived, and this program lets you dial it in and see the consequences.
What this program is, and what it does not model. This is a homogeneous-and-isotropic background calculation — it has no perturbations, no structure formation, no photons decoupling to make a cosmic microwave background, no primordial nucleosynthesis chemistry, and no inflationary epoch. \(a\to0\) is treated as a mathematical limit of the classical Friedmann equation, not a claim about what actually happened at the Big Bang, where quantum gravity is expected to take over. Use the program to build intuition for how the geometry and material content of an FLRW spacetime control its expansion history and age — not as a cosmological-parameter-fitting pipeline.

Physical Background

The Cosmological Principle and the FLRW Metric

On sufficiently large scales the universe looks, by observation, the same in every direction (isotropic) and from every location (homogeneous). The unique metric consistent with that symmetry is the Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker metric,

FLRW metric
\[ds^2=-c^2dt^2+a(t)^2\left[\frac{dr^2}{1-kr^2}+r^2\left(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right],\]

where \(a(t)\) is the dimensionless scale factor (normalised to \(a=1\) today) and \(k\in\{-1,0,+1\}\) fixes the spatial geometry: open (hyperbolic), flat (Euclidean), or closed (spherical). All of the geometry and all of the dynamics are carried by the single function \(a(t)\); this program's entire job is to compute it.

Three Familiar Relations, Two of Them Independent

Substituting the FLRW metric into the Einstein field equations with a perfect-fluid stress-energy tensor yields three familiar relations for \(a(t)\): the first Friedmann equation, the second (acceleration) Friedmann equation, and a fluid continuity equation — of which only two are independent (each pair implies the third, a consequence of the same Bianchi identity that makes the Einstein tensor's divergence vanish). The first equation is the one usually integrated; the second is an independent statement about the acceleration, given the first. This program's \(q(a)\) diagnostic evaluates that second equation algebraically from the same \(\Omega_i(a)\) the first equation already produced, so agreement between them checks that the two formulas are being applied consistently — it is not, by itself, an independent check that the numerical integration of \(a(t)\) is correct, since a systematically wrong trajectory would still satisfy both algebraic formulas at every point along it. EXP-11 has you build the genuinely independent check, by numerically differentiating the integrated \(a(t)\) itself and comparing that to the algebraic \(q(a)\).

Redshift

A photon's wavelength stretches with the universe: \(1+z\equiv a_0/a=1/a\) in this program's convention (\(a_0=1\) today). Redshift is simply a relabelling of scale factor, but it is the observationally natural one, and results are quoted in both throughout.

Geometry Used to Determine Destiny; Now It Doesn't

In a universe with no dark energy, the classical result is clean: a closed universe (\(k=+1\), equivalently \(\Omega_{\rm total}>1\)) always recollapses, while an open or flat universe expands forever. Once a dark-energy component is admitted, that clean correspondence breaks: a flat or even open universe with enough phantom dark energy (\(w<-1\)) can still be torn apart in finite time (a "Big Rip"), while a closed universe with enough positive cosmological constant can expand forever, never recollapsing. Geometry alone no longer determines fate; the full energy budget does. This program is built specifically so that this point can be demonstrated rather than asserted — compare the closed and phantom presets in --mode compare.

A Short History of a Changing Answer

Einstein originally added the cosmological constant \(\Lambda\) to permit a static universe. He is often quoted as having called it his "greatest blunder" once Hubble's 1929 observations showed the universe expanding — a colorful anecdote (attributed secondhand by George Gamow) whose sourcing is disputed and which should be read as a memorable story, not a documented quotation. For most of the twentieth century, "which Friedmann model are we in?" was framed as measuring \(\Omega_m\) alone, with an implicit assumption \(\Omega_\Lambda=0\). The 1998 discovery, from Type Ia supernovae, that the expansion is accelerating (Riess et al. 1998; Perlmutter et al. 1999) reintroduced \(\Lambda\) — or something behaving like it — not by theoretical preference but because the data required \(q_0<0\), which no clean matter-only model can produce. What that dark-energy component actually is remains unknown; the CPL parametrization used here (Chevallier & Polarski 2001; Linder 2003) is the field's standard way of asking the data "how far from a cosmological constant can it be?" without committing to a specific model.

A live question, as of this writing (2026); check for updates. Baryon acoustic oscillation measurements from the DESI survey's second data release (DESI Collaboration 2025, Phys. Rev. D 112, 083515, arXiv:2503.14738), combined with the cosmic microwave background and Type Ia supernovae, show a preference for the quadrant \(w_0>-1\), \(w_a<0\) over a pure cosmological constant, at a significance that depends on which supernova compilation is combined with DESI (roughly \(2.8\)-\(4.2\sigma\) across published combinations, \(3.1\sigma\) for DESI+CMB alone) — suggestive, not yet a discovery, and an active area of debate over systematics. Note the sign of \(w_a\): the evolving_de preset here uses \(w_0=-0.8\), \(w_a=+0.3\) — positive \(w_a\), which moves \(w(a)\) toward less negative values into the past and is therefore in the opposite quadrant from DESI's hint. The separate desi_like preset (\(w_0=-0.7\), \(w_a=-1.0\)) sits in the sign quadrant DESI favors; it is chosen only to illustrate that quadrant and is not a fit to DESI's data or covariance.

The Flatness Problem, Briefly

For the standard radiation-and-matter content of our own universe, the curvature term dilutes as \(a^{-2}\) while radiation dilutes as \(a^{-4}\) and matter as \(a^{-3}\), so \(\Omega_k(a)\to0\) as \(a\to0\): the early universe looks flat almost by necessity of that scaling, which means a universe observed to be as flat as ours is today (\(|\Omega_{k0}|\lesssim10^{-3}\)) must have been flat to an extraordinary number of decimal places at, say, the Planck time. That fine-tuning puzzle is a major part of the historical motivation for cosmic inflation. This conclusion depends on radiation or matter — components that grow faster than curvature into the past — actually dominating there; it is not automatic for every CPL dark-energy or negative-density toy model this program will accept (an early-time-dominant dark-energy term, when the program detects one, can change which term wins as \(a\to0\)). This program can display the scaling directly (its \(\Omega_i(a)\) panel), but it does not model inflation and starts its clock well after any inflationary epoch would have ended.

Governing Equations

First Friedmann Equation

Exact, given the stated energy content EXACT / ODE
\[\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2=H(a)^2=H_0^2\,E(a)^2,\qquad E(a)^2=\Omega_{m0}a^{-3}+\Omega_{r0}a^{-4}+\Omega_{k0}a^{-2}+\Omega_{\rm DE,0}\,g(a).\]

This is the equation the program integrates, written as \(da/dt=aH_0E(a)\). Neither \(G\) nor \(c\) needs to appear: both are absorbed into the definitions of \(H_0\) and the critical density \(\rho_{\rm crit}=3H_0^2/8\pi G\), and every \(\Omega_i\equiv\rho_i/\rho_{\rm crit}\) is dimensionless by construction.

Closure Relation for the Curvature Density

Algebraic identity DERIVED
\[\Omega_{m0}+\Omega_{r0}+\Omega_{k0}+\Omega_{\rm DE,0}=1.\]

This is not a fitted constraint; it is the definition of \(\Omega_{k0}\equiv-kc^2/(H_0^2a_0^2)\) rearranged. If --omega_de is left unset, the program sets \(\Omega_{\rm DE,0}=1-\Omega_{m0}-\Omega_{r0}\) and \(\Omega_{k0}=0\) exactly (a flat universe by construction); giving --omega_de explicitly lets \(\Omega_{k0}\) come out positive (open), negative (closed), or zero, whatever the arithmetic implies.

Fractional Energy-Density Content

Algebraic identity, sums to unity DERIVED
\[\Omega_i(a)=\frac{\rho_i(a)}{\rho_{\rm crit}(a)},\qquad \Omega_m(a)+\Omega_r(a)+\Omega_k(a)+\Omega_{\rm DE}(a)=1\ \ \text{identically}.\]

The program plots all four and their sum. Because every \(\Omega_i(a)\) is defined as that component's density divided by the same total \(\rho_{\rm crit}(a)\propto E(a)^2\), the sum is 1 by algebraic construction at every scale factor where \(E(a)^2\) is safely nonzero, correct or not — it cannot, by itself, reveal a wrong \(a(t)\) trajectory or a wrong \(H(a)\), since both the numerator and denominator of every term would be equally wrong together. Treat the sum-equals-1 line as a check that the plotting and bookkeeping are self-consistent, not as a check on the physics. Exactly AT a turnaround, \(\rho_{\rm crit}(a)\to0\) while the individual densities stay finite, so every \(\Omega_i\) formally diverges there; the program reports that one row as NaN rather than a spurious finite value, and the sum-equals-1 identity should be read as holding at every regular row, not at the turnaround row itself.

Second Friedmann Equation (Acceleration Equation)

Exact, an independent GR result EXACT / DERIVED
\[\frac{\ddot a}{a}=-\frac{4\pi G}{3}\sum_i\left(\rho_i+\frac{3P_i}{c^2}\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad q(a)\equiv-\frac{\ddot a\,a}{\dot a^2}=\frac12\sum_{i=m,r,{\rm DE}}\Omega_i(a)\big(1+3w_i(a)\big).\]

Only genuine stress-energy components enter this sum; spatial curvature is a geometric bookkeeping term in the first Friedmann equation, not a fluid, and correctly contributes nothing here. \(q>0\) is decelerating, \(q<0\) is accelerating. Checks worth doing by hand: matter alone gives \(q=1/2\); radiation alone gives \(q=1\); a cosmological constant alone gives \(q=-1\) (de Sitter expansion).

Dark-Energy Equation of State (CPL)

Phenomenological ansatz FIT
\[w(a)=w_0+w_a(1-a).\]

\(w_0=-1,\ w_a=0\) is a cosmological constant. At \(a=1\) (today): \(w_0<-1\) contributes toward phantom behavior; \(-1<w_0<-1/3\) contributes toward quintessence-like behavior; \(w_0>-1/3\) does not by itself accelerate the expansion (the program checks \(1+3w_0\) and says so if that holds). These are classifications of the dark-energy component at \(a=1\), not of the whole history: whenever \(w_a\neq0\), \(w(a)\) evolves, and a model can cross between these regimes at other epochs (the evolving_de preset is a worked example — see EXP-9). "Canonical quintessence" also implies specific scalar-field microphysics that an arbitrary phenomenological \(w(a)\) does not represent; the label here means only "\(w\) in that numerical range." This linear-in-\(a\) form (Chevallier-Polarski-Linder) is the standard two-parameter way of letting the equation of state evolve without committing to a specific microphysical model of dark energy.

Continuity Equation and the Dark-Energy Density History

Exact consequence of energy-momentum conservation EXACT / DERIVED
\[\frac{d\ln\rho_i}{d\ln a}=-3\big(1+w_i(a)\big) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\rho_{\rm DE}(a)}{\rho_{\rm DE}(1)}=g(a)=a^{-3(1+w_0+w_a)}\,e^{-3w_a(1-a)}.\]

This follows from local energy-momentum conservation (equivalently, the Bianchi identity) for any equation of state \(w_i(a)\); matter (\(w=0\)) recovers the familiar \(\rho\propto a^{-3}\), radiation (\(w=1/3\)) recovers \(\rho\propto a^{-4}\). The closed-form \(g(a)\) above is the exact integral of this equation for the CPL ansatz specifically — exact given that ansatz, which is itself only a stand-in (tag FIT) for whatever dark energy actually is.

Early-Time Behaviour and the Big-Bang Time Offset

The numerical integration cannot literally start at \(a=0\). For an ordinary matter/radiation/curvature-dominated model, every one of those densities \(\rho_i\propto a^{-3(1+w_i)}\) diverges as \(a\to0\), so \(a=0\) is a genuine curvature (density) singularity of this classical description there, not a mere artifact of the coordinates chosen — no relabelling of \(t\) or \(a\) removes it. (This is exactly the kind of singularity general relativity itself signals it cannot resolve, which is why quantum gravity, not this program, is where the real \(a\to0\) physics would live.) But this is not universally true of every component the program supports: a cosmological constant's density stays exactly constant all the way to \(a=0\) (no divergence at all), a phantom dark-energy density (\(w<-1\)) actually tends to zero as \(a\to0\) (see the Big Rip section below), and the empty (Milne) universe — no matter, no radiation, no dark energy, pure curvature — is flat Minkowski spacetime in an unusual coordinate system: its \(a=0\) is a coordinate/light-cone boundary, not a curvature singularity at all (EXP-4). "Continuously connected to \(a=0\)" (what this program's Big-Bang connectivity check actually certifies — see the Algorithm section) means exactly that: a continuous, always-positive \(E(a)^2\) all the way down. It does NOT by itself mean every such model has a genuine curvature singularity there, and — the more consequential distinction — it does NOT by itself mean \(a=0\) is reached at a finite time in the past. That second question is answered separately, next.

So the integration instead starts at a small \(a_i\) and, for a model that genuinely reaches \(a=0\) at a finite past time, is given an analytic head start representing the time already elapsed since \(a=0\). As \(a\to0\), whichever density term grows fastest dominates: radiation (\(a^{-4}\)) beats matter (\(a^{-3}\)) beats a positive \(\Omega_{k0}\) contribution (open geometry, \(a^{-2}\)) — and, for an unusual choice of the dark-energy parameters, a CPL dark-energy term with \(w_0+w_a>1/3\) can beat even radiation, a case the program also detects and handles the same way. Each single-component case has an elementary exact solution:

Single-component analytic solutions DERIVED (exact under single-component domination)
\[\text{radiation: } a(t)=\sqrt{2H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{r0}}\,t}\ \Rightarrow\ t_i=\frac{a_i^2}{2H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{r0}}};\qquad \text{matter: } a(t)=\left(\tfrac32H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{m0}}\,t\right)^{2/3}\ \Rightarrow\ t_i=\frac{2}{3H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{m0}}}a_i^{3/2};\] \[\text{curvature (Milne): } a(t)=H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{k0}}\,t\ \Rightarrow\ t_i=\frac{a_i}{H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{k0}}}.\]

The program checks, after the fact, what fraction of the total early-time density budget (the sum of the absolute values of every component's term at \(a_i\), not the signed \(E(a_i)^2\) itself — a signed comparison can be fooled by cancellation from a negative component) the assumed-dominant term actually supplies; if that fraction is not extremely close to 1, it warns that --a_i should be made smaller. This offset is only asymptotically accurate: replacing the true CPL density by its \(a\to0\) limiting constant-\(w\) power law (when a dark-energy term is the assumed-dominant one) is exact only in that limit, not at the finite \(a_i\) actually used, and the dominance fraction is a check at the single point \(a_i\), not a bound on the integrated error over \((0,a_i)\) — treat it as a good practical diagnostic, not a proof. The Milne universe above is exact to floating-point round-off for this particular equation of motion for all time, not just early times, which makes it an unusually clean validation case — though that alone does not validate the integrator's handling of nonlinear rates, checkpoints, or turnaround events (see EXP-4).

Past-Eternal Models: Connectivity to a=0 Is Not the Same as a Finite-Age Big Bang

Cosmic time is related to the scale factor by \(dt/da = 1/(aH_0E(a))\). If the leading behaviour is \(E(a)^2\sim Ca^{p}\) as \(a\to0\) (with \(C>0\), already established by the connectivity check above), then \(dt/da\sim a^{-1-p/2}\), and the integral of this back to \(a=0\) converges — giving a genuine, finite elapsed time since the Big Bang — only if \(p<0\). Matter (\(p=-3\)), radiation (\(p=-4\)), and any curvature contribution (\(p=-2\)) all automatically satisfy this whenever one of them is the leading term, which is why an ordinary matter/radiation/curvature-containing model always has a well-defined finite age. The one way \(p\geq0\) can occur is a flat model (no matter, radiation, or curvature at all) whose dark-energy component's own leading exponent \(n_{\rm de}=-3(1+w_0+w_a)\) is itself \(\geq0\) — i.e. an effective early-time equation of state no more negative than \(w=-1\): a pure cosmological constant (\(p=0\), e.g. flat de Sitter) or phantom dark energy alone (\(p>0\)). Such a branch is continuously connected to \(a=0\) in exactly the sense certified above, but \(a=0\) itself lies at \(t=-\infty\): it is reached only in the infinite past, a past-eternal model, not a finite-age Big Bang.

The program reports this explicitly rather than silently computing a bogus age: past_status is "finite_big_bang" or "past_eternal" (or None if no component is present at all — not a case any valid run reaches). For a past-eternal model, age_today_gyr is deliberately None — an earlier version of this program instead reported \(H_0^{-1}\ln(1/a_i)\), the elapsed time since the arbitrary numerical starting point \(a_i\), AS IF it were the universe's age; that number grows without bound as \(a_i\to0\) and is not a physical age at all. It remains available, clearly labelled, as elapsed_ai_to_today_gyr, for anyone who specifically wants that bookkeeping quantity. The same logic applies to the Big Rip: big_rip_remaining_gyr (the time from today to the Rip) depends only on \(H_0\), \(w_0\), and \(\Omega_{\rm DE,0}\), never on \(a_i\), and is always reported when a Big Rip is certified — but the absolute \(t_{\rm rip}\), big_rip_gyr, additionally needs an absolute time origin and is left undefined for a past-eternal model for exactly the same reason age_today_gyr is. EXP-21 walks through both a flat de Sitter and a flat phantom example by hand.

Big Rip: A Finite-Time Future Singularity

Late-time, pure dark-energy-domination approximation DERIVED (approximate)
\[w_0<-1,\ w_a=0:\qquad t_{\rm rip}-t_0\approx\frac{1}{-\tfrac32(1+w_0)\,H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{\rm DE,0}}}.\]

For phantom dark energy the density grows as the universe expands (\(g(a)\to\infty\) as \(a\to\infty\)), and \(da/dt\propto a^{p}\) with \(p>1\), whose solution reaches \(a\to\infty\) at a finite time — unlike the ordinary exponential (de Sitter) expansion of an exact cosmological constant, which never does. The formula above solves that blow-up ODE exactly in the limit that matter and radiation are already negligible (an excellent approximation by the time it matters) and assumes constant \(w_0\) (\(w_a=0\)). \(w_0<-1\) alone is not sufficient for this to be the model's actual fate: a closed (\(\Omega_{k0}<0\), positively-curved geometry) phantom model can still recollapse before the phantom term ever comes to dominate, and \(w_0<-1\) alone says nothing about whether that happens. The program only reports \(t_{\rm rip}\) after checking, out to a scale factor beyond which the growing phantom term is analytically proven to outweigh the only quantity that could ever drive \(E(a)^2\) negative -- a negative \(\Omega_{k0}\) contribution (\(\Omega_{k0}<0\), i.e. closed spatial geometry), if present -- that no recollapse occurs first. Matter and radiation are never a threat on their own: both have non-negative coefficients and never grow relative to a phantom term that is itself growing, and a non-negative \(\Omega_{k0}\) is no threat either; only \(\Omega_{k0}<0\) can ever cause this check to fail. (The actual confirmation that \(E(a)^2\) stays positive throughout that range still uses the full Friedmann equation with every component together -- the \(\Omega_{k0}\)-only comparison merely picks how far out to check, conservatively.) This check has three possible outcomes, not two: it can certify no recollapse occurs first (a Big Rip is reported); it can find a genuine future recollapse instead (that recollapse is reported, beyond the requested \(a_{\max}\)/\(t_{\max}\), and no Big Rip); or, for a pathological parameter combination, it can fail to certify either outcome within this program's search budget, in which case neither a Big Rip time nor a future recollapse is reported and a warning says so plainly.

Approximations and Idealizations

This program has almost nothing to calibrate: given the density content you specify, the Friedmann equations are exact within the FLRW model. What follows is not a list of tuned parameters but a short, honest list of what has been assumed away.

The dark-energy equation of state FIT

As above: \(w(a)=w_0+w_a(1-a)\) is a two-parameter phenomenological family, not a derivation. It is flexible enough to represent a cosmological constant, quintessence, or phantom dark energy, and it is the field's standard tool for this purpose, but it is not itself a theory of dark energy.

Homogeneity and isotropy

The FLRW metric assumes the universe has no structure at all — no galaxies, clusters, or voids. Real large-scale structure is a perturbation on top of the background this program computes, and describing its growth is an entirely separate (and much harder) calculation that this program does not attempt.

A single effective dark-energy fluid

Matter and radiation here are single perfect fluids with fixed equations of state (\(w=0\) and \(w=1/3\)); baryons and cold dark matter are lumped into one matter term, and photons and (relativistic) neutrinos into one radiation term. This is standard practice for background-level calculations and does not affect \(H(a)\), but it means the model cannot separately track, say, neutrino mass effects or baryon-photon coupling before recombination.

No microphysics of the early universe

Big Bang nucleosynthesis, recombination and the cosmic microwave background, neutrino decoupling, and any inflationary epoch are entirely absent. The radiation density \(\Omega_{r0}\) is simply supplied as a number, not derived from first principles; the default, \(9.24\times10^{-5}\), is a representative effective teaching value for a photon-plus-three-neutrino-species budget under commonly assumed \(T_{\rm CMB}\), \(N_{\rm eff}\), and neutrino-mass conventions, paired here with a default \(H_0=70\) km/s/Mpc rather than derived self-consistently from it — treat it as a reasonable fixed number to start from, not a precision physical prediction.

How accurate is this, then? For a given \((\Omega_{m0},\Omega_{r0},\Omega_{k0},\Omega_{\rm DE,0},w_0,w_a)\), the numbers this program reports are accurate to the RK4 truncation error and root-finding/quadrature tolerances used internally — typically many significant figures at the default step size, and convergence with --step_frac is itself a worthwhile exercise (EXP-14). The physics content, not the numerics, is where the idealizations above live: the output is a numerical solution of the stated idealized model, subject to the approximations and convergence behavior described throughout this page — not an exact reconstruction of nature, and not exact even for the toy universe beyond the program's numerical precision. The interesting scientific question is always how well that toy universe matches the real one.

Algorithm

Mode evolve

  1. Validate all inputs (finite values, non-negative \(\Omega_{m0}\) and \(\Omega_{r0}\), \(0<a_i<1\) and \(a_i<a_{\max}\), step_frac in range).
  2. Resolve \(\Omega_{\rm DE,0}\) and \(\Omega_{k0}\) from the closure relation, forcing flatness if --omega_de was not given; reject the combination if the closure arithmetic itself overflows (finite inputs can still combine to a non-finite \(\Omega_{\rm DE,0}\)/\(\Omega_{k0}\)).
  3. Certify that \(a_i\) sits on a branch continuously connected back to the true Big Bang at \(a=0\) -- \(E(a_i)^2>0\) alone does not establish this, since a hidden forbidden interval (two nearby roots of \(E(a)^2=0\)) could sit strictly between \(a=0\) and \(a_i\), or \(E(a)^2\) itself could tend to a negative value as \(a\to0\) even though it is positive at \(a_i\). This proceeds in two stages: first, a closed-form analytic bound identifies a scale factor \(a_{\rm asym}\) below which \(E(a)^2\) is proven positive all the way to \(a=0\) (accounting for every density component's exact power-law behavior as \(a\to0\), including the dark-energy term's own constant prefactor when its leading power ties another component's); then the same certified search used elsewhere in this algorithm checks the remaining finite interval \([a_{\rm asym}, a_i]\) for a hidden root. A combination that fails this check is rejected with a clear error (not merely warned about) before any integration begins.
  4. Choose the early-time regime (radiation, matter, or curvature domination) and compute the analytic Big-Bang time offset \(t_i\); warn if the assumed-dominant term does not actually dominate \(E(a_i)^2\) to better than one part in a thousand. This is a narrower, separate question from the connectivity check above: it asks which term dominates numerically AT \(a_i\) (to judge the offset formula's accuracy), not which term dominates in the strict \(a\to0\) limit.
  5. Classify \(\texttt{past\_status}\) from the same leading exponent \(p_{\rm min}\) already identified by the connectivity check in the previous step: \(\texttt{"finite\_big\_bang"}\) if \(p_{\rm min}<0\) (matter, radiation, or curvature ever leading guarantees this), or \(\texttt{"past\_eternal"}\) if \(p_{\rm min}\geq0\) (only possible for a flat model with no matter, radiation, or curvature, whose dark-energy leading exponent is itself \(\geq0\)) -- see the Early-Time section's "Past-Eternal Models" discussion above for the derivation. This classification determines, once \(a=1\) is reached below, whether the elapsed time since \(a_i\) is reported as a genuine age_today_gyr or is withheld (and left available only as the explicitly-labelled, non-physical elapsed_ai_to_today_gyr) because no finite time origin exists for this model.
  6. Integrate \(da/dt=aH_0E(a)\) forward with fourth-order Runge-Kutta, using a Hubble-time-scaled step \(dt=\text{step\_frac}/H(a)\) — approximately a fixed fractional change in \(\ln a\) per step. This is a physically motivated variable step size, not a true error-controlled adaptive method: it carries no embedded local-error estimate, so accuracy should be checked by re-running at a smaller step_frac and confirming the results have converged (EXP-14).
  7. When the step that would straddle \(a=1\), or the final step toward --a_max, is found FAR from any turning point, do not merely interpolate between the two bracketing grid points (linear interpolation there would cap the effective accuracy at second order, regardless of RK4's fourth-order local accuracy). Instead, bisect the RK4 sub-step size itself, after first checking that the proposed upper bound actually brackets the target, so that a single RK4 step targets the requested scale factor to the stated numerical tolerance, inheriting RK4's full order.
  8. Continuously monitor, from the exact derivative of \(E(a)^2\), whether a turning point (\(H=0\)) is close ahead — \(da/dt\propto\sqrt{a_{\rm turn}-a}\) has an infinite derivative there, so RK4's usual error behaviour breaks down in the last few steps before it, however small the step size, and a single RK4 step aimed at a target close to that singularity is unsafe regardless of step size. This proximity check, together with a reactive check that catches \(E(a)^2\) having already gone negative as a safety net for a very coarse step_frac, triggers the search described in the following points; the search is extended slightly past \(a_{\max}\) as well, so that a root sitting at or just beyond \(a_{\max}\) — a boundary that can round to coincide with a genuine turnaround — is still found and handled by the accurate method below rather than being missed.
  9. The search that decides whether a genuine root of \(E(a)^2=0\) lies ahead has three possible outcomes, not two: it can find a bracketing pair of points with \(E(a)^2\geq0\) then \(E(a)^2<0\); it can certify, from a closed-form analytic bound on \(|dE(a)^2/da|\) (never from evaluating \(E(a)^2\) on a grid), that \(E(a)^2\) stays positive by a comfortable margin everywhere in the interval, so no root exists there; or, if the bound is too loose to decide either way even after bisecting the interval as far as floating-point arithmetic allows to subdivide, it can be genuinely indeterminate — a case the program treats as an error rather than silently guessing "safe," because a search that cannot prove positivity must not be reported as though it had. This closed-form bound is evaluated in ordinary float64 arithmetic (not a directed-rounding or interval-arithmetic scheme) and then deliberately inflated by a small, documented safety margin to absorb its own ordinary rounding error; it is a conservative engineering safeguard, validated by adversarial and randomized tests, not a formally rigorous proof for every possible input. Whichever outcome occurs, this is designed to correctly handle a forbidden interval bounded by two nearby positive roots (e.g. a closed universe with matter and a small positive cosmological constant, where \(E(a_{\max})^2\) can test positive again beyond such a pair) rather than skipping it -- to the extent this certified search and its documented safety margin actually can (a conservative engineering safeguard, not a formal directed-rounding proof for literally every possible float64 input, as already noted above) -- and, when the search cannot establish either outcome even after bisecting as far as its float64 arithmetic allows, it fails loudly (see above) rather than silently guessing; the physical expanding-branch solution must stop at the first root it reaches, and checking only the sign of \(E(a_{\max})^2\) could not detect this at all. The comparison that decides whether \(a_{\max}\) or a found turning point comes first uses the literal requested values with no floating-point tolerance, so that the returned trajectory never extends numerically past a hard user-supplied bound (\(a_{\max}\) or \(t_{\max}\)) merely to make the reported label read more smoothly; at the knife-edge where \(a_{\max}\) coincides with \(a_{\rm turn}\) to within roughly the precision of the internal bisection, the run may be labeled either "\(a_{\max}\)" or "turnaround" depending on bisection round-off, but the numerical bound itself is never violated either way.
  10. If the search certifies that no root exists even in the range extended past \(a_{\max}\), this may be a "loitering" cosmology where \(E(a)^2\) dips close to zero and rebounds without ever crossing it: rather than fast-forwarding across that dip by quadrature (which would discard the RK4 trajectory through it), ordinary RK4 stepping resumes, using a cancellation-safe reference expansion rate — built from the sum of the absolute values of each Friedmann-equation term rather than their signed sum — so the step size cannot blow up simply because the true, signed \(E(a)^2\) is anomalously small there. \(a_{\max}\) is then reached normally, step by step, and the remaining time to any requested \(t_{\max}\) along the way is handled exactly as in ordinary integration.
  11. If the search instead finds a genuine root, it is located precisely by direct bisection on the algebraic condition \(E(a_{\rm turn})^2=0\) (independent of the RK4 trajectory), and every time of interest in that neighborhood — the time to \(a_{\rm turn}\) itself, to \(a_{\max}\) if it precedes \(a_{\rm turn}\), to \(t_{\max}\) if it is reached first, to any checkpoint such as \(a=1\) that falls in this interval, and a fine interior grid of intermediate points spaced evenly in cosmic time across the handoff (so the plotted and CSV-exported trajectory has no unrepresented gap approaching the turnaround) — is found by Gauss-Legendre quadrature of the same integral after a substitution (\(a=a_{\rm turn}-u^2\)) that removes the endpoint singularity; this substitution stays accurate for a target arbitrarily close to \(a_{\rm turn}\), which is what makes it safe to use both for a boundary that sits immediately next to the turning point and for these interior samples. A turning point is reported only when it is genuinely the first of the three possible stopping events to occur; \(a_{\max}\) and \(t_{\max}\), when given, are hard bounds that a turning point can never override. The turnaround's OWN output row always shows the exact, known-by-construction values \(H=0\) and undefined (NaN) \(\Omega_i\)/\(q\) -- not whatever tiny nonzero residual the root-bisection's final floating-point bracket happened to leave, which by itself could otherwise look like an ordinary point with an enormous but finite \(H\) and \(\Omega_i\).
  12. Every intermediate value (the CPL density shape, \(E(a)^2\) and its derivative, each bisection midpoint, each quadrature integrand) is required to be finite; a non-finite value raises a clear error rather than being silently treated as a turning point. For a parameter combination that sits pathologically close to a hidden recollapse, the search can require a large (but budget-limited) number of evaluations before either certifying safety or finding the root, so a run very near this regime may take noticeably longer than an equally-sized ordinary run, and — per the previous point — an exhausted budget is reported as an error rather than as a quiet "no root found."
  13. Once a genuine turnaround is found, the total lifetime (twice the reported time to turnaround, by the exact time-reversal symmetry of the Friedmann equation -- \(E(a)^2\) depends on \(a\) alone, never on the sign of \(\dot a\)) is reported regardless of --continue_collapse, rather than only when the mirrored array data is also requested. Only the factor of two is exact, not the number being doubled: the reported \(t_{\rm turn}\) is itself a numerical quadrature/root-finding result, and (like the early-time Big-Bang offset it is measured from) is only as accurate as --a_i being deep enough into the assumed-dominant early-time regime for that offset to be trustworthy — see "How seriously to take each number" above. --continue_collapse controls only whether the mirrored contracting-branch array data (the actual \((t,a)\) trajectory points) is additionally generated, by mirroring the already-computed expanding branch about the turnaround with no re-integration, back down to \(a_i\) (not all the way to the \(a=0\) singularity — the omitted early interval is represented only by the analytic early-time offset, so the array's own last sampled time is slightly earlier than the reported total lifetime).
  14. Post-process the full \(a(t)\) trajectory into \(z(t)\), \(H(t)\), \(\Omega_i(a)\), \(w_{\rm DE}(a)\), and \(q(a)\) (the last from the independent second Friedmann equation, not from numerically differentiating \(a(t)\)).
  15. Locate the matter-radiation equality scale factor algebraically (\(a_{\rm eq}=\Omega_{r0}/\Omega_{m0}\)), and locate every matter-dark-energy equality and every deceleration/acceleration transition by scanning \(\Omega_m(a)-\Omega_{\rm DE}(a)\) and \(q(a)\) on an 800-point log-spaced grid over \([a_i,a_{\max}]\) for sign changes, then bisecting each one to convergence -- an ordinary, monotonic history has exactly one of each, but a non-monotonic \(w(a)\) (large \(|w_a|\)) or an unusual density budget can genuinely cross zero more than once, and every crossing found is retained, not just the first. An exact-zero sample exactly on the grid is counted as the single crossing it actually is, never double-counted as two adjacent sign flips. The full list (each crossing's \(a\), redshift, and direction of transition) is written to the run summary, the CSV provenance header, and marked on the corresponding plot panel; the first crossing is additionally exposed under the original scalar field names for backward compatibility. Two genuine crossings closer together than the grid spacing can, in principle, both fall inside a single grid cell and be missed, and a transition that only touches zero tangentially without actually changing sign is not detected by a sign-change scan at all -- both are inherent limitations of this grid-and-bisect method, not bugs, and a finer manual scan of the CSV output is the way to check a suspected missed or tangential crossing.
  16. If \(w_a=0\), \(w_0<-1\), and the requested run did not encounter a recollapse, search (out to a practical scale-factor horizon) for a future recollapse the requested run simply did not reach: if none is found and the phantom term is certified to dominate permanently beyond that horizon, compute \(\texttt{big\_rip\_remaining\_gyr}\) (the time from today to the Rip, from the closed-form estimate above -- depends only on \(H_0\), \(w_0\), and \(\Omega_{\rm DE,0}\), never on \(a_i\), and so is reported even for a past-eternal model); additionally report the absolute \(\texttt{big\_rip\_gyr}=\texttt{age\_today\_gyr}+\texttt{big\_rip\_remaining\_gyr}\) whenever \(\texttt{age\_today\_gyr}\) itself is defined (i.e. not for a past-eternal model, which has no absolute time origin to add it to). If a future recollapse is found instead, report that (and no Big Rip); if the search can certify neither outcome, report that the model's long-term fate is unresolved rather than guessing.

Mode compare

  1. Resolve the requested list of preset names (optionally including custom, built from the command-line cosmological parameters) against the built-in preset table.
  2. Run the full evolve calculation above once per cosmology, with the same --a_i, --a_max, and --step_frac for all of them so the comparison is apples-to-apples.
  3. Tabulate and plot the results together: overlaid \(a(t)\), overlaid \(q(t)\), a bar chart of ages (a model that never reaches \(a=1\) is marked, not silently omitted), and overlaid \(\Omega_{\rm DE}(a)\).

Mode age

  1. Build a linear grid of the scanned parameter (\(\Omega_{m0}\), \(\Omega_{\rm DE,0}\), \(w_0\), or \(H_0\)) between --scan_lo and --scan_hi.
  2. At each grid point, run the evolve integrator out to \(a=5\) — the finite look-ahead horizon used by this scan, which catches a recollapse before \(a=1\) as well as one between \(a=1\) and \(a=5\), but not one beyond \(a=5\); an arbitrary scan range can always place a turnaround past whatever horizon is chosen. When --no_force_flat is not given (the default) and the scanned parameter is \(\Omega_{m0}\) or \(\Omega_{\rm DE,0}\), the other of the two is adjusted at every grid point to keep \(\Omega_{k0}=0\); with --no_force_flat, curvature is instead allowed to vary implicitly.
  3. Record the age (if \(a=1\) was reached before any recollapse), the dimensionless product \(H_0t_0\), the acceleration-transition redshift, and separately flag two different outcomes: recollapse before reaching \(a=1\) (no age is defined at all) versus reaching \(a=1\) normally but being found, within the finite \(a=5\) look-ahead horizon, to recollapse at some \(a_{\rm turn}>1\) in the future. That second flag means only "recollapses by \(a=5\) in this scan" (the CSV column is named accordingly, recollapses_by_a5) — a model with a genuine \(a_{\rm turn}\) beyond that horizon is reported as not recollapsing here, not confirmed to expand forever.
  4. Plot age and \(H_0t_0\) against the scanned parameter, with reference lines at a user-supplied benchmark age (default 13 Gyr, evocative of the oldest globular clusters) and at the classic \(H_0t_0=2/3\) (Einstein-de Sitter) and \(H_0t_0=1\) (Milne) benchmarks.
Numerical safeguards. All cosmological and integration parameters must be finite; \(\Omega_{m0},\Omega_{r0}\ge0\); \(a_i\) must lie strictly between 0 and 1 and below --a_max; --step_frac is restricted to \([10^{-6},0.5]\); --t_max, if given, must exceed the analytic Big-Bang time offset at \(a_i\); --age_ref_gyr (mode age) must be greater than zero. Before integrating, the program estimates the number of RK4 steps a run would need and refuses immediately, with a clear message, if that would exceed the internal safety limit (2,000,000 steps) — this matters because the smallest allowed --step_frac combined with the default --a_i/--a_max range would need tens of millions of steps, so not every technically-in-range step_frac is actually feasible with every a_i/a_max choice. The dark-energy density shape \(g(a)\) is evaluated in log space with an explicit domain check, and every intermediate quantity (\(E(a)^2\), its derivative, each RK4 stage, each root-bisection midpoint, each quadrature evaluation, and the final output arrays) is required to be finite; a non-finite value raises a clear error immediately rather than being clipped, floored, or silently reinterpreted as a physical event such as a turning point. Every run also records why it stopped (a_max reached, t_max reached, or a genuine turnaround), printed in the run summary and written to CSV provenance. --no_plot is refused unless a CSV directory is given, so a run always produces something. Passing these checks is not a substitute for a convergence test (EXP-14).

physics_cosmo.py

Constants and unit conversion, the closure relation for \(\Omega_{k0}\), the CPL dark-energy shape and equation of state, the first and second Friedmann equations, the RK4 integrator with a_max/t_max/turnaround stop-precedence handling and turnaround (recollapse) detection by direct algebraic root-finding and singularity-removing quadrature, the exact time-reversal mirroring of a contracting branch (with the correct sign flip of \(H\) on that branch), the early-time analytic offset, the generic sign-change root finder used for equality and transition epochs, the Big Rip estimate, and the named preset cosmologies. Suggested Experiments

The exercises are divided into a core sequence, arranged from reading the output through controlled comparisons to numerical work, and a set of optional projects that connect to open questions in cosmology. Two synthesis exercises close the set. Nothing here needs anything beyond the program, a spreadsheet or short script, and the printed summary.

Core sequence · introductory

EXP-1 · introductory

Read the Concordance Universe

Run python main.py --mode evolve --H0 67.4 --omega_m 0.315 (omit --omega_de so the program forces flatness exactly; passing --omega_de 0.685 here would leave a stray \(\Omega_{k0}=-9.24\times10^{-5}\) from the default radiation density). Record the age of the universe, \(q_0\), the matter-radiation and matter-dark-energy equality redshifts, and the acceleration-transition redshift. Which happened more recently: matter-dark-energy equality, or the onset of accelerated expansion? Should those two necessarily coincide, given the formula for \(q(a)\)? For a bonus, derive \(a_{m\Lambda}=(\Omega_{m0}/\Omega_{\Lambda0})^{1/3}\) and \(a_{\rm accel}=(\Omega_{m0}/2\Omega_{\Lambda0})^{1/3}\) for flat matter+\(\Lambda\) with negligible radiation, verify both against the printed values, and explain the factor of two between them.

EXP-2 · introductory

The Historical Age Crisis

Run --mode evolve --H0 70 --omega_m 1.0 --omega_de 0.0 (Einstein-de Sitter). Record the age. The oldest globular clusters in the Milky Way are reliably dated to roughly 12-13 Gyr. Is this matter-only, flat universe old enough to contain them? This was a real crisis in cosmology before 1998 -- explain, in one sentence using only the formula \(t_0=(2/3)/H_0\), why raising \(H_0\) cannot solve the problem. Now run --mode evolve --H0 70 --omega_m 0.3 --omega_de 0.0 (an open, low-density, still matter-only universe -- no \(\Lambda\) at all) and compare its age. This is the historically important point: an open universe alone could relieve the pre-1998 age problem without dark energy. What finally made dark energy hard to avoid was a separate observation (EXP-6), not the age alone.

EXP-3 · introductory

Three Eras

Run --mode evolve with default (flat, concordance-like) parameters and study the \(\Omega_i(a)\) panel. Identify by eye the radiation era, the matter era, and the dark-energy era. Using the closed-form \(a_{\rm eq}=\Omega_{r0}/\Omega_{m0}\), predict the matter-radiation equality scale factor before checking it against the printed summary.

EXP-4 · intermediate

An Analytic Validation Case (Milne)

Run --mode evolve --omega_m 0 --omega_r 0 --omega_de 0 (the Milne universe: pure curvature, nothing else). The exact solution is \(a=H_0t\) for all time, where \(H_0\) must first be converted from km/s/Mpc to Gyr\(^{-1}\) (the program does this once, internally; do it yourself by hand here). Using the CSV output, compute the relative error \(|a_{\rm program}(t)-H_0t|/(H_0t)\) at several rows spanning the run; the run starts at a small but nonzero \(t\) set by the early-time offset (there is no literal \(t=0\) row), but skip the first row or two anyway if \(t\) there is so small that ordinary floating-point round-off in \(a_{\rm program}(t)\) dominates the relative error you are trying to measure. Why should this particular cosmology make an unusually clean test of the integrator, and what does it not test (checkpoints, turnaround handling, a nonlinear \(H(a)\))? Separately: Milne's \(a=0\) is flat Minkowski spacetime viewed in an unusual coordinate system, not a curvature singularity (see the Early-Time section) -- state in one sentence, using \(dt/da=1/(aH_0E(a))\) with \(E(a)^2=\Omega_{k0}\) constant, why this model is nonetheless a genuine finite-age Big Bang (a real \(t=0\) exists) despite \(a=0\) not being singular there.

Core sequence · introductory to intermediate

EXP-5 · introductory/intermediate

The Bookkeeping Identity

Using the evolve-mode CSV for any cosmology of your choice, verify numerically that \(\Omega_m(a)+\Omega_r(a)+\Omega_k(a)+\Omega_{\rm DE}(a)=1\) at every row where the quantities are defined, to within floating-point round-off (skip any row flagged as a turnaround/undefined row, where a vanishing denominator makes the individual \(\Omega_i\) not meaningfully defined even though the identity's derivation below still explains why it would hold in the limit). Then derive this identity algebraically in one line, starting from the definition \(\Omega_i(a)\equiv\rho_i(a)/\rho_{\rm crit}(a)\) and the first Friedmann equation. Because every term shares the same denominator, this identity holds by construction regardless of whether \(a(t)\) was integrated correctly -- it checks that the program's internal bookkeeping and CSV export are self-consistent, not that the numerical integration of \(a(t)\) is right (EXP-11 builds that independent check).

EXP-6 · introductory/intermediate

1998 in One Plot

Run the concordance cosmology and read the sign of \(q(t)\) at early times versus today. Using only the formula \(q=\tfrac12\sum\Omega_i(1+3w_i)\), explain algebraically why a universe with only matter and radiation can never have \(q<0\), and why, within General Relativity and the FLRW model, accelerating expansion requires a component with negative effective \(\rho+3p\) -- \(w<-1/3\) in the fluid picture this program implements. A cosmological constant is one realization of that component (it can be written either geometrically, on the left side of Einstein's equation, or as an exactly equivalent \(w=-1\) fluid on the right side, as this program treats it); modified gravity, which changes the left side of Einstein's equation instead of adding a fluid, is a genuinely different route to \(q<0\) and lies outside what this program can model.

Core sequence · intermediate

EXP-7 · intermediate

Open and Closed, Side by Side

Run --mode compare --presets open,closed first: these two share zero dark energy but differ in both \(\Omega_{m0}\) and the derived \(\Omega_{k0}\) (curvature makes up whatever matter does not, under flatness being dropped), so their very different fates can be explained using only the matter+curvature first Friedmann equation \(E(a)^2=\Omega_{m0}a^{-3}+\Omega_{k0}a^{-2}\), without reference to dark energy at all -- do that, tracking both terms. Then add lambdaCDM to the comparison (--presets EdS,lambdaCDM,open,closed) and note that it differs from the other three in \(H_0\), \(\Omega_{m0}\), and the presence of dark energy simultaneously, so any single difference you see there cannot be attributed to curvature alone -- fate follows from the full \(E(a)^2\) budget, not from \(\Omega_{k0}\) in isolation once dark energy is present.

EXP-8 · intermediate

Reproducing the Age-vs-\(\Omega_m\) Plot

Run --mode age --scan_param omega_m --scan_lo 0.05 --scan_hi 1.5 --scan_n 60. A finite grid will almost never land exactly on the \(\Omega_{m0}\) where the age equals your chosen --age_ref_gyr -- find the two adjacent grid rows that bracket it and linearly interpolate between them, then state your result together with the grid spacing as an honest uncertainty. Double --scan_n and see how much the estimate moves. Is your interpolated value closer to the historical assumption \(\Omega_m=1\) or to the modern concordance value \(\Omega_m\approx0.3\)? (For the specific historical question of whether a flat, positive-\(\Lambda\) universe is needed at all, restricting the scan to \(\Omega_{m0}\le1\) keeps the comparison to the flat-\(\Lambda\)-vs-\(\Lambda=0\) family discussed in EXP-2.)

EXP-9 · intermediate

Evolving Dark Energy, Phantom, and the Cosmological Constant

Run --mode compare --presets lambdaCDM,evolving_de,phantom,desi_like. All four share the same \(H_0\) and \(\Omega_{m0}\); only \(w_0\) and \(w_a\) differ, so any difference you see is attributable to the dark-energy sector alone. Compare their ages, their \(q_0\), and their \(\Omega_{\rm DE}(a)\) curves. Find the scale factor at which \(|\Omega_{\rm DE,evolving\_de}(a)-\Omega_{\rm DE,\Lambda CDM}(a)|\) first exceeds, say, 1% of \(\Omega_{\rm DE,\Lambda CDM}(a)\) (a concrete threshold, not "visibly"), and use that to say how recent an epoch you would need to observe precisely to tell the two apart. Caution: despite its name, evolving_de (\(w_0=-0.8\), \(w_a=0.3\)) is not pure quintessence over the whole range this program can show you -- using \(w(a)=w_0+w_a(1-a)\), verify for yourself that it crosses \(w=-1\) at \(a=5/3\) (since \(dw/da=-w_a\ne0\), it genuinely crosses rather than merely touching); describe it as quintessence-like for \(a\lesssim5/3\) and phantom-like beyond, both squarely within \(a\ge1\) (future) CPL extrapolation, not "three regimes from today onward." Separately, using \(w(a)=w_0+w_a(1-a)\), explain why desi_like (\(w_0=-0.7,\ w_a=-1.0\)) moves in the opposite direction into the past from evolving_de -- this is the sign distinction behind the DESI note above.

EXP-10 · intermediate

The Flatness Problem, Quantitatively

Run --mode evolve with \(\Omega_{m0}=0.3\), \(\Omega_{\rm de}=0.6999\) (so \(\Omega_{k0}=1-0.3-9.24\times10^{-5}-0.6999\approx7.6\times10^{-6}\), already tiny). With the default \(\Omega_{r0}=9.24\times10^{-5}\), matter-radiation equality is at \(a_{\rm eq}=\Omega_{r0}/\Omega_{m0}\approx3.08\times10^{-4}\), so both \(a=10^{-6}\) and \(a=10^{-8}\) lie in the radiation-dominated era, not the matter-dominated one. Using the CSV output, find the rows nearest \(a=10^{-2}\) (matter-dominated), \(a=10^{-6}\), and \(a=10^{-8}\) (both radiation-dominated) and read off \(\Omega_k(a)\) at each. With the default \(a_i=10^{-8}\), \(a=10^{-8}\) is exactly the first row of the CSV; \(a=10^{-6}\) and \(a=10^{-2}\) will not generally land on the grid exactly -- use the nearest row, or interpolate, and say which you did. By what factor does \(|\Omega_k|\) shrink for each factor of 100 decrease in \(a\)? State the scaling as a power law at each pair of points, and explain from the \(a^{-2}\) (curvature) vs. \(a^{-3}\) (matter) vs. \(a^{-4}\) (radiation) scalings in \(E(a)^2\) why it is \(\Omega_k(a)\propto a\) between \(a=10^{-2}\) and matter-radiation equality, but \(\Omega_k(a)\propto a^2\) between \(a=10^{-6}\) and \(a=10^{-8}\), deep in the radiation era.

EXP-11 · advanced

Verify the Second Friedmann Equation Independently

Using the evolve-mode CSV for the concordance cosmology, numerically differentiate \(a(t)\) twice to estimate \(q(t)=-\ddot a\,a/\dot a^2\) directly from the trajectory, at several times of your choosing, and compare with the program's own \(q(t)\) (built on the algebraic second Friedmann equation, not on differentiating the first). The output time grid is not uniform in \(t\) (the step size is Hubble-time-scaled), so a simple equal-spacing second-difference formula is not directly valid -- either use the general three-point finite-difference formula for unevenly spaced points, or first resample \(a(t)\) onto a uniform grid by interpolation before differentiating. Exclude rows right at a checkpoint or turnaround. Define precisely what you mean by "coarser cadence" (e.g., use only every \(k\)-th CSV row) before asking whether agreement gets better or worse with it.

EXP-12 · intermediate/advanced

A Universe That Recollapses

Run --mode evolve --omega_m 1.5 --omega_de 0.0 --continue_collapse. Record \(a_{\rm turn}\), \(t_{\rm turn}\), and the total lifetime. First derive \(a_{\rm turn}\) algebraically by setting \(E(a)^2=0\) with only matter and curvature (neglecting the tiny radiation term, so your hand value will be approximately, not exactly, 3) and compare with the program's value, which does include that small radiation term. Then explain, using only the fact that \(E(a)^2\) depends on \(a\) but never on the sign of \(\dot a\), why the total lifetime must be exactly twice the time to turnaround -- with no need to re-integrate the contracting half. Note that the mirrored data in the CSV run back down only to \(a_i\), not to \(a=0\); the quoted total-lifetime figure extrapolates the missing interval near each end through the analytic early-time offset, not through additional integrated points. For a convergence check, repeat at a few values of --a_i and --step_frac and see how much \(t_{\rm turn}\) moves.

EXP-13 · intermediate/advanced

Racing Toward a Big Rip

Run --mode evolve --w0 -1.2 --wa 0 --a_max 60 (the Big Rip formula used here assumes \(w_a=0\); leaving --wa at its default already satisfies this, but say so explicitly) and record the reported Big Rip time. Using the closed-form estimate in the help file's equations section, compute your own estimate by hand from \(H_0\), \(\Omega_{\rm DE,0}\), and \(w_0\), and compare -- being careful to distinguish the reported absolute cosmic time \(t_{\rm rip}\) from the time remaining \(t_{\rm rip}-t_0\) you compute by hand from the formula. Then repeat for \(w_0=-1.05\) and \(w_0=-1.5\): how sensitively does the time to the Big Rip depend on how far \(w_0\) sits below \(-1\)? Finally, run \(w_0=-1.0\) exactly and confirm the program reports no Big Rip at all -- explain why an infinitesimal change in \(w_0\) at the boundary produces a qualitative change in the universe's fate, and note that this whole estimate is a late-time, dark-energy-dominated approximation, not valid while matter, radiation, or curvature still contribute.

EXP-14 · advanced

Numerical Convergence

Repeat the concordance run with --step_frac 0.05, 0.02, 0.01, 0.005, 0.001 and record the age of the universe to six significant figures each time (0.05 is coarse enough to show visibly non-asymptotic behavior -- include it anyway, as a lesson in what "not yet converged" looks like). Demonstrate convergence, quantify the residual spread at the finest setting relative to a very fine reference run (e.g. step_frac=1e-4), and estimate the empirical order of convergence from how the error scales as step_frac is halved. Then repeat for the closed-universe turnaround in EXP-12. This is not the same convergence question as the ordinary trajectory: \(a_{\rm turn}\) is found by algebraic bisection (converges to bisection tolerance, essentially independent of step_frac), while \(t_{\rm turn}\) additionally depends on the RK4-integrated time accumulated before the handoff to that root-finder plus a separate Gauss-Legendre quadrature -- so ask separately how \(a_{\rm turn}\) and \(t_{\rm turn}\) each behave as step_frac is refined, rather than expecting one single RK4 convergence order to describe both.

EXP-16 · advanced

The Dark-Energy Degeneracy Problem

Find a second pair \((w_0,w_a)\), with \(|w_a-0.3|\geq0.1\) so it is genuinely distinct from the evolving_de preset's \((-0.8,0.3)\) rather than a rounding-level variant of it, that reproduces the concordance cosmology's age to within 0.1% and \(q_0\) to within 0.01 (searching \(w_0\in(-1.5,-1/3)\) and \(w_a\in(-2,2)\) is generous enough to contain a solution) (a little trial and error with --mode evolve is expected; note that at fixed present densities, \(q_0\) depends on \(w_0\) alone and not on \(w_a\) at all, since \(w(a{=}1)=w_0\) exactly -- so your search only needs to adjust \(w_0\) to match \(q_0\), then use \(w_a\) freely for the age match). EXP-17d · advanced (numerical-methods project)

The Closed-Matter-Only Universe, Exactly

The flat matter-only case has a closed form (\(a\propto t^{2/3}\)), but so does the closed matter-only case (\(\Omega_{r0}=\Omega_{\rm DE,0}=0\), \(\Omega_{m0}>1\)): it is the classic cycloid, \(a(\eta)=\tfrac{\Omega_{m0}}{2(\Omega_{m0}-1)}(1-\cos\eta)\), \(H_0t(\eta)=\tfrac{\Omega_{m0}}{2(\Omega_{m0}-1)^{3/2}}(\eta-\sin\eta)\), parametrized by the development angle \(\eta\in[0,2\pi]\) (\(\eta=\pi\) is turnaround). Derive this yourself from \(E(a)^2=\Omega_{m0}a^{-3}+(1-\Omega_{m0})a^{-2}\), or look up the standard derivation, and implement it (a short script, or even a spreadsheet) to compute \(a_{\rm turn}\) and \(t_{\rm turn}\) for \(\Omega_{m0}=2\) directly from these closed-form expressions, with no numerical integration at all. Compare against this program's --omega_m 2 --omega_de 0 output. Then repeat EXP-17c's experiment using this exact solution as the reference instead of the program's own first run: place --a_max and --t_max progressively closer to the exact \(a_{\rm turn}\)/\(t_{\rm turn}\) (say, at 90%, 99%, 99.9%, and 99.99% of the exact value) and track how the program's reported stopping time and scale factor converge to the exact cycloid values as the boundary approaches the singularity. This is the most demanding test of the event-ordering and turnaround machinery in the program, because it checks it against an independent solution with no integration error of its own, not just against the program's own self-consistency.

Domain of Validity

This program numerically solves the FLRW background equations for an idealized universe; it is not a cosmological-parameter-fitting pipeline and not a simulation of structure. The following are absent by design.

Accepted range is not the same as a physically meaningful range

QuantityAcceptedPhysically well-motivated
\(\Omega_{m0}\), \(\Omega_{r0}\)any non-negative finite value\(\Omega_{m0}\approx0.3\), \(\Omega_{r0}\approx9\times10^{-5}\) for our universe; other values are legitimate toy models, explicitly the point of several exercises above
\(\Omega_{k0}\)any value implied by the other three densities\(|\Omega_{k0}|\lesssim10^{-3}\) observationally; the program warns above \(|\Omega_{k0}|=0.05\)
\(w_0\), \(w_a\)any finite values that keep the CPL density shape numerically representable (very large \(|w_a|\) over a wide \(a\)-range is rejected with a clear error instead)dataset- and model-dependent: current analyses are consistent with a pure \(w_0=-1\) cosmological constant in some combinations, and show a several-\(\sigma\) preference for evolving \(w_0>-1\), \(w_a<0\) in others (see the dated DESI DR2 note above) -- this is an active, unsettled area, not a single settled number; large excursions are legitimate for exploring the CPL parameter space, not claims about the real universe
\(a_i\)any value strictly between 0 and 1deep enough into radiation (or matter, or curvature) domination that the analytic Big-Bang offset is asymptotically accurate (the program checks and warns if not); separately, the program also certifies that \(a_i\) sits on a branch continuously connected back to the true Big Bang at \(a=0\), with no hidden forbidden interval (two nearby roots of \(E(a)^2=0\)) in between and no asymptotically negative \(E(a)^2\) as \(a\to0\) -- an \(a_i\) that fails this connectivity check is rejected with a clear error, not merely warned about

One outcome deserves calling out explicitly here because it is easy to mistake for a bug: a flat model built entirely from a cosmological constant or a permanently phantom (\(w_0+w_a\leq-1\)) dark-energy term, with no matter, radiation, or curvature at all, will legitimately pass the connectivity check above yet be classified past_status="past_eternal" rather than given a finite age -- see "Past-Eternal Models" in the Physical Background section and EXP-21. This is expected, documented behavior for that specific, narrow parameter combination, not a numerical failure; every other supported combination (anything with a nonzero \(\Omega_{m0}\), \(\Omega_{r0}\), or \(\Omega_{k0}\) contribution at early times) yields an ordinary finite-age Big Bang.

What is absent

  • Structure formation. Galaxies, clusters, and the cosmic web are density perturbations on top of the homogeneous background computed here; their growth is a separate (linear, then nonlinear) calculation this program does not attempt.
  • Recombination and the cosmic microwave background. Photon decoupling at \(z\approx1100\) and the resulting CMB anisotropies require radiative transfer physics entirely outside a background-only Friedmann solver.
  • Big Bang nucleosynthesis. The synthesis of light elements in the first few minutes depends on nuclear reaction rates and particle physics not modeled here; \(\Omega_{r0}\) is simply supplied as a number.
  • Cosmic inflation. The flatness and horizon problems are mentioned in the physical background, but no inflationary epoch is modeled; the integration starts well after any such epoch would have ended.
  • Anything at or before the Planck time. \(a\to0\) is a mathematical limit of the classical field equations, not a physical claim; quantum gravity is expected to take over long before the model's own \(a\to0\) singularity is reached.
  • Neutrino mass effects, baryon-photon coupling, and multiple dark-matter species. Matter and radiation are each treated as a single perfect fluid.

What the program does capture well is the background dynamics implied by whatever energy content you choose: how \(H(a)\), the density budget, the deceleration parameter, and the age of the universe all follow from the Friedmann equations, exactly at the level of the equations themselves, once that content is given -- subject to this program's own numerical integration, root-finding, and quadrature error, which is typically small but never literally zero (see "How accurate is this, then?" above).

Related Programs

  • GravitationalWaveSources — like this program, integrates an exact leading-order GR result (the quadrupole inspiral) forward in time with adaptive RK4; a useful comparison of numerical technique across very different physical settings.
  • StellarEvolutionTracks — a one-zone model of stellar structure and evolution; unlike the Friedmann equations here, the underlying stellar-structure equations require calibrated closures (opacity, convection, nuclear reaction rates) that this program's problem does not need, since each program's governing equations call for whatever level of closure the underlying physics actually requires.
  • A distance-redshift / CMB module (not yet implemented) would be the natural next step: computing the luminosity distance \(d_L(z)\) and angular-diameter distance \(d_A(z)\) from the same \(a(t)\) this program already produces is a short addition, and is exactly the calculation that made the 1998 acceleration discovery possible.
