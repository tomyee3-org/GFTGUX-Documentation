Description

NbodyGalaxySimulator is a standalone gravitational N-body program built around a Barnes–Hut octree (Barnes & Hut 1986, Nature 324, 446) and Plummer-softened gravity. It exists to teach the two ideas that let a simulation move beyond a handful of exactly integrated bodies to hundreds or thousands of mutually interacting ones: force softening, which regularizes the otherwise-singular point-mass force at close range, and hierarchical force approximation, which reduces the cost of computing every body's force from every other body from \(O(N^2)\) to \(O(N\log N)\) by treating a distant clump of bodies as a single point mass.

One program covers three calculations, selected with --mode: cluster, a Plummer sphere of stars evolved long enough to show the onset of two-body relaxation and (with tuned parameters) genuine evaporation; galaxy, a cold uniform sphere that gravitationally collapses and “violently relaxes” (Lynden-Bell 1967) into a quasi-equilibrium remnant, the classic toy galaxy-formation experiment; and chaos, two indistinguishably close realizations of the same small cluster, integrated identically, whose separation is tracked to measure the Lyapunov time of gravitational motion.

This program assumes Multiple as a prerequisite. Multiple already covers exact, direct-summation Newtonian gravity for a handful of bodies, integrated with an adaptive predictor-corrector scheme accurate enough to follow a single close encounter. This program does not re-derive Newtonian two-body motion or re-teach what a gravitational acceleration is; it starts exactly where Multiple's exact \(O(N^2)\) approach stops being practical, and introduces two ideas Multiple has no need for — a fixed-timestep leapfrog integrator and hierarchical (tree) force approximation — to reach fifty, five hundred, or five thousand bodies. It was written as a standalone program rather than an added mode of Multiple, deliberately, so that tree codes, force softening and their attendant subtleties do not intrude on Multiple's own introductory tutorial flow; whether and how to incorporate any of this into a second edition of Gravity from the Ground Up is a decision for Prof. Schutz alone.
What this program is, and what it is not. This is a from-scratch, transparent, pure-Python teaching implementation, not a research N-body code. There is no individual (per-body) or block timestepping, no higher-order symplectic integrator, no quadrupole or higher multipole correction in the tree (monopole only), no parallelism or GPU acceleration, no gas or radiative physics, no external tidal field or cosmological expansion, and every simulation particle carries the same mass. Three limits are worth knowing before your first run: the tree code's wall-clock speed advantage over direct summation is not guaranteed at the body counts this program targets — see the Algorithm section for measured numbers and why; two-body evaporation is a slow process that the default softening actively suppresses (softening chosen for force accuracy also damps the close encounters that physically drive relaxation); and a fixed global timestep only marginally resolves the sharp density spike at the deepest point of a cold collapse. All three are turned into explicit exercises below rather than hidden.

Physical Background

Why direct summation stops scaling

Multiple computes every body's acceleration by summing the exact Newtonian contribution from every other body: for \(N\) bodies that is \(N(N-1)/2\) pairwise terms, so the cost grows as \(O(N^2)\). Doubling \(N\) quadruples the work. That is entirely practical for the handful of bodies in a planetary or few-star encounter, which is exactly Multiple's domain, but it becomes prohibitive well before \(N\) reaches even a few thousand. A star cluster with \(10^5\)–\(10^6\) stars, or a galaxy with far more, is simply out of reach of direct summation on any one machine.

Barnes & Hut (1986) noticed that a distant clump of bodies barely needs to be resolved individually: to good approximation, its combined gravitational pull can be replaced by a single point mass at its center of mass. Organizing the bodies into a spatial tree (an octree in three dimensions) and applying that approximation wherever it is accurate enough reduces the cost to \(O(N\log N)\). This is the central algorithmic idea of this program (see Algorithm below); its price is an approximate rather than exact force.

Why gravity needs softening here

The point-mass Newtonian force, \(F \propto 1/r^2\), diverges as two bodies approach each other. Multiple handles this by raising an explicit error the instant two bodies coincide, because with an adaptive, accurate integrator a genuine close encounter should simply be resolved in fine timestep detail, not glossed over. That approach does not extend to hundreds of bodies on a fixed timestep: a single close pair would demand an enormously smaller timestep than every other body needs, and Multiple's per-run predictor-corrector accuracy targets do not exist here at all — the leapfrog scheme below uses one fixed timestep for every body, for the whole run.

The standard remedy, used throughout this program, is Plummer softening: replace the Newtonian potential's \(1/r\) with \(1/\sqrt{r^2+\epsilon^2}\), where \(\epsilon\) is the softening length; taking the gradient of that softened potential gives the \((r^2+\epsilon^2)^{3/2}\) denominator in the force law shown below, not \(r^2+\epsilon^2\) itself. This has a direct physical reading: a simulation particle here rarely represents one literal star (or one dark-matter particle); it stands in for a much larger aggregate, smeared over some finite size, and \(\epsilon\) is a reasonable proxy for that size. It also has a purely numerical reading: it caps the maximum force two bodies can exert on each other, which keeps a fixed-timestep integrator stable through what would otherwise be arbitrarily close encounters. Both readings matter here, and they pull in the same direction for choosing \(\epsilon\) — but, as the Algorithm section discusses, that same suppression of close encounters also suppresses two-body relaxation itself, which is exactly the process the cluster mode is trying to show.

Two-body relaxation and evaporation

A self-gravitating system of discrete bodies is not the same as a smooth mass distribution: individual gravitational encounters between bodies exchange energy and gradually drive the system toward a broader, more relaxed velocity distribution — and a system of point masses in a finite volume has, in principle, no upper limit on relative speed reachable this way. Over many crossing times, enough encounters accumulate that some bodies are kicked above the local escape speed and leave for good: this is evaporation. The characteristic timescale for this diffusive process, the two-body relaxation time, is given below; for an isolated system without a tidal boundary, full evaporation of a Plummer-like cluster is a slow process, of order a hundred or more relaxation times (Ambartsumian 1938; Spitzer 1987).

Violent relaxation and cold collapse

A self-gravitating system released far from equilibrium — the galaxy mode's cold, uniform sphere is the classic case — does not settle down through the slow, encounter-by-encounter process above. Instead, while the overall mass distribution is still changing rapidly (during the collapse and the violent oscillations that follow it), the gravitational potential itself fluctuates on a dynamical timescale, and those fluctuations exchange energy between bodies far faster than individual two-body encounters could. Lynden-Bell (1967) named this violent relaxation and argued it explains why collisionless self-gravitating systems (galaxies, above all) reach a quasi-equilibrium, centrally concentrated state so much faster than their two-body relaxation time would allow. Direct numerical experiments of exactly this cold-collapse scenario (van Albada 1982, and many since) show the relaxation is generically incomplete in the sense that the remnant is not a smooth, isotropic equilibrium model: it carries an extended, non-Maxwellian halo of loosely bound bodies flung out during the collapse, and a density profile only qualitatively like a real galaxy's. That does not mean its virial ratio must sit visibly below 1 — in this program's own runs the remnant's virial ratio oscillates in a band close to 1 (see the virial theorem note below and Suggested Experiments), consistent with a system that is virialized on average while still relaxing in detail.

The virial theorem

For a self-gravitating system in a statistical steady state, the virial theorem requires \(2T + W_{\rm vir} = 0\), i.e. \(2T/|W_{\rm vir}| = 1\), where \(T\) is the total kinetic energy and \(W_{\rm vir} \equiv \sum_i \vec{r}_i\cdot\vec{F}_i\) is the virial (force-weighted) sum — not the softened potential energy \(U\). The two differ whenever softening is active: \(W_{\rm vir}\) is what actually enters the scalar virial theorem for a softened force law, while \(U\) is the energy bookkeeping quantity used in the total-energy conservation check elsewhere on this page. They coincide only in the unsoftened limit \(\epsilon\to 0\). This program reports \(2T/|W_{\rm vir}|\) throughout as the virial ratio: substantially below 1 means the system is still contracting (or under-supported), substantially above 1 means it is expanding (or was released with excess kinetic energy), and for a settled remnant a value oscillating in a modest band around 1 is the signature of a properly virialized system — a value that instead sits persistently far from 1 more often points to an under-resolved run (too few crossing times, too coarse a timestep, or softening much larger or smaller than the Dehnen default) than to a genuine physical departure from equilibrium.

Chaos in gravitating systems

The gravitational \(N\)-body problem is chaotic for \(N \ge 3\): two initial conditions that differ by an arbitrarily small amount separate exponentially in phase space, rather than growing only linearly as they would in a well-behaved, non-chaotic dynamical system. Miller (1964) first demonstrated this numerically for self-gravitating stellar systems; later work (e.g. Goodman, Heggie & Hut 1993) found that for small-\(N\) self-gravitating clusters the associated Lyapunov time — the e-folding time of that exponential growth — is typically of order a few crossing times. This program's chaos mode reproduces that measurement directly: see Governing Equations and Algorithm.

Governing Equations

Softened Newtonian gravity
\[ \vec{a}_i \;=\; G \sum_{j \ne i} \frac{m_j \,(\vec{x}_j - \vec{x}_i)}{\left(|\vec{x}_j-\vec{x}_i|^2 + \epsilon^2\right)^{3/2}} \]

\(\epsilon\) is the (fixed) softening length. \(\epsilon \to 0\) recovers the exact point-mass law used by Multiple.

Barnes–Hut opening-angle criterion
\[ \frac{s}{d} < \theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{treat this tree node as one point mass at its center of mass} \]

\(s\) is the node's (cubical) box size, \(d\) the distance from the body to the node's center of mass, and \(\theta\) the opening angle (--theta). \(\theta=0\) forces a full descent to individual bodies for every force evaluation (exact monopole summation, at direct-summation-like cost); \(\theta\) of order 0.5–0.8 is the usual working range.

Leapfrog (kick-drift-kick) integration
\[ \vec{v}_{1/2} = \vec{v}_0 + \tfrac{1}{2}\vec{a}(\vec{x}_0)\,\Delta t, \qquad \vec{x}_1 = \vec{x}_0 + \vec{v}_{1/2}\,\Delta t, \qquad \vec{v}_1 = \vec{v}_{1/2} + \tfrac{1}{2}\vec{a}(\vec{x}_1)\,\Delta t \]
Plummer (1911) density profile and enclosed mass
\[ \rho(r) = \frac{3M}{4\pi a^3}\left(1+\frac{r^2}{a^2}\right)^{-5/2}, \qquad \frac{M(r)}{M} = \frac{r^3}{(r^2+a^2)^{3/2}} \]

Sampled by inverting the enclosed-mass relation for \(r\) given a uniform random \(x_1\in(0,1)\), and drawing speeds by rejection sampling against the exact isotropic Plummer distribution function (Aarseth, Hénon & Wielen 1974).

Dehnen (2001) optimal softening
\[ \epsilon_{\rm opt} = 0.98\, a\, N^{-0.26} \]

The default softening length when --softening_pc is not given, minimizing the mean-square force error against the smooth Plummer field; used here as a general starting point for the uniform-sphere initial condition as well, not only the Plummer sphere it was derived for.

Characteristic timescales
\[ t_{\rm cross} = \sqrt{\frac{r_h^3}{GM}}, \qquad t_{\rm ff} = \sqrt{\frac{3\pi}{32\,G\bar\rho}}, \qquad t_{\rm relax} \approx \frac{N}{8\ln N}\, t_{\rm cross} \]

\(r_h\) is the half-mass radius; \(\bar\rho\) the mean density. The relaxation-time expression follows Binney & Tremaine, Galactic Dynamics (2nd ed.), eq. 1.38, with the Coulomb logarithm approximated as \(\ln N\); treat it as an order-of-magnitude scaling, not a precise prediction (real N-body codes commonly use \(\ln(0.4N)\) or \(\ln(N/2)\) instead, changing the result by an order-unity factor).

Virial ratio and instantaneous unboundedness criterion
\[ Q \equiv \frac{2T}{|W_{\rm vir}|}, \qquad e_i \equiv \tfrac{1}{2}v_i^2 + \Phi_i, \qquad e_i > 0 \Leftrightarrow \text{body } i \text{ is instantaneously unbound} \]

\(\Phi_i\) is the softened potential at body \(i\) due to every other body, evaluated in the system's own center-of-mass frame; \(W_{\rm vir}\) is the virial (force-weighted) sum defined above, not the softened potential energy. The energy criterion \(e_i>0\) is evaluated exactly as written — regardless of whether body \(i\) happens to be moving inward or outward at that instant — so it flags bodies that are unbound right now, not bodies that have permanently left the system; a body can cross back below \(e_i=0\) on a later snapshot; see Known Model Artefacts.

Lyapunov exponent
\[ \Delta(t) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_i |\vec{x}_{A,i}(t)-\vec{x}_{B,i}(t)|^2} \;\sim\; \Delta_0\, e^{\lambda t} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t_{\rm Lyap} = 1/\lambda \]

\(\vec{x}_A\) and \(\vec{x}_B\) are each recentred on their own mass-weighted center of mass before differencing, so a coherent overall translation between the two realizations cannot masquerade as divergence; \(\Delta\) is therefore a position-space separation, not a phase-space one (velocities are not included). \(\lambda\) is estimated by an ordinary least-squares fit of \(\ln\Delta(t)\) against \(t\), over the longest contiguous run of snapshots where \(\Delta\) has grown past \(3\Delta_0\) but not yet past half of its own eventual maximum. The fit is accepted only if it uses at least 5 points, the whole-window \(R^2\ge0.98\), and the fitted slope is consistent (to within 15%) across the first, middle and last thirds of the window — gates chosen to reject linear, quadratic, oscillatory and saturating (logistic-like) growth while still accepting a genuinely noisy exponential; a run for which no window passes all three gates reports no Lyapunov exponent rather than a misleading one.

Algorithm

Building and walking the octree

Each force evaluation over the whole body set (once per leapfrog half-step) proceeds in two stages. First, build_octree() recursively partitions the smallest cube enclosing all bodies into eight octants, subdividing any octant holding more than one body, down to a hard recursion-depth limit (MAX_TREE_DEPTH) that guarantees termination even for exactly or nearly coincident bodies — no finite number of halvings separates two identical points into different octants, so without this limit such a configuration would recurse forever. Every internal node stores its total mass and center of mass. Second, for each body, the tree is walked from the root: an internal node is accepted as a single point mass whenever the opening-angle criterion is satisfied; otherwise the walk descends into that node's (up to eight) children. A node whose accumulated mass is exactly zero (an empty octant) is skipped.

Force accuracy versus θ

Because the tree replaces exact per-body sums with a monopole approximation for distant clumps, its force differs from the exact (direct-summation) value by an amount that grows with θ. Measuring this directly (a Plummer sphere, comparing compute_accelerations_tree against compute_accelerations_direct at fixed softening) gives, typically:

These are illustrative, not guaranteed for every configuration; reproducing this table for your own --n_bodies and initial condition is Experiment 8 below. \(\theta=0\) is a genuine correctness check built into the test suite: it must reproduce direct summation to machine precision, because it forces the walk to the leaves for every body.

The tree code is not guaranteed to be faster in wall-clock terms at this program's scale. The \(O(N\log N)\) versus \(O(N^2)\) argument compares operation COUNTS. This is a pure-Python implementation: the tree walk is an explicit-stack traversal of Python objects, while direct summation is a single vectorized NumPy call per body. NumPy's vectorization has such a small constant factor per floating-point operation that, measured on this program's development machine, direct summation remained faster in wall-clock time than the tree at every body count up to MAX_BODIES = 5,000 — the crossover the operation-count argument predicts is real, but for an implementation shaped like this one it lies at a much larger \(N\) than a naive reading of “\(O(N\log N)\) beats \(O(N^2)\)” would suggest. This is not a defect to fix quietly; it is Experiment 9 below, and a genuine lesson in numerical methods: asymptotic complexity and measured wall-clock performance are different questions, and the constant factor a real implementation carries can dominate at every size you can practically test. --method lets you run either force law directly for exactly this comparison; the tree remains the pedagogically important algorithm regardless of which one wins a stopwatch race in this particular code.
The tree code does not conserve momentum exactly. Direct summation computes each pairwise force once and applies it to both bodies with an equal and opposite sign, so total momentum is conserved to machine precision (typical measured drift: relative to a natural momentum scale, of order \(10^{-16}\) over hundreds of steps). The tree's monopole approximation breaks this pairwise symmetry: body A may see body B's cell approximated as a single distant mass, while body B's own force evaluation does not necessarily see an equal and opposite contribution from a matching approximation of A's surroundings. Measured total momentum drift with the tree method is small but real (typically a few times \(10^{-4}\) relative to \(M\langle v\rangle\) over a few hundred crossing times at \(\theta=0.5\)) and grows with θ. This is a known, published property of Barnes–Hut codes, not a bug in this implementation; see Experiment 10.

Timestep selection and energy conservation

Leapfrog is a second-order symplectic integrator when the force it integrates is a genuine conservative force — a guarantee this program only establishes for method="direct". There, for a fixed timestep, leapfrog does not conserve energy exactly, but its energy error oscillates around a roughly constant offset rather than drifting secularly the way a non-symplectic scheme (e.g. forward Euler) would, provided the timestep resolves the local dynamical time; measured directly through a cold collapse, the maximum energy drift falls by close to the expected factor of 4 each time --steps_per_freefall is doubled (see Experiment 14). The tree's monopole force is not pairwise-symmetric and has not been shown to be the gradient of any single potential, so the symplectic argument does not carry over to method="tree" in the same way; measured directly, tree-method energy drift does not shrink appreciably with a smaller timestep, because it is dominated by the fixed force-approximation error set by \(\theta\), not by the timestep's own truncation error — refining \(\Delta t\) mainly buys accuracy for direct summation, while refining \(\theta\) is what matters for the tree. Each mode chooses its own default timestep as a fraction of a physically meaningful timescale computed from the requested initial condition (a fraction of the crossing time for cluster and chaos; of the free-fall time for galaxy) so that a default run is well resolved without the student needing to already know what a sound \(\Delta t\) looks like.

A fixed global timestep only marginally resolves the deepest moment of a cold collapse. During a galaxy-mode run's deepest collapse, the local dynamical time inside the transient density spike is far shorter than the free-fall time the default timestep was sized from, and energy conservation measurably worsens through that moment (typically to the percent level for the default parameters). A production N-body code would use individual or block timesteps for exactly this reason; this program uses one global \(\Delta t\) throughout, by design, to keep the leapfrog loop transparent. See Experiment 14.

Output and Plot Panels

Every run prints a text summary to the terminal, always draws a Matplotlib figure (unless --no_plot is given), and can optionally save that figure as a PNG and/or write a CSV file of the tracked diagnostics over time. This mirrors StellarEvolutionTracks's output conventions exactly, including the collision-safe timestamped filenames and the .provenance.txt sidecar written alongside every saved PNG.

Figure panels

cluster mode draws a 2×3 grid: initial positions (a projection scatter plot), final positions, Lagrangian radii (10/25/50/75/90% mass radii versus time), virial ratio \(2T/|W_{\rm vir}|\) versus time, an evaporation-diagnostics panel (the instantaneous unbound-body count n_unbound and the continuous high_velocity_fraction on a shared time axis, since the instantaneous criterion can stay at zero for a physically eventful run — see the note in Physical Background), and energy conservation (total energy relative to its initial value, with the maximum drift reported in the panel title).

galaxy mode also draws a 2×3 grid: initial cold-sphere positions, the deepest-collapse snapshot (the recorded time step with the smallest half-mass radius), Lagrangian radii through collapse and bounce, final quasi-equilibrium positions, virial ratio through violent relaxation, and energy conservation.

chaos mode (integrated with method="direct" by default; see Parameters) draws a 2×2 grid: final positions of both realizations overlaid in one projection, the position-space divergence between them on a logarithmic axis (with the exponential-growth fit window highlighted, and the fitted Lyapunov time and fit \(R^2\) in the panel title when one could be estimated), energy conservation for both realizations, and a histogram of final per-body separations between the two realizations.

Position panels use a shared axis-scaling rule: if a small number of bodies have been flung far from the bulk of the mass (common after a close encounter, and especially in galaxy mode's collapse), the axes are framed around the 95th-percentile radius rather than the true maximum, so the bulk of the system remains legible while outliers are still plotted, just possibly near the edge or clipped. This is a display choice only; no data is altered or discarded.

Energies are reported in joules (SI), matching the internal unit convention used throughout physics_nbg.py; every length quantity is converted to parsecs for readability. potential_J is the softened potential energy \(U\), used for the total-energy conservation check; virial_work_J is the separate virial (force-weighted) sum \(W_{\rm vir}\) used to compute virial_ratio — see The Virial Theorem above for why these two are not the same quantity. n_unbound is an integer count using the instantaneous unboundedness criterion (specific energy ≥ 0 relative to the system, evaluated at that snapshot regardless of the body's current radial motion — see Known Model Artefacts); high_velocity_fraction is a fraction in [0, 1].

Suggested Experiments

Most exercises below can be done with the CLI and the printed/plotted/CSV output alone. A few of the intermediate-to-advanced ones (marked accordingly) additionally call a physics_nbg function directly from a short Python snippet, or time a run via driver_nbg.run() instead of the CLI — this is called out explicitly where it happens, and none of it requires reading or modifying the program's own source. Difficulty labels are relative to this program, not to the course as a whole.

Core sequence · introductory

EXP-1 · introductory

Watch a Cluster Relax

Run python main.py --mode cluster. Read the half-mass radius r50 at the start and end from the printed summary or the Lagrangian-radii panel. Has the cluster expanded or contracted? Two-body relaxation is supposed to drive a self-gravitating system's core to contract while its outskirts expand as stars exchange energy — which Lagrangian radius (10% or 90%) moved the most, and does that match the description in Physical Background?

EXP-2 · introductory

A Cold Sphere Collapses

Run python main.py --mode galaxy. From the printed summary, note the initial virial ratio, the virial ratio at the deepest collapse, and the final virial ratio (the summary prints all three). Sketch (on paper, from the numbers, not the plot) how \(2T/|W_{\rm vir}|\) behaves over the run. Does it end at exactly 1, the value expected for a system in virial equilibrium, or does it oscillate in a band around 1? Read the virial theorem note in Physical Background to see why a settled oscillation near 1 — not a single converged number — is the expected signature here.

EXP-3 · introductory

How Fast Do Nearby Orbits Diverge?

Run python main.py --mode chaos. Read the fitted Lyapunov time off the divergence panel's title (or the terminal summary) and express it as a multiple of the initial crossing time. Miller (1964) and later studies put this ratio at a few crossing times for self-gravitating N-body systems; is your result in that range? Run it again (no --seed) and see whether the estimate is stable from realization to realization.

EXP-4 · introductory

Meet the Two Force Methods

Run the same cluster twice, once with --method tree and once with --method direct, using a fixed --seed so both start identically. Compare final energy, final virial ratio and the Lagrangian radii. They will not be pixel-identical — the tree only approximates the force — but they should tell a consistent physical story. If they disagreed wildly, what would that tell you about \(\theta\)?

Core sequence · introductory to intermediate

EXP-5 · introductory/intermediate

Relaxation Time Scales with N

Run --mode cluster with --n_bodies set to 60, 120, 250 and 500, holding --total_mass_msun and --scale_radius_pc fixed at their defaults and giving each the same --n_relax so they cover the same number of relaxation times. The crossing time depends only on \(M\) and \(R\) (not \(N\)), while the relaxation time formula in Governing Equations scales roughly as \(N/\ln N\). Does the relative amount of core contraction/outskirt expansion you see across the four runs behave the way that scaling predicts?

EXP-6 · introductory/intermediate

Crossing and Free-Fall Time Scaling

Using --mode galaxy, halve --radius_pc from its default and separately double --total_mass_msun, in two separate runs, keeping everything else fixed. Read the initial free-fall time from the terminal summary in each case. The free-fall time for a uniform sphere scales as \(t_{\rm ff}\propto\rho^{-1/2}\propto (R^3/M)^{1/2}\): predict the two new free-fall times before running, then check.

Core sequence · intermediate

EXP-7 · intermediate

Cold, Warm and Hot Collapse

Run --mode galaxy with --virial_ratio_init set to 0.0 (the cold default), 1.0 (already in virial equilibrium, in this program's \(2T/|W_{\rm vir}|\) convention) and 3.0 (unbound, expanding). Compare the three: does the 1.0 run collapse at all? Does the 3.0 run's half-mass radius ever come back down? For the cold and warm cases, compare how far the final virial ratio overshoots or undershoots 1, and connect what you see to why colder initial conditions are described as producing more “violent” relaxation.

EXP-8 · intermediate · uses the Python API

Reproduce the Opening-Angle Accuracy Table

This one needs a short Python snippet, not just the CLI: raw per-body accelerations are not part of the CLI/plot/CSV output. Call physics_nbg.plummer_sphere() to get an initial configuration (100–300 bodies is enough, fixed seed), then call compute_accelerations_direct() and compute_accelerations_tree() on it directly at several values of theta (0, 0.3, 0.5, 0.8, 1.0) and work out the RMS relative force error between them, body by body. Reproduce (or refute, with your own numbers in hand) the illustrative table in Algorithm. Confirm explicitly that \(\theta=0\) reproduces direct summation to machine precision — if it does not, something is wrong with your comparison, not the program.

EXP-9 · intermediate · uses the Python API

Is the Tree Actually Faster?

Time isolated force evaluations, not full runs: a full cluster run's step count itself grows with N (through the default relaxation-time run length), which would confound N-scaling with run-length scaling. Instead, build one fixed initial configuration per N (100, 300, 1000, 3000 bodies) with physics_nbg.plummer_sphere(), then time a fixed number of repeated calls (a few dozen, with one untimed warm-up call first) to compute_accelerations_tree() and separately to compute_accelerations_direct() on that same configuration, using Python's time module. Plot wall-clock time per force evaluation against N for both methods on log–log axes. Where, if anywhere, does the tree overtake direct summation on your machine? Compare your crossover (or lack of one) against the claim in Algorithm, and explain in your own words why an \(O(N\log N)\) algorithm can lose a wall-clock race to an \(O(N^2)\) one at practical sizes.

EXP-10 · intermediate · uses the Python API

Momentum Conservation, Tree Versus Direct

Call physics_nbg.run_cluster() directly (rather than through the CLI) with method="direct" and again with method="tree" (fixed seed), so you can keep the returned position/velocity arrays in hand; the CLI's CSV output does not include per-body vectors. From those arrays compute total momentum \(\sum_i m_i \vec v_i\) at the start and end of each run, normalized by \(M\langle |\vec v|\rangle\). Confirm direct summation conserves it to machine precision and the tree does not. Does the tree's momentum drift grow if you increase \(\theta\) toward 1.0? Relate what you find to the pairwise-symmetry argument in Algorithm.

EXP-11 · intermediate

Softening Is Hiding Your Unbound Bodies

Run the default cluster and note n_unbound (instantaneously unbound bodies) at the end — it is very often zero. Now rerun with --softening_pc set well below the Dehnen-optimal default (try roughly a tenth of it), a finer --steps_per_crossing (this is required for stability once softening shrinks), and a longer --n_relax. Do genuinely unbound bodies now appear? Explain, physically, why the softening length that best approximates the smooth gravitational field of the cluster is not the softening length that best reproduces the close two-body encounters responsible for real evaporation — these are different goals, and this program's default optimizes for the first one.

Core sequence · intermediate to advanced

EXP-12 · intermediate/advanced

Chaos Doesn't Care How Small the Nudge Is

Run --mode chaos with --relative_perturbation set to 1e-4, 1e-6, 1e-8, 1e-10 and 1e-12 (fix --seed so the underlying cluster is the same each time; let --perturbation_seed vary or fix it too, your choice, but say which). Record the fitted Lyapunov time for each. The defining feature of exponential (chaotic) sensitivity is that the growth rate should not depend on how small the initial separation was, only how long it takes to grow to a fixed macroscopic size. Is that what you find? What happens to the estimate when the perturbation is so small that numerical roundoff, not the deliberate offset, dominates the initial separation?

EXP-13 · intermediate/advanced

Does the Relaxation-Time Formula Actually Predict Anything?

Run --mode cluster at two different --n_bodies (say 80 and 400) for the same number of relaxation times (fix --n_relax), and measure the fractional growth of r50 over the run in each case. The formula in Governing Equations is explicitly an order-of-magnitude estimate (it drops factors of order unity in the Coulomb logarithm). Is the growth per relaxation time roughly comparable between your two runs, as the formula's normalization would suggest, or do you find systematic disagreement? Either answer is useful — say which, and why an order-of-magnitude formula does or does not deserve to be taken further than that.

Optional projects · advanced

EXP-14 · advanced

Timestep Convergence: Direct Versus Tree

Run --mode galaxy --method direct at --steps_per_freefall of 40, 80 (default) and 160, holding everything else (including --seed) fixed, and record the maximum relative energy drift (reported in the energy-panel title) at each resolution. You should see it fall by roughly a factor of 4 with each doubling of resolution — the expected second-order convergence of the leapfrog scheme when the force itself is exact. Now repeat the same three runs with --method tree (default \(\theta\)). Does the drift improve the same way? It should not: the tree's fixed opening-angle force-approximation error does not shrink as \(\Delta t\) shrinks, so past a certain resolution the energy error is dominated by \(\theta\), not by the timestep, and refining \(\Delta t\) further buys you little or nothing. Read Algorithm and say, in your own words, what this implies about choosing \(\theta\) versus \(\Delta t\) when tuning a tree-code run for accuracy.

EXP-15 · advanced · uses the Python API

Push the Size Limit

Extend Experiment 9's isolated force-evaluation timing (not full CLI runs, for the same fixed-step-count reason) to the largest n_bodies your machine can finish in a reasonable time (try working up toward MAX_BODIES = 5,000, but watch your runtime as you go). Does the tree ever clearly win at the sizes you can actually test? What does that say about the practical difference between an algorithm's asymptotic complexity and the complexity that matters for a program of this scale and implementation?

EXP-16 · advanced · uses the Python API

Design Your Own Diagnostic

The program tracks Lagrangian radii, virial ratio, energy, instantaneous-unboundedness counts, and position-space divergence — but not everything. Using --csvdir to get the raw time series, or by calling physics_nbg functions directly on the arrays returned by run_cluster/run_galaxy, implement one diagnostic of your own choosing: for example, a density-profile power-law slope at fixed radius over time, a measure of departure from spherical symmetry, or total angular momentum conservation (formally zero for these initial conditions, but not exactly zero numerically — how far from zero, and why?). State clearly what physical question your diagnostic answers that the built-in ones do not.

Synthesis

EXP-17 · synthesis

Where Does “Collisionless” Stop Being True?

Galaxy-formation theory usually treats a galaxy as a collisionless system: stars feel the smooth mean gravitational field of everyone else, and individual close encounters are negligible over a Hubble time (this is what the relaxation time in Physical Background quantifies). Yet the cluster mode's whole point is to show two-body relaxation acting within an achievable run length, and Experiment 11 showed you can dial evaporation in or out with the softening length alone. Using what you found in Experiments 1, 7, 11 and 13, write a short (half-page) argument, in your own words and citing your own numbers from this program, for why real star clusters (\(N\sim10^3\)–\(10^6\), many relaxation times over a Hubble time) are collisional systems while real galaxies (\(N\sim10^{10}\)–\(10^{11}\) stars, one relaxation time vastly exceeding the age of the Universe) are, to very good approximation, not.

Domain of Validity

This program is a teaching instrument for tree-code gravity and collisionless stellar dynamics, not a research-grade N-body code. The following are absent by design, and several of them dominate real star clusters and galaxies in the regimes named.

Accepted range is not trustworthy range

The bounds enforced by the input checks are the values the program will accept. Several are wider than the range over which the results carry real physical weight, deliberately, so that pushing a parameter until the physics degrades is itself part of the exercise. The two ranges are:

What is absent

  • Individual or block timesteps. One global \(\Delta t\) is used for every body at every step. Production N-body codes give close, fast-moving pairs their own short timestep; this program does not, which is why a fixed-\(\Delta t\) run only marginally resolves the deepest moment of a cold collapse (see Algorithm and Experiment 14).
  • Multipole moments beyond the monopole. Each tree cell is represented purely by its total mass and center of mass. A quadrupole correction (as in most modern tree codes) would improve the tree's accuracy at fixed \(\theta\), at the cost of a noticeably more involved cell-cell interaction.
  • Mass spectrum and mass segregation. Every body carries identical mass. Real star clusters have a range of stellar masses, and two-body relaxation preferentially sinks the heaviest stars to the core (mass segregation) — a major driver of real cluster core evolution that an equal-mass model cannot show.
  • Stellar evolution, collisions, and binary formation. Bodies are point masses that never merge, lose mass, or form bound pairs through dissipative encounters; real cluster cores can produce hard binaries that measurably alter the cluster's energy budget.
  • Gas, hydrodynamics, and star formation. This is a pure collisionless gravity code; there is no gas component; the galaxy-formation mode is only a dark-matter-like or already-formed-stars toy problem, not a model of how gas turns into stars.
  • An external tidal field or host potential. Both cluster and galaxy modes model an isolated system in otherwise empty space; a real cluster orbiting within a galaxy loses stars to the host's tidal field, a process this program does not include.
  • General relativity. All gravity here is Newtonian; nothing in this program is intended to represent compact-object or strong-field physics.

Known model artefacts and caveats

  • The tree method does not conserve momentum exactly. Documented and quantified in Algorithm; use --method direct whenever exact conservation matters more than tree-code speed.
  • The default softening is tuned for the smooth field, not for close encounters. Dehnen (2001) optimal softening minimizes the RMS force error against the true (unsoftened) mean field. It does not attempt to preserve the close two-body encounters that drive real relaxation and evaporation, which is why the default cluster run so often shows zero instantaneously unbound bodies despite genuine relaxation-driven expansion (see Experiment 11 and the note in Physical Background).
  • The unboundedness criterion uses the instantaneous potential only. identify_unbound() flags a body as unbound when its specific energy (computed from the current configuration of every other body) is non-negative — the standard practical criterion, and the physically correct one regardless of which way the body happens to be moving at that instant: a positive-energy body on an inward-moving leg of its trajectory is still, correctly, unbound. What this function does not do is confirm the body never returns: it is evaluated snapshot by snapshot rather than by integrating the body's fate forward, so in a system undergoing violent relaxation the count of unbound bodies can rise and fall from one snapshot to the next rather than increasing monotonically, and a body flagged unbound at one snapshot can be re-flagged bound at a later one as the potential itself evolves. Read n_unbound as an instantaneous census, not a cumulative escape count. high_velocity_fraction() is provided as a softer, continuously varying companion diagnostic.
  • One global softening length, not a spatially varying one. The Dehnen scaling is evaluated once from \(N\) and the initial scale radius and then held fixed for the whole run, even as the system's density profile evolves. A softening length that tracked the local density would be more accurate but would also, again, change what physics the model is capturing.

What this program does capture honestly is the logic of the two big ideas it exists to teach: that approximating distant gravity buys you a genuinely different algorithm with genuinely different error properties (not just “the same answer, faster”), and that a collisionless-looking system of a few hundred bodies is not actually collisionless — two-body relaxation and chaotic sensitivity to initial conditions are both real, measurable, and, at this \(N\), fast.

Related Programs

  • Multiple — exact, direct-summation few-body Newtonian gravity, integrated with an adaptive predictor-corrector scheme. This program's explicit prerequisite: it assumes Multiple's two-body motion and energy/momentum bookkeeping are already familiar, and starts from where Multiple's exact \(O(N^2)\) approach stops being practical for exploring collective, many-body behavior — trading Multiple's adaptive, per-step-accurate integration for this program's fixed-timestep leapfrog scheme, which scales to far more bodies at the cost of that per-step accuracy.
  • Orbit — the two-body Newtonian orbit integrator whose basic machinery (positions, velocities, a fixed timestep, conserved quantities as a correctness check) this program's leapfrog loop generalizes to many bodies.
  • Binary — two-body orbital dynamics and evolution; useful background for the pairwise gravitational interactions that, summed over many bodies (directly or via the tree approximation), drive everything in this program.
