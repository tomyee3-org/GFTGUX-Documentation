Description

NbodyGalaxySimulator is a standalone gravitational N-body program built around a Barnes–Hut octree (Barnes & Hut 1986, Nature 324, 446) and Plummer-softened gravity. It exists to teach the two ideas that let a simulation move beyond a handful of exactly integrated bodies to hundreds or thousands of mutually interacting ones: force softening, which regularizes the otherwise-singular point-mass force at close range, and hierarchical force approximation, which reduces the cost of computing every body's force from every other body from \(O(N^2)\) to \(O(N\log N)\) by treating a distant clump of bodies as a single point mass.

One program covers three calculations, selected with --mode: cluster, a Plummer sphere of stars evolved for a chosen multiple of its own nominal two-body relaxation time, though seeing relaxation and evaporation clearly requires tuned (non-default) parameters, since the default softening actively suppresses both; galaxy, a cold uniform sphere that gravitationally collapses and “violently relaxes” (Lynden-Bell 1967) — when a cold, sufficiently resolved run's late-time half-mass radius and virial ratio have actually settled (the program checks this numerically rather than assuming it) — into a quasi-equilibrium remnant, the classic toy galaxy-formation experiment; and chaos, two indistinguishably close realizations of the same small cluster, integrated identically, whose separation is tracked to produce a finite-time estimate of the Lyapunov (exponential-divergence) time of gravitational motion, not a certified asymptotic value.

Complete Multiple's own tutorial and exercises before starting this program — that is a prerequisite, not a suggestion. Multiple already covers exact, direct-summation Newtonian gravity for a handful of bodies, integrated with an adaptive predictor-corrector scheme accurate enough to follow a single close encounter. This program does not re-derive Newtonian two-body motion or re-teach what a gravitational acceleration is; it starts exactly where Multiple's exact \(O(N^2)\) approach stops being practical, and introduces two ideas Multiple has no need for — a fixed-timestep leapfrog integrator and hierarchical (tree) force approximation — to reach fifty, five hundred, or five thousand bodies. It is written as a standalone program rather than an added mode of Multiple, deliberately, so that tree codes, force softening and their attendant subtleties do not intrude on Multiple's own introductory tutorial flow.
What this program is, and what it is not. This is a from-scratch, transparent, pure-Python teaching implementation, not a research N-body code. There is no individual (per-body) or block timestepping, no higher-order symplectic integrator, no quadrupole or higher multipole correction in the tree (monopole only), no parallelism or GPU acceleration, no gas or radiative physics, no external tidal field or cosmological expansion, and every simulation particle carries the same mass. Three limits are worth knowing before your first run: the tree code's wall-clock speed advantage over direct summation is not guaranteed at the body counts this program targets — see the Algorithm section for measured numbers and why; two-body evaporation is a slow process that the default softening actively suppresses (softening chosen for force accuracy also damps the close encounters that physically drive relaxation); and a fixed global timestep only marginally resolves the sharp density spike at the deepest point of a cold collapse. All three are turned into explicit exercises below rather than hidden.

Physical Background

Why direct summation stops scaling

Multiple computes every body's acceleration by summing the exact Newtonian contribution from every other body: for \(N\) bodies that is \(N(N-1)/2\) pairwise terms, so the cost grows as \(O(N^2)\). Doubling \(N\) quadruples the work. That is entirely practical for the handful of bodies in a planetary or few-star encounter, which is exactly Multiple's domain, but it becomes prohibitive well before \(N\) reaches even a few thousand. A star cluster with \(10^5\)–\(10^6\) stars, or a galaxy with far more, is simply out of reach of direct summation on any one machine.

Barnes & Hut (1986) noticed that a distant clump of bodies barely needs to be resolved individually: to good approximation, its combined gravitational pull can be replaced by a single point mass at its center of mass. Organizing the bodies into a spatial tree (an octree in three dimensions) and applying that approximation wherever it is accurate enough reduces the cost to \(O(N\log N)\). This is the central algorithmic idea of this program (see Algorithm below); its price is an approximate rather than exact force.

Why gravity needs softening here

The point-mass Newtonian force, \(F \propto 1/r^2\), diverges as two bodies approach each other. Multiple handles this by raising an explicit error the instant two bodies coincide, because with an adaptive, accurate integrator a genuine close encounter should simply be resolved in fine timestep detail, not glossed over. That approach does not extend to hundreds of bodies on a fixed timestep: a single close pair would demand an enormously smaller timestep than every other body needs, and Multiple's per-run predictor-corrector accuracy targets do not exist here at all — the leapfrog scheme below uses one fixed timestep for every body, for the whole run.

The standard remedy, used throughout this program, is Plummer softening: replace the Newtonian potential's \(1/r\) with \(1/\sqrt{r^2+\epsilon^2}\), where \(\epsilon\) is the softening length; taking the gradient of that softened potential gives the \((r^2+\epsilon^2)^{3/2}\) denominator in the force law shown below, not \(r^2+\epsilon^2\) itself. This has a direct physical reading: a simulation particle here rarely represents one literal star (or one dark-matter particle); it stands in for a much larger aggregate, smeared over some finite size, and \(\epsilon\) is a reasonable proxy for that size. It also has a purely numerical reading: it caps the maximum force two bodies can exert on each other, which keeps a fixed-timestep integrator stable through what would otherwise be arbitrarily close encounters. Both readings matter here, and they pull in the same direction for choosing \(\epsilon\) — but, as the Algorithm section discusses, that same suppression of close encounters also suppresses two-body relaxation itself, which is exactly the process the cluster mode is trying to show.

Two-body relaxation and evaporation

A self-gravitating system of discrete bodies is not the same as a smooth mass distribution: individual gravitational encounters between bodies exchange energy and gradually drive the system toward a broader, more relaxed velocity distribution — and a system of point masses in a finite volume has, in principle, no upper limit on relative speed reachable this way. Over many crossing times, enough encounters accumulate that some bodies are kicked above the local escape speed and leave for good: this is evaporation. The characteristic timescale for this diffusive process, the two-body relaxation time, is given below; for an isolated system without a tidal boundary, full evaporation of a Plummer-like cluster is a slow process, of order a hundred or more relaxation times (Ambartsumian 1938; Spitzer 1987).

Violent relaxation and cold collapse

A self-gravitating system released far from equilibrium — the galaxy mode's cold, uniform sphere is the classic case — does not settle down through the slow, encounter-by-encounter process above. Instead, while the overall mass distribution is still changing rapidly (during the collapse and the violent oscillations that follow it), the gravitational potential itself fluctuates on a dynamical timescale, and those fluctuations exchange energy between bodies far faster than individual two-body encounters could. Lynden-Bell (1967) named this violent relaxation and argued it explains why collisionless self-gravitating systems (galaxies, above all) reach a quasi-equilibrium, centrally concentrated state so much faster than their two-body relaxation time would allow. Direct numerical experiments of exactly this cold-collapse scenario (van Albada 1982, and many since) show the relaxation is generically incomplete in the sense that the remnant is not a smooth, isotropic equilibrium model: it carries an extended, non-Maxwellian halo of loosely bound bodies flung out during the collapse, and a density profile only qualitatively like a real galaxy's. That does not mean its virial ratio must sit visibly below 1 — in this program's own runs the remnant's virial ratio oscillates in a band close to 1 (see the virial theorem note below and Suggested Experiments), consistent with a system that is virialized on average while still relaxing in detail.

The virial theorem

For a self-gravitating system in a statistical steady state, the virial theorem requires \(2T + W_{\rm vir} = 0\), i.e. \(2T/|W_{\rm vir}| = 1\), where \(T\) is the system's internal kinetic energy, measured relative to its own instantaneous center-of-mass velocity, and \(W_{\rm vir} \equiv \sum_i \vec{r}_i\cdot\vec{F}_i\) is the virial (force-weighted) sum — not the softened potential energy \(U\). \(T\) is deliberately not the same kinetic-energy quantity used for the total-energy conservation check elsewhere on this page: that check needs the raw (lab-frame) kinetic energy, since it is what the integrator actually conserves, while the virial theorem is a statement about internal dynamics only — a uniform bulk velocity added to every body changes the lab-frame kinetic energy without changing the system's internal state at all, and would silently distort the reported ratio if it were used here. \(W_{\rm vir}\) and \(U\) also differ whenever softening is active: \(W_{\rm vir}\) is what actually enters the scalar virial theorem for a softened force law, while \(U\) is the energy bookkeeping quantity used in the total-energy conservation check. They coincide only in the unsoftened limit \(\epsilon\to 0\). This program reports \(2T/|W_{\rm vir}|\) throughout as the virial ratio: substantially below 1 means the system is still contracting (or under-supported), substantially above 1 means it is expanding (or was released with excess kinetic energy), and for a settled remnant a value oscillating in a modest band around 1 is consistent with a properly virialized system — a value that instead sits persistently far from 1 more often points to an under-resolved run (too few crossing times, too coarse a timestep, or softening much larger or smaller than the default) than to a genuine physical departure from balance. What this ratio does and does not show: \(2T/|W_{\rm vir}|=1\) is a single global, instantaneous constraint on the two scalars \(T\) and \(W_{\rm vir}\) — it is exact scalar virial balance, not by itself proof of a genuine dynamical (phase-space) equilibrium. cluster initial conditions are always rescaled to exact scalar virial balance, \(Q=1\), for the actual discrete, softened realization; galaxy initial conditions are rescaled to whatever initial virial ratio \(Q_0\) was requested (see Parameters and Suggested Experiments), which equals scalar virial balance only when \(Q_0=1\) is requested. Even when \(Q=1\) at \(t=0\), that is a necessary condition for a settled state but not a sufficient one: a system can be rescaled to \(2T/|W_{\rm vir}|=1\) by a single global velocity rescale while still being far from phase-space stationarity, and a modest readjustment transient over the first few crossing times is a normal, physically uninteresting consequence of that rescale, not evidence of two-body relaxation on its own.

Chaos in gravitating systems

Generic gravitational systems with three or more mutually interacting bodies exhibit chaotic sensitivity to initial conditions: two initial conditions that differ by an arbitrarily small amount typically separate exponentially in phase space, rather than growing only linearly as they would in a well-behaved, non-chaotic dynamical system — though special regular or symmetric configurations (a small number of exact periodic solutions among an infinite space of initial conditions) also exist and are not themselves chaotic. Miller (1964) first demonstrated this numerically for self-gravitating stellar systems; later work (e.g. Goodman, Heggie & Hut 1993) found that for small-\(N\) self-gravitating clusters the associated Lyapunov time — the e-folding time of that exponential growth, where one exists — is typically of order a few crossing times. This program's chaos mode reproduces that generic behavior for its randomly sampled initial conditions: see Governing Equations and Algorithm.

Governing Equations

Softened Newtonian gravity
\[ \vec{a}_i \;=\; G \sum_{j \ne i} \frac{m_j \,(\vec{x}_j - \vec{x}_i)}{\left(|\vec{x}_j-\vec{x}_i|^2 + \epsilon^2\right)^{3/2}} \]

\(\epsilon\) is the (fixed) softening length. \(\epsilon \to 0\) recovers the exact point-mass law used by Multiple.

Barnes–Hut opening-angle criterion
\[ \frac{s}{d} < \theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{treat this tree node as one point mass at its center of mass} \]

\(s\) is the node's (cubical) box size, \(d\) the distance from the body to the node's center of mass, and \(\theta\) the opening angle (--theta). \(\theta=0\) forces a full descent to individual bodies for every force evaluation (exact monopole summation, at direct-summation-like cost); \(\theta\) of order 0.5–0.8 is the usual working range.

Leapfrog (kick-drift-kick) integration
\[ \vec{v}_{1/2} = \vec{v}_0 + \tfrac{1}{2}\vec{a}(\vec{x}_0)\,\Delta t, \qquad \vec{x}_1 = \vec{x}_0 + \vec{v}_{1/2}\,\Delta t, \qquad \vec{v}_1 = \vec{v}_{1/2} + \tfrac{1}{2}\vec{a}(\vec{x}_1)\,\Delta t \]
Plummer (1911) density profile and enclosed mass
\[ \rho(r) = \frac{3M}{4\pi a^3}\left(1+\frac{r^2}{a^2}\right)^{-5/2}, \qquad \frac{M(r)}{M} = \frac{r^3}{(r^2+a^2)^{3/2}} \]

Sampled by inverting the enclosed-mass relation for \(r\) given a uniform random \(x_1\in(0,1)\), and drawing speeds by rejection sampling against the exact isotropic Plummer distribution function (Aarseth, Hénon & Wielen 1974).

Athanassoula et al. (2000) optimal softening
\[ \epsilon_{\rm opt} = 0.98\, a\, N^{-0.26} \]

The default softening length when --softening_pc is not given, minimizing the mean-square force error against the smooth Plummer field. This program also applies it, as a deliberate but UNVALIDATED heuristic reuse rather than a separately derived result, to the uniform-sphere galaxy initial condition, which is not the density profile it was fit to (Athanassoula, Fady, Lambert & Bosma 2000; see References).

Characteristic timescales
\[ t_{\rm cross} = \sqrt{\frac{r_h^3}{GM}}, \qquad t_{\rm ff} = \sqrt{\frac{3\pi}{32\,G\bar\rho}}, \qquad t_{\rm relax} \approx \frac{N}{8\ln N}\, t_{\rm cross} \]

\(r_h\) is the half-mass radius; \(\bar\rho\) the mean density. The relaxation-time expression follows Binney & Tremaine, Galactic Dynamics (2nd ed.), eq. 1.38, with the Coulomb logarithm approximated as \(\ln N\); treat it as a nominal, UNSOFTENED order-of-magnitude scaling, not a precise prediction (real N-body codes commonly use \(\ln(0.4N)\) or \(\ln(N/2)\) instead, changing the result by an order-unity factor). It assumes point-mass two-body encounters at arbitrarily close range — exactly what this program's force softening exists to suppress — so it is not a prediction of how fast this program's own softened, discrete cluster-mode realization will actually relax; the true softened rate is suppressed below this nominal value, sometimes to the point of no measurable relaxation at all within a practical run length at default softening (see Physical Background, the credibility table, and Experiment 11).

Virial ratio and instantaneous unboundedness criterion
\[ Q \equiv \frac{2T}{|W_{\rm vir}|}, \qquad e_i \equiv \tfrac{1}{2}v_i^2 + \Phi_i, \qquad e_i > 0 \Leftrightarrow \text{body } i \text{ is instantaneously unbound} \]

\(\Phi_i\) is the softened potential at body \(i\) due to every other body, evaluated in the system's own center-of-mass frame; \(W_{\rm vir}\) is the virial (force-weighted) sum defined above, not the softened potential energy. The energy criterion \(e_i>0\) is evaluated exactly as written — regardless of whether body \(i\) happens to be moving inward or outward at that instant — so it flags bodies that are unbound right now, not bodies that have permanently left the system; a body can cross back below \(e_i=0\) on a later snapshot; see Known Model Artefacts.

Heuristic finite-time Lyapunov-exponent fit
\[ \Delta(t) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_i |\vec{x}_{A,i}(t)-\vec{x}_{B,i}(t)|^2} \;\sim\; \Delta_0\, e^{\lambda t} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t_{\rm Lyap} = 1/\lambda \]

\(\vec{x}_A\) and \(\vec{x}_B\) are each recentred on their own mass-weighted center of mass before differencing, so a coherent overall translation between the two realizations cannot masquerade as divergence; \(\Delta\) is therefore a position-space separation, not a phase-space one (velocities are not included). \(\lambda\) is estimated by an ordinary least-squares fit of \(\ln\Delta(t)\) against \(t\), over the longest contiguous run of snapshots where \(\Delta\) has grown past \(3\Delta_0\) but not yet past half of its own eventual maximum, requiring at least 5 points in that window. Three further checks decide whether that window is trustworthy enough to report a rate at all. First, the window's own log-amplitude span — how many natural-log e-foldings of growth it actually covers — must reach at least \(\ln(10)\approx2.303\) (one order of magnitude): a curve need only look log-linear over a short, narrow slice of amplitude to be indistinguishable from a true exponential there (a logistic/saturating curve is, by construction, asymptotically exponential in its own early-growth regime), but a genuine chaotic instability keeps growing across many orders of magnitude before it saturates near the system's own size, so requiring real dynamic range is what actually separates the two in practice — a representative chaos-mode run's selected window typically spans roughly 16-17 e-folds, comfortably above the threshold, while a saturating (logistic-like) curve's early, still-plausibly-exponential-looking phase spans only about 2.1, comfortably below it. Second, the whole-window fit must reach \(R^2\ge0.90\), rejecting a window with no clear overall log-linear trend at all. Third, a separate quadratic-in-\(t\) regression, \(\ln\Delta = c_0+c_1t+c_2t^2\), is fit not to the window's raw points but to their means within 20 fixed, time-ordered bins (\(t\) centered and scaled purely for numerical conditioning, which leaves the resulting statistic mathematically unchanged) — binning first, rather than fitting the raw points directly, is necessary because the raw points are dense, correlated samples of one smooth curve rather than independent draws, so a raw-point fit's apparent statistical strength grows mechanically with how densely a run happens to be recorded, flagging an unchanged trajectory as more "curved" purely because more of it was stored; averaging down to a fixed number of representative bins removes that spurious dependence. The window is rejected if the binned fit's curvature coefficient's own OLS \(t\) statistic exceeds 10 in magnitude — this catches a different failure mode than the e-fold check: a family of stretched/compressed-exponential curves, \(\Delta(t)=\Delta_0\exp\!\big(A(t/T)^p\big)\) for \(p\ne1\), can accumulate more than an order of magnitude of growth (passing the second check) and still fit a straight line in log space acceptably well (passing \(R^2\ge0.90\)) while following a genuinely different growth law throughout, not merely a locally-exponential early slice of one, and it is exactly this genuine difference in shape that the curvature statistic is built to detect. For this program's own dynamics, a genuinely chaotic divergence trace typically reaches a curvature-statistic magnitude well under 10, while that same stretched/compressed-exponential family, and a noisy quadratic growth curve, typically score at least about 12 in magnitude — a comfortable, non-overlapping margin on both sides of the threshold. This binned design does NOT, however, reliably reject every smooth non-exponential shape: a logistic curve is, by construction, asymptotically exponential during its own early growth, so a logistic whose selected window happens to clear the e-fold floor above by only a moderate margin can still show too little curvature, within that specific narrow window, for this check to catch — a sufficiently tall logistic curve sampled this way is still accepted roughly two-thirds of the time (see Known Model Artefacts). This is a heuristic gate, not a formal, rigorously calibrated hypothesis test, and it is not guaranteed to accept every genuinely chaotic run or reject every possible adversarial input. A run for which no window passes every gate reports no Lyapunov exponent (with an explanatory note) rather than a misleading one — inspect the plotted divergence curve directly in that case, since a real exponential stretch may still be visible even when this automated check does not confirm it.

Algorithm

Building and walking the octree

Each force evaluation over the whole body set (once per leapfrog half-step) proceeds in two stages. First, build_octree() recursively partitions the smallest cube enclosing all bodies into eight octants, subdividing any octant holding more than one body, down to a hard recursion-depth limit (MAX_TREE_DEPTH) that guarantees termination even for exactly or nearly coincident bodies — no finite number of halvings separates two identical points into different octants, so without this limit such a configuration would recurse forever. Every internal node stores its total mass and center of mass. Second, for each body, the tree is walked from the root: an internal node is accepted as a single point mass whenever the opening-angle criterion is satisfied; otherwise the walk descends into that node's (up to eight) children. A node whose accumulated mass is exactly zero (an empty octant) is skipped. One further check runs before the opening-angle test on every candidate node: because a body's own position is one of the points that went into that node's mass and center of mass, a node that geometrically contains the target body's own position is never accepted as a monopole, regardless of \(\theta\) — the walk is forced to descend into its children instead, all the way to the leaf actually holding that body, so the body's own mass can never be approximated as pulling on itself. (In the direct-summation path this is not needed, since the \(i=j\) term is simply excluded from the sum by index.) Omitting this containment test creates a spurious self-force at coarse \(\theta\): a body could then be accepted as pulling on a node that geometrically contains its own position, which produces no error and is invisible at small \(\theta\) or when bodies are well separated — it only shows up as a large, unphysical force once a body and a coarse, large-\(\theta\) node happen to overlap.

Because both columns range this widely across ordinary seeds, a single reproduction attempt landing near the edge of (or briefly outside) these ranges is not, by itself, evidence that anything is wrong.

The tree code is not guaranteed to be faster in wall-clock terms at this program's scale. The \(O(N\log N)\) versus \(O(N^2)\) argument compares operation COUNTS. This is a pure-Python implementation: the tree walk is an explicit-stack traversal of Python objects, while direct summation is a single vectorized NumPy call per body. NumPy's vectorization has such a small constant factor per floating-point operation that, on ordinary hardware, direct summation remains faster in wall-clock time than the tree at every body count up to MAX_BODIES = 5,000 — the crossover the operation-count argument predicts is real, but for an implementation shaped like this one it lies at a much larger \(N\) than a naive reading of “\(O(N\log N)\) beats \(O(N^2)\)” would suggest. This is not a defect to fix quietly; it is Experiment 9 below, and a genuine lesson in numerical methods: asymptotic complexity and measured wall-clock performance are different questions, and the constant factor a real implementation carries can dominate at every size you can practically test. --method lets you run either force law directly for exactly this comparison; the tree remains the pedagogically important algorithm regardless of which one wins a stopwatch race in this particular code.
The tree code does not conserve momentum exactly. This program's direct-summation implementation loops once per target body and, for each one, sums the exact softened contribution from every other body (a full source-loop evaluation per target, vectorized over sources); it does not literally compute one shared value per pair and apply it with a flipped sign to both bodies. Momentum is nonetheless conserved to machine precision (typical measured drift: relative to a natural momentum scale, of order \(10^{-16}\) over hundreds of steps), because the underlying force LAW is exactly antisymmetric between any pair (\(\vec F_{ij}=-\vec F_{ji}\) by construction), so the two independently-evaluated contributions cancel algebraically even though the code does not share the computation between them. The tree's monopole approximation breaks this pairwise symmetry at the level of the approximation itself, not merely at the level of code structure: body A may see body B's cell approximated as a single distant mass, while body B's own force evaluation does not necessarily see an equal and opposite contribution from a matching approximation of A's surroundings. Measured total momentum drift with the tree method is small but real (typically a few times \(10^{-4}\) relative to \(M\langle v\rangle\) over a few hundred crossing times at \(\theta=0.5\)) and grows with θ. This is a known, published property of Barnes–Hut codes, not a bug in this implementation; see Experiment 10.

Timestep selection and energy conservation

Leapfrog is a second-order symplectic integrator when the force it integrates is a genuine conservative force — a guarantee this program only establishes for method="direct". There, for a fixed timestep, leapfrog does not conserve energy exactly, but its energy error oscillates around a roughly constant offset rather than drifting secularly the way a non-symplectic scheme (e.g. forward Euler) would, provided the timestep resolves the local dynamical time; measured directly through a cold collapse, the maximum SAMPLED energy drift (evaluated only at stored snapshots, not every integration step) falls by close to the expected factor of 4 each time --steps_per_freefall is doubled (see Experiment 14). The tree's monopole force is not pairwise-symmetric and has not been shown to be the gradient of any single potential, so the symplectic argument does not carry over to method="tree" in the same way; measured directly, tree-method energy drift does not shrink appreciably with a smaller timestep, because it is dominated by the fixed force-approximation error set by \(\theta\), not by the timestep's own truncation error — refining \(\Delta t\) mainly buys accuracy for direct summation, while refining \(\theta\) is what matters for the tree. Each mode chooses its own default timestep as a fraction of a physically meaningful timescale computed from the requested initial condition (a fraction of the crossing time for cluster and chaos; of the free-fall time for galaxy) so that a default run is well resolved without the student needing to already know what a sound \(\Delta t\) looks like.

A fixed global timestep only marginally resolves the deepest moment of a cold collapse. During a galaxy-mode run's deepest collapse, the local dynamical time inside the transient density spike is far shorter than the free-fall time the default timestep was sized from, and energy conservation measurably worsens through that moment (typically to the percent level for the default parameters). A production N-body code would use individual or block timesteps for exactly this reason; this program uses one global \(\Delta t\) throughout, by design, to keep the leapfrog loop transparent. See Experiment 14.

There are likewise two distinct kinetic-energy columns: kinetic_J is the raw, lab-frame total kinetic energy (so that energy_J = kinetic_J + potential_J exactly, matching the quantity the integrator conserves), while kinetic_com_J is the system's internal kinetic energy, measured relative to its own instantaneous center-of-mass velocity; virial_ratio is computed from kinetic_com_J, not kinetic_J — i.e. virial_ratio \(\approx\) 2*kinetic_com_J/abs(virial_work_J) — so that a uniform bulk velocity applied to the whole system (which changes kinetic_J but has nothing to do with internal virial balance) leaves the reported virial ratio unchanged. n_unbound is an integer count using the instantaneous unboundedness criterion (specific energy > 0 relative to the system, evaluated at that snapshot regardless of the body's current radial motion — see Known Model Artefacts); high_velocity_fraction is a fraction in [0, 1].

A few of the intermediate-to-advanced ones (marked accordingly) additionally call a physics_nbg function directly from a short Python snippet, or time a run via driver_nbg.run() instead of the CLI — this is called out explicitly where it happens, and none of it requires reading or modifying the program's own source. Core sequence · introductory

EXP-1 · introductory

Does a Default Cluster Actually Relax?

Run python main.py --mode cluster --seed 0. Read the Lagrangian radii r10 and r90 at the start and end from the printed summary or the Lagrangian-radii panel, and note n_unbound and the near-escape tail. Two-body relaxation is supposed to drive a self-gravitating system's core to contract while its outskirts expand as stars exchange energy — but at this program's default softening, a short default run often shows only a small, non-monotonic change in both radii (sometimes even the "wrong" direction relative to that textbook story), because the default softening actively suppresses the close encounters that drive relaxation (see Physical Background) and because the initial virial-balance rescale itself produces a brief readjustment transient that is NOT relaxation. Repeat with --seed 1 and --seed 2: is any core-contracts/outskirts-expand pattern consistent across seeds, or is it dominated by run-to-run scatter? Now try Experiment 11's reduced-softening setup and compare — that is where this program's relaxation signal actually becomes reliably visible.

EXP-2 · introductory

A Cold Sphere Collapses

Run python main.py --mode galaxy. From the printed summary, note the initial virial ratio, the virial ratio at the deepest collapse, and the final virial ratio (the summary prints all three). Those three numbers alone can tell you whether the run was monotonic or not (e.g. whether the deepest-collapse value is far from both the initial and final ones) but three points cannot by themselves show a genuine repeated oscillation — predict, from just those three numbers, whether \(2T/|W_{\rm vir}|\) looks like it settled at a single value or is still moving by the end of the run, THEN look at the plotted virial-ratio panel (see Output) to see its actual behavior across every recorded snapshot. Does the plotted curve end at exactly 1, the value corresponding to exact scalar virial balance, or does it keep oscillating in a band around 1 for as long as the run lasts? Read the virial theorem note in Physical Background — including its caveat that scalar balance is not, by itself, proof of a settled dynamical equilibrium — to see why a settled oscillation near 1, not a single converged number, is the expected signature here.

EXP-3 · introductory

How Fast Do Nearby Orbits Diverge?

Run python main.py --mode chaos. If the divergence panel's title reports a fitted Lyapunov time (or the terminal summary shows one), express it as a multiple of the initial crossing time; Miller (1964) and later studies put this ratio at a few crossing times for self-gravitating N-body systems — is your result in that range? Run it again (no --seed) and see whether the estimate is stable from realization to realization. This program's fit is a heuristic, permissive-but-imperfect gate (see Governing Equations), so some runs will legitimately report no fit at all — if that happens, look at the plotted divergence curve directly: does it still show a stretch of roughly straight-line growth on the logarithmic axis, even though the automated check didn't confirm it? Either outcome (a numeric answer, or a documented non-result with a visual read of the curve) is an acceptable result for this exercise.

EXP-4 · introductory

Meet the Two Force Methods

Run the same cluster twice, once with --method tree and once with --method direct, using a fixed --seed so both start identically. Compare final energy, final virial ratio and the Lagrangian radii. They will not be pixel-identical — the tree only approximates the force — but they should tell a consistent physical story. If they disagreed wildly, what would that tell you about \(\theta\)?

EXP-5 · introductory/intermediate · pilot-study caveat

Does Normalizing by the N/ln N Formula Collapse the Curves?

Run --mode cluster with --n_bodies set to 60, 120, 250 and 500, holding --total_mass_msun and --scale_radius_pc fixed at their defaults, fixing --seed 0 at every N, and giving each the same --n_relax. Because this program's physical run length is itself n_relax times the N-dependent NOMINAL relaxation time (see Governing Equations, which scales roughly as \(N/\ln N\)), fixing n_relax already makes each run's physical duration and step count scale by roughly that same \(N/\ln N\) factor — a much longer run for N=500 than for N=60. This means the experiment CANNOT be used to ask whether the raw amount of relaxation grows with N in absolute terms: that comparison is exactly what fixing n_relax normalizes away by construction. What it CAN ask is a different, still useful question: does the amount of core contraction/outskirt expansion you observe come out roughly comparable across all four N values, despite their very different absolute run lengths? If the relaxation-time formula is doing real work capturing how relaxation scales with N, comparable outcomes across N at fixed n_relax is exactly what you would expect; a result that instead varies wildly and systematically with N (after accounting for run-to-run scatter) would suggest the formula is not fully capturing this discrete, softened system's actual behavior. Two things confound a clean reading and should be kept in mind rather than treated as noise: this program's default softening changes with N (\(\epsilon_{\rm opt}\propto N^{-0.26}\)), so the close-encounter physics is not held fixed across your four runs; and a single seed per N cannot separate a genuine N-dependence from that one realization's own sampling scatter (repeat at least one N value with a second seed to get a feel for the scatter's size). Treat this as a qualitative pilot check of whether the formula's normalization is self-consistent, not a clean quantitative verification of the \(N/\ln N\) scaling itself.

The free-fall time for a uniform sphere scales as \(t_{\rm ff}\propto\rho^{-1/2}\propto (R^3/M)^{1/2}\): predict the two new free-fall times before running, then check.

EXP-7 · intermediate

Cold, Warm and Hot Collapse

Run --mode galaxy with --virial_ratio_init set to 0.0 (the cold default), 1.0 (started in exact instantaneous scalar virial balance, in this program's \(2T/|W_{\rm vir}|\) convention) and 3.0 (strongly supervirial). EXP-11 · intermediate · uses the Python API

Softening Is Hiding Your Unbound Bodies

Run the default cluster and note n_unbound (instantaneously positive-energy bodies) at the end — it is very often zero. Now rerun with --softening_pc set well below the Athanassoula-et-al.-optimal default, a finer --steps_per_crossing (this is required for stability once softening shrinks), and a longer --n_relax, e.g. python main.py --mode cluster --n_bodies 60 --softening_pc 0.0338 --steps_per_crossing 150 --n_relax 40 --seed 0. Treat this as a starting point to try, not a guaranteed outcome: this is a chaotic calculation, and how many instantaneously-unbound bodies it ends with (out of 60) varies by seed, including seeds that end with none at all — do not expect every seed to show a nonzero count. Run the same command across several seeds and report the FRACTION of seeds that show at least one instantaneously-unbound body, and discuss why that fraction is not 100% even at this lowered softening. For stronger evidence that a flagged body is genuinely escaping (rather than briefly crossing back and forth), you need per-body identity across snapshots, which the CLI/CSV output does not carry — call the Python API directly instead:

import numpy as np
import physics_nbg as phys
r = phys.run_cluster(n_bodies=60, total_mass_msun=1e3, scale_radius_pc=1.0,
                      softening_pc=0.0338, steps_per_crossing=150,
                      n_relax=40, seed=0)
n_snap = len(r["t"])
for k in range(max(n_snap - 5, 0), n_snap):    # the last 5 stored snapshots
    pos, vel, m = r["positions"][k], r["velocities"][k], r["masses"]
    e = phys.specific_energies(pos, vel, m, r["softening"])
    com = phys.center_of_mass(pos, m)
    com_vel = phys.center_of_mass_velocity(vel, m)
    rel_pos = pos - com                        # center-relative position (m)
    rel_vel = vel - com_vel                    # center-relative velocity (m/s):
                                                # dr/dt needs velocity measured
                                                # in the SAME frame as the
                                                # radius, or a bulk cluster
                                                # drift alone (which changes no
                                                # distance between bodies) would
                                                # masquerade as inward/outward
                                                # motion
    radius_m = np.linalg.norm(rel_pos, axis=1)
    with np.errstate(invalid="ignore"):
        v_radial_m_s = np.divide(
            np.einsum("ij,ij->i", rel_pos, rel_vel), radius_m,
            out=np.full_like(radius_m, np.nan), where=radius_m > 0)
    radius_pc = radius_m / phys.PC                        # r/v arrays are SI;
    v_radial_pc_myr = v_radial_m_s * phys.MYR / phys.PC   # convert to match
                                                            # the printed labels
    for i in np.nonzero(e > 0)[0]:
        print(f"snapshot {k}  body {i}  e={e[i]:.3e}  "
              f"r={radius_pc[i]:.4g} pc  v_r={v_radial_pc_myr[i]:.4g} pc/Myr")

and check whether the same body ID reappears across every one of those five snapshots with a growing radius and a positive radial velocity at every snapshot — not merely at the one moment it was first flagged, and not just once or twice (v_r itself need not keep growing; it only needs to stay positive for the radius to keep increasing). A body whose radius keeps increasing and whose v_r stays positive at every one of those snapshots is showing stronger evidence of a persistent escape, not proof of one — five snapshots is still a short window against the evolving potential this Help file describes elsewhere; one that shows up once, or whose radius stalls or turns back over, or whose v_r sign flips, is more likely a body that is only briefly, instantaneously unbound before falling back in. You should see it fall by roughly a factor of 4 with each doubling of resolution — the expected second-order convergence of the leapfrog scheme when the force itself is exact. It should not: the tree's fixed opening-angle force-approximation error does not shrink as \(\Delta t\) shrinks, so past a certain resolution the energy error is dominated by \(\theta\), not by the timestep, and refining \(\Delta t\) further buys you little or nothing. EXP-17 · advanced · multi-seed

Which Remnant Features Survive Changing N and Softening?

Galaxy mode's particles are a scaled-down stand-in for a much larger real system (see run_galaxy()'s docstring and Physical Background), so any apparent feature of the collapsed remnant — a particular final half-mass radius, a specific late-time virial-ratio oscillation amplitude, a visible density concentration — needs to survive a resolution check before you trust it as physics rather than as a numerical artifact of this particular N, softening, or realization. A single run per N is not enough to show that: a shared --seed does not produce matched realizations across different N (the initial-condition sampler draws a different amount of random state at each N), so one run per N confounds genuine resolution trends with ordinary seed-to-seed scatter, exactly the pitfall EXP-13 warns about for cluster mode.

Part A — N convergence. Run --mode galaxy at --n_bodies of 100, 300 (default) and 1000, using several different seeds at EACH N (e.g. 4-5 seeds per N). Do NOT simply leave softening at its default for all three: this program's default softening is itself a function of N (epsilon = 0.98 * scale_radius * N^-0.26, printed in every run's summary as Softening), so leaving it unset changes N and softening together, and any difference you see could be caused by either one. To make this an N-ONLY comparison, run once at N=300 first, read its default softening value off the summary, and then pass that SAME number explicitly via --softening_pc at N=100 and N=1000 as well, so every run in this part shares one fixed physical softening length. (Deliberately varying N and its default softening together instead is a legitimate but DIFFERENT experiment — a joint N/epsilon refinement path, not an N-only resolution test — and should be labeled as such if you choose to run it.) For each N, collect the final half-mass radius and the late-time virial-ratio range across your seeds, and report each group's spread as its RANGE (maximum minus minimum across your 4-5 seeds at that N) — a specific, easy-to-compute number, not an unspecified "spread." Comparing detection to convergence is the whole point of this exercise, so keep the two questions separate: a change in a feature's typical value FROM ONE N TO THE NEXT that is clearly LARGER than the seed-to-seed range WITHIN a single N is real evidence that this feature genuinely DEPENDS on resolution at these N — report it, and note that it is evidence AGAINST having converged, not for it (unless you can also show that change shrinking further as N grows, which this exercise's three N values alone are too few to establish). When the between-N change instead stays comparable to (not clearly larger than) the within-N range, that does NOT prove the feature has converged — four or five seeds only estimate the true scatter roughly, and a real but moderate resolution dependence can easily hide inside that much noise. Call that outcome INCONCLUSIVE, or "no N-dependence detected at this resolution and seed count," rather than "survives" or "behaves as converged."

Part B — softening sensitivity. Separately, holding N and the scale radius fixed at their defaults, repeat the same multi-seed comparison at the Athanassoula-et-al.-optimal softening for N=300 (printed in the summary) and at roughly half and roughly double that same numeric value, again passed explicitly via --softening_pc at all three settings. Report each group's spread the same way as Part A (the range across seeds at that softening). Apply the same logic as Part A, not its mirror image: a between-softening change in a feature's typical value that is clearly larger than the within-softening seed-to-seed range demonstrates real softening SENSITIVITY (evidence against this feature being robust to your softening choice) — report it. A between-softening change that stays comparable to the within-softening range is, again, INCONCLUSIVE at this seed count, not proof of robustness. Keep this axis separate from Part A — do not change N and softening in the same run, or you will not know which one caused any difference you see.

Across both parts, resist reading "I didn't detect a difference" as "there is no difference": with only 4-5 seeds per setting, a range-based comparison has real power to catch a LARGE dependence and comparatively little power to rule out a moderate one. A demonstrated, clearly-larger-than-range change is trustworthy evidence of a real dependence on N, softening, or realization, and should be read as not-yet-converged (or genuinely softening-sensitive) physics rather than as noise. An inconclusive comparison is exactly that — inconclusive, at most weak support for treating a feature as resolution-independent, not a demonstration of convergence or robustness; strengthening it takes more seeds, more N/softening values, or a trend of shrinking differences toward a stable limit, not a bigger claim from the same four or five runs. This is the same kind of resolution question Experiment 5 asks for cluster mode, applied to the collapse remnant instead of the relaxation rate.

EXP-18 · synthesis

Where Does “Collisionless” Stop Being True?

Galaxy-formation theory usually treats a galaxy as a collisionless system: stars feel the smooth mean gravitational field of everyone else, and individual close encounters are negligible over a Hubble time (this is what the relaxation time in Physical Background quantifies). Yet the cluster mode's whole point is to show two-body relaxation acting within an achievable run length, and Experiment 11 showed you can dial evaporation in or out with the softening length alone. Using what you found in Experiments 1, 7, 11 and 13, write a short (half-page) argument, in your own words and citing your own numbers from this program, for why real star clusters (\(N\sim10^3\)–\(10^6\), many relaxation times over a Hubble time) are collisional systems while real galaxies (\(N\sim10^{10}\)–\(10^{11}\) stars, one relaxation time vastly exceeding the age of the Universe) are, to very good approximation, not.

Domain of Validity

This program is a teaching instrument for tree-code gravity and collisionless stellar dynamics, not a research-grade N-body code. The following are absent by design, and several of them dominate real star clusters and galaxies in the regimes named.

What is absent

  • Individual or block timesteps. One global \(\Delta t\) is used for every body at every step. Production N-body codes give close, fast-moving pairs their own short timestep; this program does not, which is why a fixed-\(\Delta t\) run only marginally resolves the deepest moment of a cold collapse (see Algorithm and Experiment 14).
  • Multipole moments beyond the monopole. Each tree cell is represented purely by its total mass and center of mass. A quadrupole correction (as in most modern tree codes) would improve the tree's accuracy at fixed \(\theta\), at the cost of a noticeably more involved cell-cell interaction.
  • Mass spectrum and mass segregation. Every body carries identical mass. Real star clusters have a range of stellar masses, and two-body relaxation preferentially sinks the heaviest stars to the core (mass segregation) — a major driver of real cluster core evolution that an equal-mass model cannot show.
  • Stellar evolution, collisions, and binary formation. Bodies are point masses that never merge, lose mass, or form bound pairs through dissipative encounters; real cluster cores can produce hard binaries that measurably alter the cluster's energy budget.
  • Gas, hydrodynamics, and star formation. This is a pure collisionless gravity code; there is no gas component; the galaxy-formation mode is only a dark-matter-like or already-formed-stars toy problem, not a model of how gas turns into stars.
  • An external tidal field or host potential. Both cluster and galaxy modes model an isolated system in otherwise empty space; a real cluster orbiting within a galaxy loses stars to the host's tidal field, a process this program does not include.
  • General relativity. All gravity here is Newtonian; nothing in this program is intended to represent compact-object or strong-field physics.

Known model artefacts and caveats

  • The tree method does not conserve momentum exactly. Documented and quantified in Algorithm; use --method direct whenever exact conservation matters more than tree-code speed.
  • The default softening is tuned for the smooth field, not for close encounters. The Athanassoula, Fady, Lambert & Bosma (2000) optimal-softening scaling used as this program's default minimizes the RMS force error against the true (unsoftened) mean field. It does not attempt to preserve the close two-body encounters that drive real relaxation and evaporation, which is why the default cluster run so often shows zero instantaneously unbound bodies AND a flat or non-monotonic near-escape tail and half-mass radius — not merely a subtler signal, but frequently no significant relaxation signal at all within a practical run length at default parameters (see Experiment 11 and the note in Physical Background).
  • The unboundedness criterion uses the instantaneous potential only. identify_unbound() flags a body as unbound when its specific energy (computed from the current configuration of every other body) is strictly positive — the standard practical criterion, and the physically correct one regardless of which way the body happens to be moving at that instant: a positive-energy body on an inward-moving leg of its trajectory is still, correctly, unbound. What this function does not do is confirm the body never returns: it is evaluated snapshot by snapshot rather than by integrating the body's fate forward, so in a system undergoing violent relaxation the count of unbound bodies can rise and fall from one snapshot to the next rather than increasing monotonically, and a body flagged unbound at one snapshot can be re-flagged bound at a later one as the potential itself evolves. Read n_unbound as an instantaneous census, not a cumulative escape count. high_velocity_fraction() is provided as a softer, finer-grained companion diagnostic (a body approaching its local escape speed counts before it formally crosses it) — it is still a count divided by \(N\), so at the body counts this program targets it moves in visible steps of \(1/N\) and, like n_unbound, is not guaranteed to grow monotonically over a run.
  • One global softening length, not a spatially varying one. The optimal-softening scaling is evaluated once from \(N\) and the initial scale radius and then held fixed for the whole run, even as the system's density profile evolves. A softening length that tracked the local density would be more accurate but would also, again, change what physics the model is capturing.
  • The chaos-mode Lyapunov fit is a heuristic, not a formal chaos test. Its accept/reject gates (see Governing Equations) reliably reject several specific families of smooth non-exponential growth: a stretched/compressed exponential across a range of exponents and noise levels, a noisy quadratic, and a noiseless linear or quadratic trend — they are not a hypothesis test with a known, rigorously calibrated false-positive or false-negative rate outside those specific families. In particular, an ordinary logistic (saturating) curve whose automatically-selected fit window happens to clear the minimum-dynamic-range gate by only a moderate margin is NOT reliably rejected: such a curve is still accepted roughly two-thirds of the time, because a logistic is, by construction, asymptotically exponential during its own early growth and no shape statistic computed within a narrow amplitude window can always tell the two apart. A real chaotic run, separately, can and sometimes does fail every gate and report no fit. A reported nan Lyapunov exponent should be read as “this heuristic could not confirm a clean exponential stretch,” not as “this run is not chaotic” — and, symmetrically, a reported number should be read as this heuristic's finite-time estimate for that one realization, not as a certified measurement, particularly for a saturating-type growth curve given the limitation just described.
  • Scalar virial balance at \(t=0\) is not proof of dynamical equilibrium. cluster and galaxy initial conditions are rescaled to exact instantaneous \(2T/|W_{\rm vir}|=1\) (or a requested Q0) for the actual discrete, softened realization, which corrects a global energy-scale mismatch but does not guarantee the realization sits at a stationary point of the collisionless Boltzmann equation. A modest readjustment transient over the first few crossing times can appear for this reason alone; see The Virial Theorem in Physical Background and Experiment 7.
  • The printed “settled into a quasi-equilibrium remnant” verdict for galaxy mode is a specific, numeric, driver-level heuristic, not a claim from the underlying physics. It is computed from a DENSE diagnostic series sampled at every integration step (not from however many snapshots happen to be STORED for plotting/CSV output — that count is set separately by --target_snapshots and does not affect this verdict at all: two runs with bit-for-bit identical integrated trajectories that differ only in --target_snapshots always reach the same verdict). A cold (Qinitial ≤ 0.3) run only earns a “settled” verdict when its final 20% of elapsed time contains at least 5 dense integration-step samples (fewer, and the driver reports the check as inconclusive rather than guessing) AND every one of the following holds within that late window: (1) a GENUINE collapse occurred before the window — the run's global half-mass-radius minimum, over the WHOLE run, must fall strictly before the window starts, strictly after the run's very first sample, and at least 10% below the initial half-mass radius (a series that only ever expands does not qualify merely because its smallest-value-so-far happens to sit at its own first sample); (2) a material rebound — the window's own mean half-mass radius must be at least 1.10× that global minimum, not a flat tail parked at its deepest point; (3) the virial ratio is centered near the Q=1 balance condition, within 0.25, not merely quiet at some other level; (4) a bounded secular trend — the window's best-fit linear drift in half-mass radius, as a fraction of the window-mean radius, stays within 0.20, catching a sustained one-way expansion or contraction that a range check alone can miss; (5) the half-mass radius varies by no more than 30% of its own mean value over the window; and (6) the virial ratio's range over the window is no more than 0.60. All of these are evaluated by elapsed TIME, not by any stored-snapshot count. A run that starts cold, reaches every one of the classic collapse/bounce milestones, but still fails any one of these six is reported plainly as not-yet-settled rather than folded into the classic narrative; running longer and/or finer (raising --n_freefall and/or --steps_per_freefall) is the usual fix for a run that is simply too short, or too coarsely integrated, to tell — raising --target_snapshots alone does nothing for this verdict.

What this program does capture honestly is the logic of the two big ideas it exists to teach: that approximating distant gravity buys you a genuinely different algorithm with genuinely different error properties (not just “the same answer, faster”), and that a collisionless-looking system of a few hundred bodies is not actually collisionless — two-body relaxation and chaotic sensitivity to initial conditions are both real, measurable, and, at this \(N\), fast.
