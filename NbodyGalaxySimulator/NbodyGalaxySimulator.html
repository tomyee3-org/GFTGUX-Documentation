Description

NbodyGalaxySimulator is a standalone gravitational N-body program built around Plummer-softened gravity and a Barnes–Hut octree (Barnes & Hut 1986, Nature 324, 446). It teaches methods that let a simulation move beyond a handful of exactly integrated bodies to hundreds or thousands of mutually interacting ones: force softening, which regularizes the otherwise-singular point-mass force at close range, and hierarchical force approximation, which, typically, for a reasonably well-distributed tree and a nonzero opening angle, reduces the cost of computing every body's force from every other body from \(O(N^2)\) to \(O(N\log N)\) by treating a distant clump of bodies as a single point mass.

Beat 0 · Plummer Softening

The point-mass Newtonian force, \(F \propto 1/r^2\), diverges as two bodies approach each other. Program Multiple handles this by raising an explicit error the instant two bodies coincide, because with an adaptive, accurate integrator a genuine close encounter should simply be resolved in fine timestep detail, not glossed over. That approach does not extend to hundreds of bodies on a fixed timestep: a single close pair would demand an enormously smaller timestep than every other body needs, and Multiple's per-run predictor-corrector accuracy targets do not exist here at all — the leapfrog scheme used by this program uses one fixed timestep for every body, for the whole run.

Softened Newtonian gravity
\[ \vec{a}_i \;=\; G \sum_{j \ne i} \frac{m_j \,(\vec{x}_j - \vec{x}_i)}{\left(|\vec{x}_j-\vec{x}_i|^2 + \epsilon^2\right)^{3/2}} \]

\(\epsilon\) is the (fixed) softening length. \(\epsilon \to 0\) recovers the exact point-mass law used by Multiple.

This has a direct physical reading: a simulation particle here rarely represents one literal star (or one dark-matter particle); it stands in for a much larger aggregate, smeared over some finite size, and \(\epsilon\) is a reasonable proxy for that size. It also has a purely numerical reading: it caps the maximum force two bodies can exert on each other, which keeps a fixed-timestep integrator stable through what would otherwise be arbitrarily close encounters. Both readings matter here, and they pull in the same direction for choosing \(\epsilon\) — but that same suppression of close encounters also suppresses two-body relaxation itself, distorting the process that cluster mode is trying to show.

Compare the outputs of these two runs. The first is nearly unsoftened, while the second uses a published recommended value (Athanassoula et al., 2000).

python main.py --mode cluster --n_bodies 60 --softening_pc  0.0001 --seed 7 --n_relax 50 --outdir ./figures
python main.py --mode cluster --n_bodies 60 --softening_pc  0.2471 --seed 7 --n_relax 50 --outdir ./figures

Energy conservation

In the nearly unsoftened run (\(\epsilon = 0.0001\text{ pc}\)), energy conservation breaks down severely with a total energy drift of 217.23% over the course of the run, the energy being lost in steps as bodies escape. Without softening, close two-body encounters generate extreme gravitational forces that exceed the resolving capacity of the fixed time step (\(\Delta t = 0.011676\text{ Myr}\)).

With default softening, (\(\epsilon = 0.2471\text{ pc}\)), the energy drift drops to 0.155%. Softening bounds the maximum gravitational force during close encounters, preventing spurious numerical acceleration and maintaining integration accuracy over 50 relaxation times (\(64.16\text{ Myr}\)).

Virial Equilibrium & Structural Evolution

The virial theorem is an important relation between the long-term average internal kinetic energy \(\langle T \rangle \) and potential energy \( \langle U \rangle \) of a system of particles bound by conservative forces. For gravitational forces, \( 2 \langle T \rangle = \langle U \rangle .\)

Because of softening, we use \(W_{\rm vir} \equiv \sum_i \vec{r}_i\cdot\vec{F}_i ,\) the the virial (force-weighted) sum rather than the softened potential energy \(U.\) \(W_{\rm vir}\) and \(U\) coincide only in the unsoftened limit \(\epsilon\to 0\).

With \(\epsilon = 0.0001\text{ pc}\), spurious energy computations resulting from encounters approaching distances less than the integration step size push the cluster wildly out of equilibrium, increasing the virial ratio from \(2T/\vert{}W\vert{} = 1.0\) to \(10.19\). The cluster expands dramatically: the half-mass radius grows from \(1.302\text{ pc}\) to \(8.028\text{ pc}\), and the \(90\%\) Lagrangian radius reaches over \(80\text{ pc}\).

With default softening, The cluster remains bound and quasi-static near virial equilibrium throughout the run. (The \(2T/\vert{}W\vert{}\) ratio fluctuates closely around 1.0, ending at 0.8255). The half-mass radius undergoes minimal expansion from \(1.302\text{ pc}\) to \(1.671\text{ pc}\).

Stellar Escapes (Unbound Fraction)

With \(\epsilon = 0.0001\text{ pc}\), 17 out of 60 stars become instantaneously unbound by the end of the simulation. However, because of energy non-conservation issues, many of these "ejections" are likely to be numerical integration errors rather than pure physical two-body relaxation.

With default softening, no bodies become unbound. While softening enforces numerical stability, softening suppresses genuine two-body scattering that would be expected to result in physical escapes in small systems.

Trade-offs

The default softening parameter provides numerical stability and long-term energy conservation, but at the cost of suppressing close physical two-body interactions.

Beat 1 · Stochastic Variance

Consider the outputs of the following two simulation runs, the only difference being between the starting seeds:

python main.py --mode cluster --n_bodies 60 --softening_pc 0.0338 --steps_per_crossing 150 --n_relax 40 --seed 0 --outdir ./figures
python main.py --mode cluster --n_bodies 60 --softening_pc 0.0338 --steps_per_crossing 150 --n_relax 40 --seed 2 --outdir ./figures

Chaotic Dynamics (High Sensitivity to Initial Conditions)

Comparing these two runs, which differ only in the random seed, yields several key conclusions about stochasticity and chaotic dynamics in small star clusters:

Seed 0: The cluster remains fully bound over \(58.69\text{ Myr}\), with the half-mass radius expanding modestly from \(1.424\text{ pc}\) to \(1.638\text{ pc}\) and the \(90\%\) Lagrangian radius reaching \(\approx 7\text{ pc}\).

Seed 2: Two stars (\(3.33\%\)) become instantaneously unbound by the end of the run, pushing the half-mass radius out to \(1.978\text{ pc}\) and the \(90\%\) Lagrangian radius up to \(\approx 11\text{ pc}\).

This is because N-body gravitational systems are fundamentally chaotic (sensitive to initial stellar positions and velocities).

Good Numerical Stability and Convergence

Energy Conservation: Both runs exhibit well-controlled maximum sampled energy drift \( (0.569\%), \) indicating that steps_per_crossing = 150 with \(\epsilon = 0.0338\text{ pc}\) provides robust numerical integration

Virial Equilibrium: Both clusters stay centered around virial equilibrium (\(2T/\vert{}W\vert{} \approx 1.0\)) over \( \approx 1.05\text{ Myr} ,\) despite fluctuations due to the small \(N\).

In the Beat 0 setup with default softening (\(\epsilon = 0.2471\text{ pc}\)), close interactions were overly smoothed out. By reducing softening to \(\epsilon = 0.0338\text{ pc}\) and decreasing the timestep (steps_per_crossing = 150), the simulation can capture physical two-body scattering events leading to bona fide stellar ejections while maintaining integration fidelity.

Key Takeaway

Single N-body realizations of small clusters carry high stochastic variance. To draw statistically valid conclusions about escape rates or core collapse timescales at reduced softening, you should run an ensemble of random seeds and average the results across runs.

Beat 2 · Barnes & Hut

Beats 0 through 8 of Multiple compute every body's acceleration by summing the exact Newtonian contribution from every other body: for \(N\) bodies that is \(N(N-1)/2\) pairwise terms, so the cost grows as \(O(N^2)\). Doubling \(N\) quadruples the work. That is entirely practical for the handful of bodies in a planetary or few-star encounter, which is exactly Multiple's domain in its early beats, but it becomes prohibitive well before \(N\) reaches even a few thousand. A star cluster with \(10^5\)–\(10^6\) stars, or a galaxy with far more, is simply out of reach of direct summation on any one machine.

Barnes & Hut (1986) noticed that a distant clump of bodies barely needs to be resolved individually: to good approximation, its combined gravitational pull can be replaced by a single point mass at its center of mass. Organizing the bodies into a spatial tree (an octree in three dimensions) and applying that approximation wherever it is accurate enough reduces the cost to, typically, \(O(N\log N)\) for a reasonably well-distributed tree and a nonzero opening angle — not a worst-case guarantee, since a degenerate distribution of near-coincident bodies or an opening angle of zero can approach the direct method's \(O(N^2)\) cost instead (this program's own tree explicitly handles that case). This is the central algorithmic idea of this program; its price is an approximate rather than exact force.

Here is how the method works and why it is so effective for large-scale gravitational dynamics:
  1. Spatial Decomposition (Tree Construction)
    Instead of calculating the force between every single pair of bodies, Barnes & Hut groups distant particles together spatially using a hierarchical tree structure:
    • Quadtree (2D) or Octree (3D): The simulation space containing all bodies is enclosed within a root square (or cube) bounding box.
    • Recursive Subdivision: This box is recursively divided into 4 equal quadrants (in 2D) or 8 equal octants (in 3D) until every sub-box contains at most one particle (or is empty).
    • Mass Aggregation: As the tree is built, every internal node (box) calculates and stores the total mass and the center of mass of all particles contained within its sub-tree.
  2. The Opening Criterion (\(\theta\))
    When computing the net gravitational force acting on a specific body, the algorithm traverses the tree from the root node downward, deciding whether a distant group of stars can be treated as a single composite mass or if it needs to be resolved individually:
    • For any given tree node (box), the algorithm evaluates the opening criterion: \[\frac{s}{d} \lt \theta \] where:
      • \(s\) is the physical width (side length) of the box.
      • \(d\) is the distance from the target body to the center of mass of that box.
      • \(\theta\) is the dimensionless opening angle (set to 0.50 in your simulator runs).
  3. Force Evaluation Rules
    During the tree traversal for each body:
    • If the criterion is met (\(s/d < \theta\)): The box is treated as "far enough away." The entire cluster of stars inside that box is approximated as a single point mass located at the box's center of mass, and a single force calculation is performed.
    • If the criterion is NOT met (\(s/d \ge \theta\)): The box is too close, meaning individual substructures within it could introduce significant force errors. The algorithm "opens" the box and recursively evaluates its child sub-boxes.
  4. Key Advantages in Simulations
    • Speedup (\(O(N \log N)\)): For \(N\) particles, direct summation requires evaluating \(N(N-1)/2\) pairs. The tree traversal reduces this to roughly \(N \log N\) operations, making simulations of thousands to millions of stars computationally practical.
    • Tunable Accuracy (theta):
      • A smaller \(\theta\) (e.g., \(0.2\)) forces the tree to open more boxes, increasing accuracy and approaching direct summation at higher computational cost.
      • A larger \(\theta\) (e.g., \(0.8\) or \(1.0\)) speeds up the calculation further by approximating more distant groups, but increases force errors and can introduce artificial momentum drift. The default \(\theta = 0.50\) provides an optimal balance between speed and precision for stellar dynamics.

Because the tree replaces exact per-body sums with a monopole approximation for distant clumps, its force differs from the exact (direct-summation) value by an amount that grows with θ. Beat 3 · Violent relaxation and cold collapse

A self-gravitating system released far from equilibrium — the galaxy mode's cold, uniform sphere is the classic case — does not settle down through the slow, encounter-by-encounter process above. Instead, while the overall mass distribution is still changing rapidly (during the collapse and the violent oscillations that follow it), the gravitational potential itself fluctuates on a dynamical timescale, and those fluctuations exchange energy between bodies far faster than individual two-body encounters could. Lynden-Bell (1967) named this violent relaxation and argued it explains why collisionless self-gravitating systems (galaxies, above all) reach a quasi-equilibrium, centrally concentrated state so much faster than their two-body relaxation time would allow. Direct numerical experiments of exactly this cold-collapse scenario (van Albada 1982, and many since) show the relaxation is generically incomplete in the sense that the remnant is not a smooth, isotropic equilibrium model: it carries an extended, non-Maxwellian halo of loosely bound bodies flung out during the collapse, and a density profile only qualitatively like a real galaxy's. That does not mean its virial ratio must sit visibly below 1 — in this program's own runs the remnant's virial ratio oscillates in a band close to 1 (see the virial theorem note below and Suggested Experiments), consistent with a system that is virialized on average while still relaxing in detail.

Consider the following scenario:

python main.py --mode galaxy --seed 3 --outdir ./figures

The console output and set of figures illustrate a classic galaxy-formation cold collapse (gravitational collapse of an unvirialized, nearly motionless gas/star cloud) simulated using an \(N\)-body Barnes-Hut tree algorithm (\(N = 300\) bodies, total mass \(10^6\text{ M}_\odot\)).

Here is a breakdown of what is happening physically and numerically throughout the run:

  1. Initial State: The Cold Sphere (\(t = 0\text{ Myr}\))
    • Setup: 300 bodies are distributed uniformly across an initial radius of \(200\text{ pc}\) with a virial ratio of \(2T/\vert{}W\vert{} = 0.000\) (completely cold, meaning particles start virtually stationary with no thermal or kinetic support against gravity).
    • Free-Fall Time: The global free-fall time scale for this system is \(t_{\text{ff}} = 46.84\text{ Myr}\), and the simulation runs for \(8 \times t_{\text{ff}}\) (\(374.7\text{ Myr}\) total).
  2. Gravitational Collapse and Deepest Collapse (\(t \approx 55.6\text{ Myr}\))
    • The Crash: Lacking kinetic energy, gravity pulls all particles inward toward the center.
    • Minimum Radius: The half-mass radius plummets dramatically from an initial \(162.6\text{ pc}\) down to a minimum core size of \(18.54\text{ pc}\) at \(t = 55.6\text{ Myr}\) (roughly one free-fall time).
    • Virial Spike: As particles rush through the center and overshoot, kinetic energy spikes violently. The virial ratio reaches a transient peak of \(2T/\vert{}W\vert{} = 5.55\) (deepest collapse panel), indicating a heavily over-energized, outward-bounding system.
  3. Violent Relaxation and Rebound
    • Lynden-Bell's Mechanism: As the fluctuating, rapidly changing gravitational potential rattles the system, stars rapidly exchange energy in a process known as violent relaxation (Lynden-Bell 1967).
    • Oscillation & Remnant Formation: The system rebounds from its central compression, re-expanding to settle into a quasi-equilibrium remnant. By the end of the run (\(374.7\text{ Myr}\)), the half-mass radius stabilizes at around \(169.3\text{ pc}\).
    • Quasi-Equilibrium (\(Q_{\text{final}} \approx 0.95\)): Over the final 20% of the simulation, the virial ratio hovers around a mean value of \(0.913\) (ending at \(0.9525\)), which is close to the virial-balance condition (\(2T/\vert{}W\vert{} = 1\)).
  4. Numerical Health
    • Energy Conservation: The maximum sampled energy drift is tightly constrained to \(1.697\%\) over the entire \(374.7\text{ Myr}\) run, verifying that the Barnes-Hut tree integration (\(\theta = 0.50\)) and Athanassoula optimal softening (\(\epsilon = 44.48\text{ pc}\)) maintained high numerical stability.

Beat 4 · Chaos in gravitating systems

Generic gravitational systems with three or more mutually interacting bodies exhibit chaotic sensitivity to initial conditions: two initial conditions that differ by an arbitrarily small amount typically separate exponentially in phase space, rather than growing only linearly as they would in a well-behaved, non-chaotic dynamical system — though special regular or symmetric configurations (a small number of exact periodic solutions among an infinite space of initial conditions) also exist and are not themselves chaotic. Miller (1964) first demonstrated this numerically for self-gravitating stellar systems; later work (e.g. Goodman, Heggie & Hut 1993) found that for small-\(N\) self-gravitating clusters the associated Lyapunov time — the e-folding time of that exponential growth, where one exists — is typically of order a few crossing times. This program's chaos mode reproduces that generic behavior for its randomly sampled initial conditions.

The following equation defines a heuristic method for estimating the Lyapunov time (\(t_{\text{Lyap}}\)) and quantifying the chaotic divergence of trajectories in N-body stellar and galactic systems.
Heuristic finite-time Lyapunov-exponent fit
\[ \Delta(t) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_i |\vec{x}_{A,i}(t)-\vec{x}_{B,i}(t)|^2} \;\sim\; \Delta_0\, e^{\lambda t} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t_{\rm Lyap} = 1/\lambda \]
  • Two Neighboring Realizations (\(A\) and \(B\)): To compute a finite-time Lyapunov exponent empirically, the simulation must be run twice from slightly different initial conditions (e.g., perturbing a single star's position by a tiny offset \(\Delta_0\), or using two different random seeds/perturbations). Here, \(\vec{x}_{A,i}(t)\) and \(\vec{x}_{B,i}(t)\) represent the position vector of body \(i\) in run \(A\) and run \(B\) at time \(t.\)
  • Mean Root-Square Separation \(\Delta(t)\): The equation measures the root-mean-square (RMS) spatial distance between corresponding bodies across the two runs averaged over all \(N\) bodies.
  • Exponential Divergence (\(e^{\lambda t}\)): In a chaotic dynamical system, small initial perturbations do not remain small; they grow exponentially at a rate governed by the Lyapunov exponent \(\lambda.\)
  • Lyapunov Time (\(t_{\text{Lyap}} = 1/\lambda\)): This represents the characteristic e-folding timescale over which the system loses memory of its initial conditions—effectively marking the horizon beyond which individual particle trajectories become unpredictable.
  • "Heuristic" Caveat: The simulator labels this as a heuristic fit because true Lyapunov exponents require rigorous tangent-space integration (computing shadow trajectories and periodic renormalization) rather than just tracking a single finite separation \(\Delta(t)\), which can saturate once particles wander across the entire spatial extent of the galaxy or cluster.

We will see how this equation works when we analyze the following:

python main.py --mode chaos --seed 1 --perturbation_seed 1 --outdir ./figures
The console output and plots depict a chaos-mode sensitivity test (--mode chaos) using \(N = 40\) bodies in a Plummer sphere configuration, integrated via direct summation (\(\theta = 0.50\)) to ensure high force precision.
  • Initial Perturbation: Two identical system realizations (A and B) are initialized with a microscopic spatial offset of \(\Delta_0 = 2.716 \times 10^{-8}\text{ pc}\) (a relative perturbation of \(10^{-8}\) relative to the core radius).
  • Top-Left Panel (Final Positions): At \(t = 79.1\text{ Myr}\) (\(120\) crossing times), Realization A (blue) and Realization B (coral) have completely diverged spatially. Although they share identical macro-properties, individual particle trajectories are entirely uncorrelated.
  • Top-Right Panel (Divergence & Lyapunov Fit): Displays the root-mean-square (RMS) separation \(\Delta(t)\) on a logarithmic scale over time.
    • The green line highlights the linear exponential growth phase (\(R^2 = 0.99450\), spanning \(16.43\) e-folds across \(145\) snapshots).
    • Beyond \(t \approx 60\text{ Myr}\), the curve saturates and flattens out near \(\sim 2.5\text{ pc}\), which corresponds to the physical size of the cluster itself (at this point, the particles are completely randomized relative to each other).
  • Bottom-Left Panel (Energy Conservation): Both realizations show pristine energy conservation, with a maximum sampled energy drift of just \(0.003\%\), confirming that the divergence is physical (due to chaos) rather than numerical (due to integration errors).

Verification Using the Lyapunov Equation

We can verify the program's reported Lyapunov time (\(t_{\text{Lyap}}\)) and exponent (\(\lambda\)) using the exponential divergence equation: \[\Delta(t) = \Delta_0\, e^{\lambda t} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t_{\text{Lyap}} = \frac{1}{\lambda}\]

  1. Checking the Lyapunov Time

    2. From the console output:
    • Lyapunov exponent (\(\lambda\)): \(0.2994\text{ Myr}^{-1}\)
    • Reported Lyapunov time (\(t_{\text{Lyap}}\)): \(3.34\text{ Myr}\)
    Plugging \(\lambda\) into the inverse relation: \[t_{\text{Lyap}} = \frac{1}{0.2994\text{ Myr}^{-1}} \approx 3.340\text{ Myr}\] This matches the program's reported value exactly. In terms of crossing times (\(t_{\text{cross}} = 0.659\text{ Myr}\)), this equals: \[\frac{3.340\text{ Myr}}{0.659\text{ Myr}} \approx 5.07\,t_{\text{cross}}\]

  2. Checking the E-Folds Scaling

    3. The fit spans \(16.43\) e-folds over the linear growth window. We can check how much the separation grew during this exponential phase:
    • Initial separation (\(\Delta_0\)): \(2.716 \times 10^{-8}\text{ pc}\)
    • Final saturation separation (\(\Delta_{\text{final}}\)): \(\approx 2.382\text{ pc}\)
    Taking the natural logarithm of the total growth factor: \[\ln\left(\frac{\Delta_{\text{final}}}{\Delta_0}\right) = \ln\left(\frac{2.382}{2.716 \times 10^{-8}}\right) = \ln(8.77 \times 10^7) \approx 18.29 \text{ total e-folds}\] The linear fit captures the active exponential growth phase (\(16.43\) e-folds) before the curve levels off into saturation. This confirms that the program's regression fit properly isolates the exponential divergence regime before boundary saturation limits further growth.

Beat 5 · Evaporation

Two-body relaxation and evaporation

A self-gravitating system of discrete bodies is not the same as a smooth mass distribution: individual gravitational encounters between bodies exchange energy and gradually drive the system toward a broader, more relaxed velocity distribution — and a system of point masses in a finite volume has, in principle, no upper limit on relative speed reachable this way. Over many crossing times, enough encounters accumulate that some bodies are kicked above the local escape speed and leave for good: this is evaporation. The characteristic timescale for this diffusive process, the two-body relaxation time, is given below; for an isolated system without a tidal boundary, full evaporation of a Plummer-like cluster is a slow process, of order a hundred or more relaxation times (Ambartsumian 1938; Spitzer 1987).

EXP-1 · introductory

Does a Default Cluster Actually Relax?

Run python main.py --mode cluster --seed 0. Read the Lagrangian radii r10 and r90 at the start and end from the printed summary or the Lagrangian-radii panel, and note n_unbound and the near-escape tail. Two-body relaxation is supposed to drive a self-gravitating system's core to contract while its outskirts expand as stars exchange energy — but at this program's default softening, a short default run often shows only a small, non-monotonic change in both radii (sometimes even the "wrong" direction relative to that textbook story), because the default softening actively suppresses the close encounters that drive relaxation (see Physical Background) and because the initial virial-balance rescale itself produces a brief readjustment transient that is NOT relaxation. Repeat with --seed 1 and --seed 2: is any core-contracts/outskirts-expand pattern consistent across seeds, or is it dominated by run-to-run scatter? Now try Experiment 11's reduced-softening setup and compare — that is where this program's relaxation signal actually becomes reliably visible.

EXP-2 · introductory

A Cold Sphere Collapses

Run python main.py --mode galaxy. From the printed summary, note the initial virial ratio, the virial ratio at the deepest collapse, and the final virial ratio (the summary prints all three). Those three numbers alone can tell you whether the run was monotonic or not (e.g. whether the deepest-collapse value is far from both the initial and final ones) but three points cannot by themselves show a genuine repeated oscillation — predict, from just those three numbers, whether \(2T/|W_{\rm vir}|\) looks like it settled at a single value or is still moving by the end of the run, THEN look at the plotted virial-ratio panel (see Output) to see its actual behavior across every recorded snapshot. Does the plotted curve end at exactly 1, the value corresponding to exact scalar virial balance, or does it keep oscillating in a band around 1 for as long as the run lasts? Read the virial theorem note in Physical Background — including its caveat that scalar balance is not, by itself, proof of a settled dynamical equilibrium — to see why a settled oscillation near 1, not a single converged number, is the expected signature here.

EXP-3 · introductory

How Fast Do Nearby Orbits Diverge?

Run python main.py --mode chaos. If the divergence panel's title reports a fitted Lyapunov time (or the terminal summary shows one), express it as a multiple of the initial crossing time; Miller (1964) and later studies put this ratio at a few crossing times for self-gravitating N-body systems — is your result in that range? Run it again (no --seed) and see whether the estimate is stable from realization to realization. This program's fit is a heuristic, permissive-but-imperfect gate (see Governing Equations), so some runs will legitimately report no fit at all — if that happens, look at the plotted divergence curve directly: does it still show a stretch of roughly straight-line growth on the logarithmic axis, even though the automated check didn't confirm it? Either outcome (a numeric answer, or a documented non-result with a visual read of the curve) is an acceptable result for this exercise.

EXP-4 · introductory

Meet the Two Force Methods

Run the same cluster twice, once with --method tree and once with --method direct, using a fixed --seed so both start identically. Compare final energy, final virial ratio and the Lagrangian radii. They will not be pixel-identical — the tree only approximates the force — but they should tell a consistent physical story. If they disagreed wildly, what would that tell you about \(\theta\)?

EXP-5 · introductory/intermediate · pilot-study caveat

Does Normalizing by the N/ln N Formula Collapse the Curves?

Run --mode cluster with --n_bodies set to 60, 120, 250 and 500, holding --total_mass_msun and --scale_radius_pc fixed at their defaults, fixing --seed 0 at every N, and giving each the same --n_relax. Because this program's physical run length is itself n_relax times the N-dependent NOMINAL relaxation time (see Governing Equations, which scales roughly as \(N/\ln N\)), fixing n_relax already makes each run's physical duration and step count scale by roughly that same \(N/\ln N\) factor — a much longer run for N=500 than for N=60. This means the experiment CANNOT be used to ask whether the raw amount of relaxation grows with N in absolute terms: that comparison is exactly what fixing n_relax normalizes away by construction. What it CAN ask is a different, still useful question: does the amount of core contraction/outskirt expansion you observe come out roughly comparable across all four N values, despite their very different absolute run lengths? If the relaxation-time formula is doing real work capturing how relaxation scales with N, comparable outcomes across N at fixed n_relax is exactly what you would expect; a result that instead varies wildly and systematically with N (after accounting for run-to-run scatter) would suggest the formula is not fully capturing this discrete, softened system's actual behavior. Two things confound a clean reading and should be kept in mind rather than treated as noise: this program's default softening changes with N (\(\epsilon_{\rm opt}\propto N^{-0.26}\)), so the close-encounter physics is not held fixed across your four runs; and a single seed per N cannot separate a genuine N-dependence from that one realization's own sampling scatter (repeat at least one N value with a second seed to get a feel for the scatter's size). Treat this as a qualitative pilot check of whether the formula's normalization is self-consistent, not a clean quantitative verification of the \(N/\ln N\) scaling itself.

EXP-11 · intermediate · uses the Python API

Softening Is Hiding Your Unbound Bodies

Run the default cluster and note n_unbound (instantaneously positive-energy bodies) at the end — it is very often zero. Now rerun with --softening_pc set well below the Athanassoula-et-al.-optimal default, a finer --steps_per_crossing (this is required for stability once softening shrinks), and a longer --n_relax, e.g. python main.py --mode cluster --n_bodies 60 --softening_pc 0.0338 --steps_per_crossing 150 --n_relax 40 --seed 0. Treat this as a starting point to try, not a guaranteed outcome: this is a chaotic calculation, and how many instantaneously-unbound bodies it ends with (out of 60) varies by seed, including seeds that end with none at all — do not expect every seed to show a nonzero count. Run the same command across several seeds and report the FRACTION of seeds that show at least one instantaneously-unbound body, and discuss why that fraction is not 100% even at this lowered softening. For stronger evidence that a flagged body is genuinely escaping (rather than briefly crossing back and forth), you need per-body identity across snapshots, which the CLI/CSV output does not carry — call the Python API directly instead:

import numpy as np
import physics_nbg as phys
r = phys.run_cluster(n_bodies=60, total_mass_msun=1e3, scale_radius_pc=1.0,
                      softening_pc=0.0338, steps_per_crossing=150,
                      n_relax=40, seed=0)
n_snap = len(r["t"])
for k in range(max(n_snap - 5, 0), n_snap):    # the last 5 stored snapshots
    pos, vel, m = r["positions"][k], r["velocities"][k], r["masses"]
    e = phys.specific_energies(pos, vel, m, r["softening"])
    com = phys.center_of_mass(pos, m)
    com_vel = phys.center_of_mass_velocity(vel, m)
    rel_pos = pos - com                        # center-relative position (m)
    rel_vel = vel - com_vel                    # center-relative velocity (m/s):
                                                # dr/dt needs velocity measured
                                                # in the SAME frame as the
                                                # radius, or a bulk cluster
                                                # drift alone (which changes no
                                                # distance between bodies) would
                                                # masquerade as inward/outward
                                                # motion
    radius_m = np.linalg.norm(rel_pos, axis=1)
    with np.errstate(invalid="ignore"):
        v_radial_m_s = np.divide(
            np.einsum("ij,ij->i", rel_pos, rel_vel), radius_m,
            out=np.full_like(radius_m, np.nan), where=radius_m > 0)
    radius_pc = radius_m / phys.PC                        # r/v arrays are SI;
    v_radial_pc_myr = v_radial_m_s * phys.MYR / phys.PC   # convert to match
                                                            # the printed labels
    for i in np.nonzero(e > 0)[0]:
        print(f"snapshot {k}  body {i}  e={e[i]:.3e}  "
              f"r={radius_pc[i]:.4g} pc  v_r={v_radial_pc_myr[i]:.4g} pc/Myr")

and check whether the same body ID reappears across every one of those five snapshots with a growing radius and a positive radial velocity at every snapshot — not merely at the one moment it was first flagged, and not just once or twice (v_r itself need not keep growing; it only needs to stay positive for the radius to keep increasing). A body whose radius keeps increasing and whose v_r stays positive at every one of those snapshots is showing stronger evidence of a persistent escape, not proof of one — five snapshots is still a short window against the evolving potential this Help file describes elsewhere; one that shows up once, or whose radius stalls or turns back over, or whose v_r sign flips, is more likely a body that is only briefly, instantaneously unbound before falling back in. Explain, physically, why the softening length that best approximates the smooth gravitational field of the cluster is not the softening length that best reproduces the close two-body encounters responsible for real evaporation — these are different goals, and this program's default optimizes for the first one.

Does the Relaxation-Time Formula Actually Predict Anything?

Timestep Convergence: Direct Versus Tree

EXP-17 · advanced · multi-seed

Which Remnant Features Survive Changing N and Softening?

Galaxy mode's particles are a scaled-down stand-in for a much larger real system (see run_galaxy()'s docstring and Physical Background), so any apparent feature of the collapsed remnant — a particular final half-mass radius, a specific late-time virial-ratio oscillation amplitude, a visible density concentration — needs to survive a resolution check before you trust it as physics rather than as a numerical artifact of this particular N, softening, or realization. A single run per N is not enough to show that: a shared --seed does not produce matched realizations across different N (the initial-condition sampler draws a different amount of random state at each N), so one run per N confounds genuine resolution trends with ordinary seed-to-seed scatter, exactly the pitfall EXP-13 warns about for cluster mode.

Part A — N convergence. Run --mode galaxy at --n_bodies of 100, 300 (default) and 1000, using several different seeds at EACH N (e.g. 4-5 seeds per N). Do NOT simply leave softening at its default for all three: this program's default softening is itself a function of N (epsilon = 0.98 * scale_radius * N^-0.26, printed in every run's summary as Softening), so leaving it unset changes N and softening together, and any difference you see could be caused by either one. To make this an N-ONLY comparison, run once at N=300 first, read its default softening value off the summary, and then pass that SAME number explicitly via --softening_pc at N=100 and N=1000 as well, so every run in this part shares one fixed physical softening length. (Deliberately varying N and its default softening together instead is a legitimate but DIFFERENT experiment — a joint N/epsilon refinement path, not an N-only resolution test — and should be labeled as such if you choose to run it.) For each N, collect the final half-mass radius and the late-time virial-ratio range across your seeds, and report each group's spread as its RANGE (maximum minus minimum across your 4-5 seeds at that N) — a specific, easy-to-compute number, not an unspecified "spread." Also report each group's "typical value" as its MEDIAN across those same seeds at that N (not the mean, which one outlier seed can shift more than a sample this small should tolerate) — every "typical value" mentioned in this exercise means the median specifically. Comparing detection to convergence is the whole point of this exercise, so keep the two questions separate: the two N's being compared each have their OWN within-N range, and these two ranges can differ, so use one deterministic rule rather than picking whichever range you like — an ABSOLUTE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN MEDIANS FROM ONE N TO THE NEXT that is clearly LARGER than BOTH within-N ranges (equivalently, larger than the MAXIMUM of the two) is real evidence that this feature genuinely DEPENDS on resolution at these N — report it, and note that it is evidence AGAINST having converged, not for it (unless you can also show that change shrinking further as N grows, which this exercise's three N values alone are too few to establish). When the between-N change instead stays comparable to (not clearly larger than) the larger of the two within-N ranges, that does NOT prove the feature has converged — four or five seeds only estimate the true scatter roughly, and a real but moderate resolution dependence can easily hide inside that much noise. Call that outcome INCONCLUSIVE, or "no N-dependence detected at this resolution and seed count," rather than "survives" or "behaves as converged."

Part B — softening sensitivity. Separately, holding N and the scale radius fixed at their defaults, repeat the same multi-seed comparison at the Athanassoula-et-al.-optimal softening for N=300 (printed in the summary) and at roughly half and roughly double that same numeric value, again passed explicitly via --softening_pc at all three settings. Report each group's spread and typical value the same way as Part A (the range across seeds at that softening, and the median across those same seeds). Apply the same logic as Part A, not its mirror image, and the same deterministic rule: an absolute difference between medians from one softening setting to the next that is clearly larger than BOTH within-softening seed-to-seed ranges (equivalently, larger than their maximum) demonstrates real softening SENSITIVITY (evidence against this feature being robust to your softening choice) — report it. A between-softening change that stays comparable to the larger of the two within-softening ranges is, again, INCONCLUSIVE at this seed count, not proof of robustness. Keep this axis separate from Part A — do not change N and softening in the same run, or you will not know which one caused any difference you see.

Across both parts, resist reading "I didn't detect a difference" as "there is no difference": with only 4-5 seeds per setting, a range-based comparison has real power to catch a LARGE dependence and comparatively little power to rule out a moderate one. A demonstrated, clearly-larger-than-range change is trustworthy evidence of a real dependence on N, softening, or realization, and should be read as not-yet-converged (or genuinely softening-sensitive) physics rather than as noise. An inconclusive comparison is exactly that — inconclusive, at most weak support for treating a feature as resolution-independent, not a demonstration of convergence or robustness; strengthening it takes more seeds, more N/softening values, or a trend of shrinking differences toward a stable limit, not a bigger claim from the same four or five runs. This is the same kind of resolution question Experiment 5 asks for cluster mode, applied to the collapse remnant instead of the relaxation rate.

EXP-18 · synthesis

Where Does “Collisionless” Stop Being True?

Galaxy-formation theory usually treats a galaxy as a collisionless system: stars feel the smooth mean gravitational field of everyone else, and individual close encounters are negligible over a Hubble time (this is what the relaxation time in Physical Background quantifies). Yet the cluster mode's whole point is to show two-body relaxation acting within an achievable run length, and Experiment 11 showed you can dial evaporation in or out with the softening length alone. Using what you found in Experiments 1, 7, 11 and 13, write a short (half-page) argument, in your own words and citing your own numbers from this program, for why real star clusters (\(N\sim10^3\)–\(10^6\), many relaxation times over a Hubble time) are collisional systems while real galaxies (\(N\sim10^{10}\)–\(10^{11}\) stars, one relaxation time vastly exceeding the age of the Universe) are, to very good approximation, not.

Domain of Validity

This is a pure-Python equal-mass, softened Newtonian particle model for teaching; there is no gas, radiation, external tide, cosmological expansion, individual timestep or tree quadrupole term. A cold-collapse classifier's positive result means a finite final window met its six numerical gates (including a rebound and a near-unity mean virial ratio); it does not prove full phase-space equilibrium. --target_snapshots changes stored output, not the dense per-integration-step settling check.

The finite-time chaos-fit significance gate was calibrated in this program's tested regime; do not extrapolate it to a certified asymptotic exponent. A saturating, logistic-like separation curve can still pass a selected-window growth fit under contrived conditions; inspect the entire curve, including its saturation. Extremely unphysical mixtures differing by hundreds of orders of magnitude can escape the numerical safeguards; ordinary solar-mass and parsec-scale experiments stay inside the intended regime. Default cluster softening inhibits close encounters, so it cannot by itself establish a physical evaporation timescale.
