Description

NbodyGalaxySimulator is a standalone gravitational N-body program built around Plummer-softened gravity and a Barnes–Hut octree (Barnes & Hut 1986, Nature 324, 446). It teaches methods that let a simulation move beyond a handful of exactly integrated bodies to hundreds or thousands of mutually interacting ones: force softening, which regularizes the otherwise-singular point-mass force at close range, and hierarchical force approximation, which, typically, for a reasonably well-distributed tree and a nonzero opening angle, reduces the cost of computing every body's force from every other body from \(O(N^2)\) to \(O(N\log N)\) by treating a distant clump of bodies as a single point mass.

This has no effect on the program itself, which needs no internet access to run.

Setup

Use Python 3.10 or later with NumPy and Matplotlib. Run from the directory containing main.py. The program assumes you have already tried the project’s Multiple program. Each command displays a Matplotlib figure and saves a timestamped PNG and provenance sidecar in ./figures. Running python main.py --help provides basic information about the input parameters.

Reading the Diagnostics

Each dot is a simulation particle. Positions are evolved in three dimensions; the position panels show an x-y projection. A Plummer sphere is a centrally concentrated spherical cluster model, not a uniform ball. Its scale radius a is not its outer boundary or its half-mass radius.

Lagrangian Radius

The Lagrangian radius r_f is the three-dimensional radius about the center of mass enclosing fraction f of the total mass. Thus r50 is the half-mass radius; r10 and r90 describe the inner and outer mass distribution. These are recalculated at every snapshot: a curve does not follow the same named group of stars forever. Escaping particles remain included, so an outer radius can grow while the inner system stays compact.

Unbound Count

The unbound count uses positive instantaneous specific energy in the center-of-mass frame. It is not a cumulative count of permanent escapes. The near-escape fraction counts still-bound bodies moving at 90–100% of their local escape speed. Both can rise or fall. In the cluster figure the orange curve uses the left count axis; the dashed blue curve uses the right percentage axis.

Virial Equilibrium & Structural Evolution

Let T be kinetic energy measured relative to the system’s center-of-mass velocity. For an isolated system whose overall size remains statistically steady, the virial theorem gives 2\(\langle T \rangle\) + \( \langle W_{vir} \rangle \) = 0. Here \(W_{vir} = \sum_i r_i · F_i\) is negative for attractive gravity, and \(r_i\) is measured from the center of mass. Thus the plotted ratio \(Q = 2T/|W_{vir}|\) fluctuates around 1 in a virially balanced system. For unsoftened gravity \(W_{vir}\) equals the potential energy U; with softening, these are different quantities.

\(Q\) is an energy-balance diagnostic, not a direct measurement of inward or outward motion, and one value near 1 does not establish equilibrium.

Display Note

Position panels can use different axis limits, and final panels can clip distant outliers to keep the bulk visible. A particle disappearing off a panel is not deleted from the run.

Integration Method

The program uses a fixed global kick-drift-kick leapfrog step: update velocities for half a step, positions for a full step, then velocities for the remaining half-step. More steps per crossing/free-fall time mean a smaller timestep. Smaller timesteps reduce integration error; smaller theta reduces tree-force error. Neither change removes errors caused by the chosen particle number or softening.

Useful definitions

The timescales printed in the run summary are calculated from the initial configuration.

t_crossCrossing time (a characteristic dynamical time): \(\sqrt{r_h^3 / (G M)}\), where \(r_h = r50\) and \(M\) = total mass.
t_ffFree-fall time (the ideal cold uniform-sphere collapse timescale): \(\sqrt{3 \pi / (32 G \rho_{\rm mean}) }\), where \( \rho_{\rm mean} = 3M/(4 \pi R^3) \)
t_relax Relaxation time: \( \approx [N /(8 \ln{N})] t_{\rm cross},\) estimates the cumulative effect of many gravitational encounters. This last formula is nominal and unsoftened. Setting --n_relax 40 requests 40 of these nominal relaxation times; it does not prove that the softened cluster has actually undergone that much relaxation.

Beat 0 · Plummer Softening

The point-mass Newtonian force, \(F \propto 1/r^2\), diverges as two bodies approach each other. Program Multiple handles this by raising an explicit error the instant two bodies coincide, because with an adaptive, accurate integrator a genuine close encounter should simply be resolved in fine timestep detail, not glossed over. That approach does not extend to hundreds of bodies on a fixed timestep: a single close pair would demand an enormously smaller timestep than every other body needs, and Multiple's per-run predictor-corrector accuracy targets do not exist here at all — the leapfrog scheme used by this program uses one fixed timestep for every body, for the whole run.

Softened Newtonian gravity
\[ \vec{a}_i \;=\; G \sum_{j \ne i} \frac{m_j \,(\vec{x}_j - \vec{x}_i)}{\left(|\vec{x}_j-\vec{x}_i|^2 + \epsilon^2\right)^{3/2}} \]

\(\epsilon\) is the (fixed) softening length. \(\epsilon \to 0\) recovers the exact point-mass law used by Multiple.

This has a direct physical reading: a simulation particle here rarely represents one literal star (or one dark-matter particle); it stands in for a much larger aggregate, smeared over some finite size, and \(\epsilon\) is a reasonable proxy for that size. It also has a purely numerical reading: it caps the maximum force two bodies can exert on each other, which keeps a fixed-timestep integrator stable through what would otherwise be arbitrarily close encounters. Both readings matter here, and they pull in the same direction for choosing \(\epsilon\) — but that same suppression of close encounters also suppresses two-body relaxation itself, distorting the process that cluster mode is trying to show.

In the context of N-body simulations of collisionless stellar systems (such as star clusters and galaxies), Athanassoula, Fady, Lambert & Bosma (2000) provided an empirically derived Plummer softening length recommendation intended to minimize force errors and artificial relaxation caused by the discrete representation of a smooth mass distribution, where \(a\) is the Plummer scale radius and \(N\) is the number of particles used in the simulation:
Athanassoula et al. (2000) recommended softening
\[ \epsilon_{\rm opt} = 0.98\, a\, N^{-0.26} \]

Note: The Athanassoula et al. recommended parameter should not be considered a universal optimum for collisional clusters or the uniform-sphere model.

Unsoftened versus Softened Comparison

Compare the outputs of these two runs. The first is nearly unsoftened. If, for the second run, we simply omitted the --softening_pc parameter, the program would automatically compute the recommended parameter using the formula above, but we have instead included the rounded value 0.3380 pc explicitly:

python main.py --mode cluster --n_bodies 60 --softening_pc  0.3380 --seed 7 --n_relax 50 --outdir ./figures

The Linux run reproduced in the Sample Outputs Guide gave a maximum sampled energy error of 278.212%, a final Q of 10.5780, a final half-mass radius of 8.064 pc, and 12 of 60 instantaneously unbound bodies. Both the Windows and Linux recordings of the nearly unsoftened run fail energy conservation badly; their endpoints are not reliable predictions of physical escape.
  • Because of the sensitivity of the simulations to the particulars of your runtime environment, it is possible that the details of your run outputs may differ from the results described below. (See Beats 1 and 4 on Stochastic Variance and Chaos.) The major conclusions that you derive from this experiment should, however, be mostly unchanged.
  • The nearly unsoftened run fails its energy-conservation check: unresolved close encounters introduce large numerical energy errors, with a total energy drift of 217.23% over the course of the run. Close encounters whose duration is too short to be resolved by the fixed timestep produce inaccurate trajectories. On the other hand, Athanassoula recommended softening (\(\epsilon = 0.3380\text{ pc}\)) smooths the potential and makes close encounters easier to resolve with a fixed timestep. As a result, the energy drift drops to 0.237%. In these examples, 217.23% and 0.237% are the maximum sampled absolute fractional energy changes. Because the initial energy is negative, a negative plotted fractional change means that the energy has increased. The quoted maxima are not necessarily the final values.
  • The continuous time-series plots reveal that because of small \(N\) stochasticity, the instantaneous metrics of the softened run may still experience pronounced fluctuations. However, the softened example shows much less structural evolution than the nearly unsoftened example.
  • With \(\epsilon = 0.0001\text{ pc}\), numerical errors from close encounters that are too rapid for the fixed timestep push the cluster far from its initial state, increasing the virial ratio from \(2T/\vert{}W\vert{} = 1.0\) to \(10.19\). The cluster expands dramatically: the half-mass radius grows from \(1.302\text{ pc}\) to \(8.028\text{ pc}\), and the \(90\%\) Lagrangian radius reaches over \(80\text{ pc}\). Softening makes close encounters easier to resolve, but accuracy still depends on the timestep and force approximation.
  • With \(\epsilon = 0.0001\text{ pc}\), 17 out of 60 stars become instantaneously unbound by the end of the simulation. However, because of energy non-conservation issues, many of these "ejections" are likely to be numerical integration errors rather than pure physical two-body relaxation. With recommended softening, no bodies become unbound. Softening suppresses two-body scattering that would be expected to result in physical escapes in small systems.

Trade-offs

Athanassoula recommended softening makes close encounters easier to resolve, but accuracy depends on the timestep and force approximation. It also suppresses close encounters that contribute to physical relaxation.

Key Takeaway

Single N-body realizations of small clusters carry high stochastic variance. To draw statistically valid conclusions about escape rates or core collapse timescales, you should run an ensemble of random seeds and average the results across runs.

Beat 2 · Barnes & Hut

The early beats of Multiple compute every body's acceleration by summing the exact Newtonian contribution from every other body: for \(N\) bodies that is \(N(N-1)/2\) pairwise terms, so the cost grows as \(O(N^2)\). Doubling \(N\) quadruples the work. That is entirely practical for the handful of bodies in a planetary or few-star encounter, which is exactly Multiple's domain in its early beats, but it becomes prohibitive well before \(N\) reaches even a few thousand. A star cluster with \(10^5\)–\(10^6\) stars, or a galaxy with far more, is impractical for this teaching implementation.

Barnes & Hut (1986) noticed that a distant clump of bodies barely needs to be resolved individually: to good approximation, its combined gravitational pull can be replaced by a single point mass at its center of mass. Organizing the bodies into a spatial tree (an octree in three dimensions) and applying that approximation wherever it is accurate enough reduces the cost to, typically, \(O(N\log N)\) for a reasonably well-distributed tree and a nonzero opening angle — not a worst-case guarantee, since a degenerate distribution of near-coincident bodies or an opening angle of zero can approach the direct method's \(O(N^2)\) cost instead (this program's own tree explicitly handles that case). This is the central algorithmic idea of this program; its price is an approximate rather than exact force.

Here is how the method works and why it is so effective for large-scale gravitational dynamics:
  1. Spatial Decomposition (Tree Construction)
    Instead of calculating the force between every single pair of bodies, Barnes & Hut groups distant particles together spatially using a hierarchical tree structure:
    • Octree: The simulation space containing all bodies is enclosed within a root cubic bounding box.
    • Recursive Subdivision: This box is recursively divided into 8 equal octants until every sub-box contains at most one particle (or is empty).
    • Mass Aggregation: As the tree is built, every internal node (box) calculates and stores the total mass and the center of mass of all particles contained within its sub-tree.
  2. The Opening Criterion (\(\theta\))
    When computing the net gravitational force acting on a specific body, the algorithm traverses the tree from the root node downward, deciding whether a distant group of stars can be treated as a single composite mass or if it needs to be resolved individually:
    • For any given tree node (box), the algorithm evaluates the opening criterion: \[\frac{s}{d} \lt \theta \] where:
      • \(s\) is the physical width (side length) of the box.
      • \(d\) is the distance from the target body to the center of mass of that box.
      • \(\theta\) is the dimensionless opening angle (set to 0.50 in your simulator runs).
  3. Force Evaluation Rules
    During the tree traversal for each body:
    • If the criterion is met (\(s/d \lt \theta\)): The box is treated as "far enough away." The entire cluster of stars inside that box is approximated as a single point mass located at the box's center of mass, and a single force calculation is performed.
    • If the criterion is NOT met (\(s/d \ge \theta\)): The box is too close, meaning individual substructures within it could introduce significant force errors. The algorithm "opens" the box and recursively evaluates its child sub-boxes. A node containing the target particle is always opened, so the particle cannot attract itself. At the depth limit, inseparable particles are kept together and handled without endlessly subdividing the same region.
  4. Key Advantages in Simulations
    • Speedup (\(O(N \log N)\)): For \(N\) particles, direct summation requires evaluating \(N(N-1)/2\) pairs. The tree traversal reduces this to roughly \(N \log N\) operations, enabling much larger simulations in optimized codes; this teaching implementation caps \(N\) at 5,000.
    • Tunable Accuracy (theta):
      • A smaller \(\theta\) (e.g., \(0.2\)) forces the tree to open more boxes, increasing accuracy and approaching direct summation at higher computational cost.
      • A larger \(\theta\) (e.g., \(0.8\) or \(1.0\)) speeds up the calculation further by approximating more distant groups, but increases force errors and can introduce artificial momentum drift. The default \(\theta = 0.50\) provides a practical balance between speed and precision for stellar dynamics.
    • Caveat:Fewer force operations do not guarantee a shorter run in this Python implementation. Direct summation uses vectorized NumPy operations, while the tree walks Python objects. Measure their timing rather than assuming the tree wins at the particle counts available here.

Beat 3 · Violent relaxation and cold collapse

A self-gravitating system released far from equilibrium — the galaxy mode's cold, uniform sphere is the classic case — does not settle down through the slow, encounter-by-encounter process above. Instead, while the overall mass distribution is still changing rapidly (during the collapse and the violent oscillations that follow it), the gravitational potential itself fluctuates on a dynamical timescale, and those fluctuations exchange energy between bodies far faster than individual two-body encounters could. Lynden-Bell (1967) named this violent relaxation and argued it explains why collisionless self-gravitating systems (galaxies, above all) reach a quasi-equilibrium, centrally concentrated state so much faster than their two-body relaxation time would allow. Direct numerical experiments of exactly this cold-collapse scenario (van Albada 1982, and many since) show the relaxation is generically incomplete in the sense that the remnant is not a smooth, isotropic (i.e. the same in every direction) equilibrium model: The collapse can leave a concentrated component and an extended halo of loosely bound bodies. The velocity distribution need not follow the thermal distribution familiar from a gas.

That does not mean its virial ratio must sit visibly below 1 — in this program's own runs the remnant's virial ratio oscillates in a band close to 1 (see Reading the diagnostics and Suggested Experiments), consistent with a system that is virialized on average while still relaxing in detail.

Consider the following scenario:

python main.py --mode galaxy --seed 3 --outdir ./figures --csvdir ./data

The console output, the set of figures, and the CSV output illustrate a classic galaxy-formation cold collapse (gravitational collapse of a cloud of initially stationary gravitating particles) simulated using an \(N\)-body Barnes-Hut tree algorithm (\(N = 300\) bodies, total mass \(10^6\text{ M}_\odot\)).

Beat 4 · Chaos in gravitating systems

Generic gravitational systems with three or more mutually interacting bodies exhibit chaotic sensitivity to initial conditions: two initial conditions that differ by an arbitrarily small amount typically separate exponentially in positions and velocities, rather than growing only linearly as they would in a well-behaved, non-chaotic dynamical system — though special regular or symmetric configurations (a small number of exact periodic solutions among an infinite space of initial conditions) also exist and are not themselves chaotic. Miller (1964) first demonstrated this numerically for self-gravitating stellar systems.

Later work (e.g. Goodman, Heggie & Hut 1993) found that, when every close interaction is included, errors grow with an e-folding time of roughly a tenth of a conventional crossing time; softening slows that growth. This program's default softening is a sizeable fraction of the cluster's half-mass radius, so its Lyapunov times are longer, typically a few of the program's crossing times. This program's chaos mode reproduces that generic behavior for its randomly sampled initial conditions.

The following equation defines a heuristic method for estimating the Lyapunov time (\(t_{\text{Lyap}}\)) and quantifying the chaotic divergence of trajectories in N-body stellar and galactic systems.
Heuristic finite-time Lyapunov-exponent fit
\[ \Delta(t) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_i |\vec{x}_{A,i}(t)-\vec{x}_{B,i}(t)|^2} \;\sim\; \Delta_0\, e^{\lambda t} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t_{\rm Lyap} = 1/\lambda \]
  • Two Neighboring Realizations (\(A\) and \(B\)): To compute a finite-time Lyapunov exponent empirically, the program compares results of integrating the initial realization versus integrating a copy of the initial realization where every position has been perturbed by a minute amount given by relative_perturbation, leaving the velocities unchanged. The program's position_space_divergence function calculates the separation between periodic snapshots of the two evolving realizations after first removing any coherent center-of-mass translation between the two realizations.
  • Root-mean-square separation: The plotted \(\Delta(t)\) is the RMS distance between corresponding bodies, after measuring each realization's positions relative to its own center of mass. It is reported in parsecs. The parameter --relative_perturbation specifies the initial RMS displacement as a fraction of the original cluster's RMS radius.
  • Exponential Divergence (\(e^{\lambda t}\)): In a chaotic dynamical system, small initial perturbations do not remain small; they grow exponentially at a rate governed by the Lyapunov exponent \(\lambda.\)
  • Lyapunov Time (\(t_{\text{Lyap}} = 1/\lambda\)): This represents the characteristic e-folding timescale. During an exponential-growth interval, the separation increases by a factor of e in one Lyapunov time. How long a prediction remains useful also depends on its initial uncertainty and required accuracy. Separation saturation does not demonstrate completely randomized particles, entirely uncorrelated trajectories, nor identical later macroscopic properties.
  • Why this is an estimate: The program measures the growth rate over a selected interval where the separation curve is roughly straight on the logarithmic plot. Once the separation becomes comparable to the cluster's size, the curve can flatten. The reported Lyapunov time describes the selected growth interval; it does not describe every stage of the later evolution.

  • Top-Left Panel (Final Positions): At \(t = 79.1\text{ Myr}\) (\(120\) crossing times), Realization A (blue) and Realization B (coral) differ visibly. This does not by itself measure how strongly their trajectories remain correlated.

Verification Using the Lyapunov Equation

We can verify the program's reported Lyapunov time (\(t_{\text{Lyap}}\)) and exponent (\(\lambda\)) using the exponential divergence equation: \[\Delta(t) = \Delta_0\, e^{\lambda t} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t_{\text{Lyap}} = \frac{1}{\lambda}\]
  1. Checking the Lyapunov Time
    2. From the console output:
    • Lyapunov exponent (\(\lambda\)): \(0.2994\text{ Myr}^{-1}\)
    • Reported Lyapunov time (\(t_{\text{Lyap}}\)): \(3.34\text{ Myr}\)
    Plugging \(\lambda\) into the inverse relation: \[t_{\text{Lyap}} = \frac{1}{0.2994\text{ Myr}^{-1}} \approx 3.340\text{ Myr}\] This matches the program's reported value to the displayed precision. In terms of crossing times (\(t_{\text{cross}} = 0.659\text{ Myr}\)), we can write: \[t_{\rm Lyap}\approx 3.34\,{\rm Myr} \approx 5.07\,t_{\rm cross}.\]
  2. Checking the E-Folds Scaling
    3. The selected fit covers \(16.43\) e-folds. For comparison, calculate the total initial-to-final growth, including the part outside the selected interval:
    • Initial separation (\(\Delta_0\)): \(2.716 \times 10^{-8}\text{ pc}\)
    • Final saturation separation (\(\Delta_{\text{final}}\)): \(\approx 2.382\text{ pc}\)
    Taking the natural logarithm of the total growth factor: \[\ln\left(\frac{\Delta_{\text{final}}}{\Delta_0}\right) = \ln\left(\frac{2.382}{2.716 \times 10^{-8}}\right) = \ln(8.77 \times 10^7) \approx 18.29 \text{ total e-folds}\] The initial-to-final growth corresponds to about 18.29 e-folds. The selected fit window covers 16.43 e-folds. These describe different intervals; their comparison does not validate the window selection.

Beat 5 · Evaporation

Two-body relaxation and evaporation

A self-gravitating system of discrete bodies is not the same as a smooth mass distribution: individual gravitational encounters between bodies exchange energy and gradually drive the system toward a broader, more relaxed velocity distribution — and a system of point masses in a finite volume has, in principle, no upper limit on relative speed reachable this way. Over many crossing times, enough encounters accumulate that some bodies are kicked above the local escape speed and leave for good: this is evaporation. The characteristic timescale for this diffusive process, the two-body relaxation time, is defined in Reading the Diagnostics; for an isolated system without a tidal boundary, full evaporation of a Plummer-like cluster is a slow process, on the order of a hundred or more relaxation times (Ambartsumian 1938; Spitzer 1987).

Depending on the number of logical processors available on your personal computer (Windows users may check the Task Manager on the performance tab), run four or more instances of the following code in separate command windows, choosing a different --seed value for each instance:

python main.py --mode cluster --n_bodies 60 --softening_pc 0.3380 --steps_per_crossing 200 ^
--n_relax 100 --seed SEED --outdir ./figures --csvdir ./data
(macOS and Linux users: Replace ^ with the \ line continuation symbol)

Likewise, run four or more instances of the following code in separate command windows, choosing a different --seed value for each instance:

python main.py --mode cluster --n_bodies 60 --softening_pc 0.0338 --steps_per_crossing 2000 ^
--n_relax 100 --seed SEED --outdir ./figures --csvdir ./data
The second set requests about ten times as many integration steps as the first. One independently tested run took about 71 minutes; your hardware and competing jobs will affect the time. Try one run before launching several, and avoid starting more simultaneous runs than your computer can comfortably handle. There is no progress output during integration. Optionally, you may add --no_plot to avoid leaving several windows open; using this option, no figure is drawn or saved; but the CSV still records the run summary and sampled diagnostics.

The second set of commands uses a softening distance 0.1 times that of the first set of commands, which had explicitly input the Athanassoula et al. recommended value rather than allowed the program to compute it automatically; to maintain integration accuracy, the timesteps for this second set of commands are correspondingly decreased by a factor of 10.

The output CSV files document the input parameters (both explicitly entered and assumed defaults), the console output, and detailed snapshot values of the relevant calculated values that were graphed in the output PNG files.

As mentioned in Beat 0, with recommended softening, it is likely that you will witness no evaporating particles in the first set of runs since the default softening tends to suppress the genuine two-body scattering that drives relaxation and that would be expected to result in physical escapes in small systems. Indeed, as you discovered in EXP-1, softening can cause the direction of change of the Lagrangian radii to go in entirely the "wrong" direction relative to the textbook expectation that "the core-contracts while the outskirts expand", especially in short runs.

EXP-1 · introductory

Does a Default Cluster Actually Relax?

Run python main.py --mode cluster --seed 0 --csvdir ./data. Read the Lagrangian radii r10 and r90 from the first and last rows of the CSV file, and note n_unbound and the near-escape tail. Two-body relaxation is supposed to drive a self-gravitating system's core to contract while its outskirts expand as stars exchange energy — but at this program's default softening, a short default run often shows only a small, non-monotonic change in both radii (sometimes even the "wrong" direction relative to that textbook story), because the default softening actively suppresses the close encounters that drive relaxation (see Beat 0) and because the initial virial-balance rescale itself produces a brief readjustment transient that is NOT relaxation.

A Cold Sphere Collapses

EXP-5 · introductory/intermediate · pilot-study caveat

Does Normalizing by the N/ln N Formula Collapse the Curves?

Run --mode cluster with --n_bodies set to 60, 120, 250 and 500, holding --total_mass_msun and --scale_radius_pc fixed at their defaults, fixing --seed 0 at every N, and giving each the same --n_relax. Because this program's physical run length is itself n_relax times the N-dependent NOMINAL relaxation time (see timescale definitions, which scales roughly as \(N/\ln N\)), fixing n_relax already makes each run's physical duration and step count scale by roughly that same \(N/\ln N\) factor — a much longer run for N=500 than for N=60. This means the experiment CANNOT be used to ask whether the raw amount of relaxation grows with N in absolute terms: that comparison is exactly what fixing n_relax normalizes away by construction. What it CAN ask is a different, still useful question: does the amount of core contraction/outskirt expansion you observe come out roughly comparable across all four N values, despite their very different absolute run lengths?


If the relaxation-time formula is doing real work capturing how relaxation scales with N, comparable outcomes across N at fixed n_relax is exactly what you would expect; a result that instead varies wildly and systematically with N (after accounting for run-to-run scatter) would suggest the formula is not fully capturing this discrete, softened system's actual behavior. Two things confound a clean reading and should be kept in mind rather than treated as noise: this program's default softening changes with N (\(\epsilon_{\rm opt}\propto N^{-0.26}\)), so the close-encounter physics is not held fixed across your four runs; and a single seed per N cannot separate a genuine N-dependence from that one realization's own sampling scatter (repeat at least one N value with a second seed to get a feel for the scatter's size). Treat this as a qualitative pilot check of whether the formula's normalization is self-consistent, not a clean quantitative verification of the \(N/\ln N\) scaling itself.

EXP-7 · intermediate

Cold, Warm and Hot Collapse

Run --mode galaxy --csvdir ./data using a constant value of --seed and with --virial_ratio_init set to 0.0 (the cold default), 1.0 (started in exact instantaneous scalar virial balance, in this program's \(2T/|W_{\rm vir}|\) convention), 2.0, and 3.0 (strongly supervirial). Compare the four: does the 1.0 run collapse at all, or does it show only a modest early readjustment before settling? Does the 3.0 run's half-mass radius ever come back down, or does it generally keep expanding in this setup?


For each of the four runs, read energy_J in the first CSV data row (t_Myr = 0). Is it negative or positive? This tests whether the initial system is energetically bound as a whole; it does not count the particles that will eventually escape.

  • Negative energy_J: the isolated system cannot disperse entirely to infinity without gaining energy. Individual particles can still escape.
  • Positive energy_J: the system is not energetically bound as a whole. This does not imply that every particle escapes; a bound remnant may remain.
  • Zero energy_J: the marginal case.

The potential has its zero at infinite separation. The initial row answers what the chosen initial state was; the energy-error diagnostic checks how well the numerical evolution preserves its energy.


As an optional follow-up, repeat the \(Q_0 = 0\) run with --softening_pc 10 --steps_per_freefall 640 --method direct and compare the peak \(Q.\)

Note that \(Q_0 = 1\) being in scalar virial balance at \(t=0\) is a global energy-scale statement, not a guarantee of zero evolution (see Reading the diagnostics) — a small amount of early readjustment in the 1.0 run is expected and is not itself evidence of two-body relaxation.

Without softening, \(Q_0 = 2\) would give exactly zero total energy. Read energy_J for your \(Q_0 = 2\) run, and compare potential_J with virial_work_J in the first row. Why is the softened system still bound?

EXP-11 · intermediate · uses the Python API

Softening Is Hiding Your Unbound Bodies

Run the default cluster and note n_unbound (instantaneously positive-energy bodies) at the end — it is very often zero. Now rerun with --softening_pc set well below the Athanassoula-et-al.-optimal default, a finer --steps_per_crossing (this is required for stability once softening shrinks), and a longer --n_relax, e.g. python main.py --mode cluster --n_bodies 60 --softening_pc 0.0338 --steps_per_crossing 150 --n_relax 40 --seed 0. Treat this as a starting point to try, not a guaranteed outcome: this is a chaotic calculation, and how many instantaneously-unbound bodies it ends with (out of 60) varies by seed, including seeds that end with none at all — do not expect every seed to show a nonzero count. Run the same command across several seeds and report the FRACTION of seeds that show at least one instantaneously-unbound body, and discuss why that fraction is not 100% even at this lowered softening. For stronger evidence that a flagged body is genuinely escaping (rather than briefly crossing back and forth), you need per-body identity across snapshots, which the CLI/CSV output does not carry — call the Python API directly instead:

import numpy as np
import physics_nbg as phys
r = phys.run_cluster(n_bodies=60, total_mass_msun=1e3, scale_radius_pc=1.0,
                      softening_pc=0.0338, steps_per_crossing=150,
                      n_relax=40, seed=0)
n_snap = len(r["t"])
for k in range(max(n_snap - 5, 0), n_snap):    # the last 5 stored snapshots
    pos, vel, m = r["positions"][k], r["velocities"][k], r["masses"]
    e = phys.specific_energies(pos, vel, m, r["softening"])
    com = phys.center_of_mass(pos, m)
    com_vel = phys.center_of_mass_velocity(vel, m)
    rel_pos = pos - com                        # center-relative position (m)
    rel_vel = vel - com_vel                    # center-relative velocity (m/s):
                                                # dr/dt needs velocity measured
                                                # in the SAME frame as the
                                                # radius, or a bulk cluster
                                                # drift alone (which changes no
                                                # distance between bodies) would
                                                # masquerade as inward/outward
                                                # motion
    radius_m = np.linalg.norm(rel_pos, axis=1)
    with np.errstate(invalid="ignore"):
        v_radial_m_s = np.divide(
            np.einsum("ij,ij->i", rel_pos, rel_vel), radius_m,
            out=np.full_like(radius_m, np.nan), where=radius_m > 0)
    radius_pc = radius_m / phys.PC                        # r/v arrays are SI;
    v_radial_pc_myr = v_radial_m_s * phys.MYR / phys.PC   # convert to match
                                                            # the printed labels
    for i in np.nonzero(e > 0)[0]:
        print(f"snapshot {k}  body {i}  e={e[i]:.3e}  "
              f"r={radius_pc[i]:.4g} pc  v_r={v_radial_pc_myr[i]:.4g} pc/Myr")

and check whether the same body ID reappears across every one of those five snapshots with a growing radius and a positive radial velocity at every snapshot — not merely at the one moment it was first flagged, and not just once or twice (v_r itself need not keep growing; it only needs to stay positive for the radius to keep increasing). A body whose radius keeps increasing and whose v_r stays positive at every one of those snapshots is showing stronger evidence of a persistent escape, not proof of one — five snapshots is still a short window against the evolving potential this Help file describes elsewhere; one that shows up once, or whose radius stalls or turns back over, or whose v_r sign flips, is more likely a body that is only briefly, instantaneously unbound before falling back in. Explain, physically, why the softening length that best approximates the smooth gravitational field of the cluster is not the softening length that best reproduces the close two-body encounters responsible for real evaporation — these are different goals, and this program's default optimizes for the first one.

EXP-18 · synthesis

Where Does “Collisionless” Stop Being True?

Galaxy-formation theory usually treats a galaxy as a collisionless system: stars feel the smooth mean gravitational field of everyone else, and individual close encounters are negligible over a Hubble time (this is what the relaxation time in timescale definitions quantifies). Yet the cluster mode's whole point is to show two-body relaxation acting within an achievable run length, and Experiment 11 showed you can dial evaporation in or out with the softening length alone. Collisionality depends on relaxation time relative to the system’s age; it is not guaranteed for every cluster. “Collisional” here means gravitational encounters, not stars touching.

Using what you found in Experiments 1, 7, 11 and 13, explain why gravitational encounters can matter in a cluster whose relaxation time is shorter than its age, while the main stellar body of a galaxy can often be treated as collisionless over its lifetime. Distinguish what this small, softened model illustrates from what it can establish about a particular real system.

The CSV, PNG and provenance sidecar for a saved run share the same run-start timestamp and identifying suffix, including when the files are saved in different directories. After its provenance comments, the CSV includes a copy of the console summary headed # Console summary:. The data header and snapshot rows follow.

All three modes write one row per recorded snapshot, with a provenance comment header (mode, parameters, BUILD_ID) prepended as #-prefixed lines before the column header row. Units are mixed and named explicitly per column, not a single system: times and lengths (columns ending _Myr/_pc) are in the astronomer-friendly units used throughout this program's display and printed output, while energies (columns ending _J) are in SI joules, matching the internal unit convention used throughout physics_nbg.py; see the column-by-column notes below.

Energies are reported in joules (SI), matching the internal unit convention used throughout physics_nbg.py; every length quantity is converted to parsecs for readability. potential_J is the softened potential energy \(U\), used for the total-energy conservation check; virial_work_J is the separate virial (force-weighted) sum \(W_{\rm vir}\) used to compute virial_ratio — see Reading the diagnostics above for why these two are not the same quantity. There are likewise two distinct kinetic-energy columns: kinetic_J is the raw, lab-frame total kinetic energy (so that energy_J = kinetic_J + potential_J exactly, matching the quantity the integrator conserves), while kinetic_com_J is the system's internal kinetic energy, measured relative to its own instantaneous center-of-mass velocity; virial_ratio is computed from kinetic_com_J, not kinetic_J — i.e. virial_ratio \(\approx\) 2*kinetic_com_J/abs(virial_work_J) — so that a uniform bulk velocity applied to the whole system (which changes kinetic_J but has nothing to do with internal virial balance) leaves the reported virial ratio unchanged. n_unbound is an integer count using the instantaneous unboundedness criterion (specific energy > 0 relative to the system, evaluated at that snapshot regardless of the body's current radial motion — see Domain of Validity); high_velocity_fraction is a fraction in [0, 1].

Domain of Validity

This is a pure-Python equal-mass, softened Newtonian particle model for teaching; there is no gas, radiation, external tide, cosmological expansion, individual timestep or tree quadrupole term. The console's 'settled' note checks the final 20% of the run for small changes in r50 and Q; passing it does not prove full equilibrium. --target_snapshots changes stored output, not the dense per-integration-step settling check.

The finite-time chaos-fit significance gate was calibrated in this program's tested regime; do not extrapolate it to a certified asymptotic exponent. A saturating, S-shaped (logistic-like) separation curve can still pass a selected-window growth fit under contrived conditions; inspect the entire curve, including its saturation. Extremely unphysical mixtures differing by hundreds of orders of magnitude can escape the numerical safeguards; ordinary solar-mass and parsec-scale experiments stay inside the intended regime. Default cluster softening inhibits close encounters, so it cannot by itself establish a physical evaporation timescale.
