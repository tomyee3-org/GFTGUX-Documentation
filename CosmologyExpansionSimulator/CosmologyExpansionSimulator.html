Description

CosmologyExpansionSimulator integrates the Friedmann equation for a homogeneous, isotropic (FLRW) universe filled with matter, radiation, spatial curvature, and a dark-energy fluid, and tracks the resulting scale factor \(a(t)\), Hubble parameter \(H(t)\), fractional energy-density budget \(\Omega_i(a)\), and deceleration parameter \(q(t)\) from deep in the radiation era to a chosen point in the future. It answers, numerically and for any cosmology you specify, the question every introductory cosmology course eventually asks: how old is the universe, and how does the answer depend on what the universe is made of?

One program covers three calculations, selected with --mode: a single expansion history (evolve), an overlay of several named or custom cosmologies with an age comparison (compare), and a systematic scan of the age of the universe across one cosmological parameter (age) — reproducing, among other things, the historical "age crisis": the Einstein-de Sitter model (flat, matter-only, \(\Omega_{m0}=1\)) could be too young for the oldest globular clusters. A lower matter density makes the universe older either by dropping flatness (an open, low-density, matter-only universe, no \(\Lambda\) at all, is old enough) or, if flatness is imposed instead, by adding a compensating dark-energy component to make up the difference. Either route by itself does not require dark energy — only the flatness-preserving route does, and only because flatness was assumed going in. What actually made dark energy hard to avoid was a separate line of evidence — the 1998 discovery, from Type Ia supernovae, that the expansion is accelerating, which no matter-only model, open or flat, can produce.

The Cosmological Principle and the FLRW Metric: On sufficiently large scales the universe looks, by observation, the same in every direction (isotropic) and from every location (homogeneous). The unique metric consistent with that symmetry is the Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker metric,

\[ds^2=-c^2dt^2+a(t)^2\left[\frac{dr^2}{1-kr^2}+r^2\left(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right],\]

where \(a(t)\) is the dimensionless scale factor (normalised to \(a=1\) today) nd \(k\in\{-1,0,+1\}\) fixes the spatial geometry: open (hyperbolic), flat (Euclidean), or closed (spherical). All of the geometry and all of the dynamics are carried by the single function \(a(t)\); this program's entire job is to compute it.

Beat 0 · Evolution

Substituting the FLRW metric into the Einstein field equations yields three relations for \(a(t)\): the first Friedmann equation, the second (acceleration) Friedmann equation, and a fluid continuity equation.

First Friedmann Equation, Dimensional form

\[H^2 = \left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho - \frac{k c^2}{a^2} + \frac{\Lambda c^2}{3}\]

where \(a(t)\) is the scale factor, \(G\) is the gravitational constant, \(c\) is the speed of light, and \(k\) represents spatial curvature.

Instead of the dimensional form, this program uses the modern dimensionless parameterization of the Friedmann equation which includes separate terms for the contributions of matter density, radiation density, and dark energy density:

First Friedmann Equation, Dimensionless Parameterization

\[\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2=H(a)^2=H_0^2\,E(a)^2,\qquad E(a)^2=\Omega_{m0}a^{-3}+\Omega_{r0}a^{-4}+\Omega_{k0}a^{-2}+\Omega_{\rm DE,0}\,g(a).\]

This is the equation the program integrates, written as \(da/dt=aH_0E(a)\). Neither \(G\) nor \(c\) needs to appear: both are absorbed into the definitions of \(H_0\) and the critical density \(\rho_{\rm crit}=3H_0^2/8\pi G\), and every \(\Omega_i\equiv\rho_i/\rho_{\rm crit}\) is dimensionless by construction.

Einstein-de Sitter space

In 1932, Albert Einstein and Willem de Sitter proposed a simplified model by applying three specific conditions to the general Friedmann equation: (a) The universe is spatially flat \(k = 0\); (b) the cosmological constant \(\Lambda\) is zero; (b) the energy density es entirely made up of non-relativistic matter.

For decades, the Einstein-de Sitter model served as the standard "textbook" model of cosmology because of its mathematical elegance and because early observational data could not easily distinguish it from other geometries.

However, beginning in the 1970s and especially in the 1980s and 1990s, measurements of the Hubble constant tended to give values around \(H_{\rm 0} \approx 70 \, \text{--} \, 100 \, \rm{ km \, s^{−1} \, Mpc^{−1} }\). Plugging these into the EdS formula gave ages of only about 6.56 to 56.5 billion years for \( H_{\rm 0} = 100 \) and 9.39 to 39.3 billion years for \( H_{\rm 0} = 70 \).

In contrast, observations of the oldest globular clusters and stellar evolution models often suggested ages of 12 to 16 billion years. So the universe predicted by the EdS model appeared to be younger than some of the stars inside it. That is the historical "age crisis."

We first explore the consequences of the Einstein-de Sitter model: 
# Einstein-de Sitter: flat, matter only -- the historical "age crisis" case
python main.py --mode evolve --omega_m 1.0 --omega_de 0.0

In the Scale-factor evolution plot in the upper left, we see that the usiverse has been continuously expanding from a singularity, but that the rate of expansion has been decelerating (The semi-log plot obscures this fact. Use your cursor to read off the scale facter versus time and plot on plain graph paper to see this.), the current age of the usiverse is 9.311 Gyr. Early int the universe's histery, the scale factor expanded rapidly, but at late time, the growth becomes extremely sluggish as the matter density dilutes.

The Hubble parameter plot tells us that initially, H(t) was immensely huge, but dropped off rapidly over the first few billion years. Using the zoom feature of Matplotlib, you can read off today's Hubble constant \(\approx 70\) at the current age of the universe \(\approx 9.3 \, \rm{Gyr} \)

The fractional energy density plot tells us that radiation dominated at the earliest times, but matter has dominated the universe since \( a \approx 10^{-4} \). The gray horizontal line \( \Omega_k(a) \) stays at zero across the entire timeline, indicating that the universe has been spatially flat throughout its history.

The lower right plot uses results from the Second Friedmann Equation:

\[\frac{\ddot a}{a}=-\frac{4\pi G}{3}\sum_i\left(\rho_i+\frac{3P_i}{c^2}\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad q(a)\equiv-\frac{\ddot a\,a}{\dot a^2}=\frac12\sum_{i=m,r,{\rm DE}}\Omega_i(a)\big(1+3w_i(a)\big).\]

\(q>0\) is decelerating, \(q<0\) is accelerating. Matter alone gives \(q=1/2\); radiation alone gives \(q=1\); a cosmological constant alone gives \(q=-1\) (de Sitter expansion).

The lower right plot tells us that after a brief spike at the origin, the expansion of the universe has been decelerating at a constant rate through outout its history. The constant deceleration parameter of \( q = 0.5 \) is the theoretical hallmark of a flat, matter-dominated universe.

The concordance cosmology

The flat \(\Lambda\)CDM (Lambda Cold Dark Matter) model is the current standard model of Big Bang cosmology. The model emerged as a consensus interpretation of unexpected observational data that forced physicists to abandon simpler models like Einstein-de Sitter in favor of a universe driven by dark energy.

  • Until 1998, the Einstein-de Sitter model was favored due to its theoretical simplicity and adaptability to observations consistent with cosmic inflation.
  • In 1998, however, two competing teams of researchers stunned the scientific community with data showing that the universe's expansion is accelerating, not decelerating. Explaining this required a non-zero cosmological constant \( \Lambda .\)
  • Multiple independent observations, including datasets collected by the WMAP (Wilkinson Microwave Anisotropy Probe) satellite launched in 2001, combined with large-scale galaxy surveys and CMB balloon experiments, led to a consensus view of a spatially flat universe comprised of roughly ~70% dark energy, ~25% cold dark matter, and ~5% ordinary matter.
  • The European Space Agency's Planck satellite provided high-precision mapping of the Cosmic Microwave Background. The Planck 2018 data release reinforced \(\Lambda\)CDM as the current best cosmological model.

In the following, we explore the \(\Lambda\)CDM model: 

# The concordance cosmology (Planck-2018-like flat LCDM). Omitting
# --omega_de forces flatness exactly; passing --omega_de 0.685 explicitly
# here instead would leave a stray Omega_k0 = -9.24e-5 from the default
# radiation density.

python main.py --mode evolve --H0 67.4 --omega_m 0.315

In the Scale-factor evolution plot, the vertical dashed line marks the present day at \(13.79\text{ Gyr}\) which is significantly older than the \(9.3\text{ Gyr}\) predicted by the Einstein-de Sitter model. The text box notes that the universe underwent an acceleration transition at \(z = 0.632\), marking when dark energy overtook matter as the main driver of xosmic evolution. Confirm this claim by using the zoom feature of Matplotlib to expand the plot between 0 and 2 Gyr, then plotting scale-factor versus time on plain graph paper. The rate of expansion slows down betwen 0 and 0.632 Gyr, then speeds back up again. Late in time, past about 10 Gyr, the curve actually bends upward, as you can confirm by plotting the graph between 5 and 40 Gyr and \(a\) between 0.4 to 5 on plain graph paper.

As with the Einstein-de Sitter model, the Hubble parameter history in the flat \(\Lambda\)CDM model shows a rapid decline from immenmsely high values early in the universe history to its present value of about \(H_0 = 67.4\text{ km s}^{-1}\text{ Mpc}^{-1}\) today. However, unlike the Einstein-de Sitter model, this curve flattens out into a nearly constant value rather than continuing to drop toward zero. This is the signature of dark energy, which drives an accelerating expansion and maintains a constant Hubble parameter into the distant future.

Beat 1 · An age scan is a conditional claim

Hold H0 fixed. How strongly can the composition change the time to reach a=1?

python main.py --mode age --scan_param omega_m --scan_lo 0.05 --scan_hi 1.5 --scan_n 50 --outdir ./figures

Read the endpoints in the summary: 20.75 and 8.105 Gyr for Ωm0=0.05 and 1.5. Locate the 13 Gyr reference line. The default scan adjusts dark energy to keep the models flat. Seventeen points are marked as recollapsing after reaching today, within the scan's look-ahead to a=5. An unmarked point has not been proved to expand forever.

DERIVED / NUMERICAL t0=∫₀¹ da/[a H0 E(a)] for a finite-age model. The numerical trajectory begins at a_i>0 with an analytic early-time offset. Its caption is a conditional calculation, not an independent measurement of the Universe's age.

Beat 2 · Four histories, one calculation

Compare the present-time marks and the printed age table. Which parameter set recollapses? Which has a future Big Rip?

python main.py --mode compare --presets EdS,lambdaCDM,closed,phantom --outdir ./figures

Keep the table alongside the figure: EdS 9.312 Gyr, lambdaCDM 13.79 Gyr, closed 8.538 Gyr and phantom 14.15 Gyr. The phantom note diagnoses an eventual fate; no plot here reaches infinite a. Each named preset supplies its own component budget.

DERIVED The acceleration relation is q=½Ωm+Ωr+½(1+3w)ΩDE. Curvature does not contribute a stress-energy term to this equation. Ask why similar Hubble rates can give different ages.

Beat 3 · Stop at a genuine turnaround

Trace the closed model up to its maximum a and back. Which sign of H belongs to each branch?

python main.py --mode evolve --omega_m 1.5 --omega_de 0.0 --continue_collapse --outdir ./figures

The summary reports a_turn≈2.9995, at 93.06 Gyr, and an estimated total lifetime of 186.1 Gyr. The contracting plot ends at a_i, not at the singularity. The q curve is undefined at turnaround where H=0.

ODE / SYMMETRY A true turnaround solves E(a)²=0. Reflecting the solution in time changes the sign of H. The doubling of turnaround time follows from the model's symmetry; the time itself is a numerical estimate.

Suggested experiments

  1. python main.py --mode evolve --omega_m 1 --omega_r 0 --omega_de 0 — compare the printed age to two thirds of the Hubble time.
  2. python main.py --mode age --scan_param omega_m --scan_lo 0.2 --scan_hi 1.2 --scan_n 20 --no_force_flat — which derived density now changes as you scan?
  3. python main.py --mode evolve --omega_m 1.5 --omega_de 0 --a_max 2 — does a plotting bound before the turnaround change the model's fate?
  4. python main.py --mode evolve --w0 -1.2 --a_max 60 — is a Big Rip on the plot, in the summary, or both?
  5. python main.py --mode evolve --omega_m 0 --omega_r 0 --omega_d e 1 --a_i 0.01 — what appears in the physical age field for this past-eternal case?
  6. python main.py --mode evolve --H0 67.4 --omega_m 0.315 --step_frac 0.0025 — how much do the printed age and transition redshift move?
  7. python main.py --mode evolve --w0 -1 --wa 0.2 --a_max 2 — evaluate w(a)=w0+wa(1−a) at a=2. Is that formula a measured far-future law?

Parameters

Run python main.py --help for the current command summary, and python main.py --version for the model version, which is also written into every CSV header. Options that do not apply to the selected mode are ignored, and the driver prints a short note if you changed one of them from its default anyway, so a scan option left on by accident during an evolve run does not go unnoticed. Check the printed summary, or the parameter block at the top of the CSV file, to confirm which values a given run actually used.

Where the model stops

The Friedmann equations describe an idealized homogeneous background. w(a)=w0+wa(1−a) is a phenomenological dark-energy fit, with density shape g(a)=a^[-3(1+w0+wa)] exp[-3wa(1−a)], not a measured future law. Fate classifications, early-time offsets and numerical ages have stated bounds; the age scan checks recollapse only through a=5. No plot includes galaxy structure, dark-energy microphysics or the quantum beginning of the Universe. A mathematical toy model need not describe the observed Universe.
