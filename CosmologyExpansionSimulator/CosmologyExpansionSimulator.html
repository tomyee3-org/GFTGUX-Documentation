Description

CosmologyExpansionSimulator integrates the Friedmann equation for a homogeneous, isotropic (FLRW) universe filled with matter, radiation, spatial curvature, and a dark-energy fluid, and tracks the resulting scale factor \(a(t)\), Hubble parameter \(H(t)\), fractional energy-density budget \(\Omega_i(a)\), and deceleration parameter \(q(t)\) from deep in the radiation era to a chosen point in the future. It answers, numerically and for any cosmology you specify, the question every introductory cosmology course eventually asks: how old is the universe, and how does the answer depend on what the universe is made of?

One program covers three calculations, selected with --mode: a single expansion history (evolve), an overlay of several named or custom cosmologies with an age comparison (compare), and a systematic scan of the age of the universe across one cosmological parameter (age) — reproducing, among other things, the historical "age crisis": the Einstein-de Sitter model (flat, matter-only, \(\Omega_{m0}=1\)) could be too young for the oldest globular clusters.

A lower matter density can lengthen the age in two ways: by dropping flatness and allowing an open model without dark energy, or by retaining flatness and adding a compensating dark-energy component. Whether a particular model is old enough to contain the oldest stars depends on its parameters. The age argument alone therefore does not require dark energy unless additional assumptions, such as flatness, are imposed.

What actually made dark energy hard to avoid was a separate line of evidence — the 1998 discovery, from Type Ia supernovae, that the expansion is accelerating, which no matter-only model, open or flat, can produce.

The Cosmological Principle and the FLRW Metric: On sufficiently large scales we model the universe as homogeneous and isotropic, consistent with observations. The unique metric consistent with that symmetry is the Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker metric,

\[ds^2=-c^2dt^2+a(t)^2\left[\frac{dr^2}{1-kr^2}+r^2\left(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right].\]

Here \(a(t)\) is dimensionless and normalized to one today, \(r\) is a comoving radial coordinate with units of length, and \(k\) is a constant with units of inverse length squared. Negative, zero and positive \(k\) describe open, flat and closed spatial geometry, respectively. In this convention, \(\Omega_{k0}=-kc^2/H_0^2\). Symmetry reduces the expansion dynamics to the function \(a(t)\), while \(k\) separately specifies the spatial curvature.

Beat 0 · Cosmological Evolution

Substituting the FLRW metric into the Einstein field equations yields three relations for \(a(t)\): the first Friedmann equation, the second (acceleration) Friedmann equation, and a fluid continuity equation.

First Friedmann Equation, Dimensional form

\[H^2 = \left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho - \frac{k c^2}{a^2} + \frac{\Lambda c^2}{3}\]

where \(a(t)\) is the scale factor, \(G\) is the gravitational constant, \(c\) is the speed of light, \(k\) represents spatial curvature, and \(\rho\) is the combined mass-equivalent density of baryonic matter, dark matter and radiation (energy density divided by \(c^2\)). The cosmological-constant contribution is written separately through the \(\Lambda\) term.

Instead of the dimensional form, this program uses the modern dimensionless parameterization of the Friedmann equation which includes separate terms for the contributions of matter density, radiation density, curvature and dark energy density. In the dimensionless formulation, the cosmological constant contribution is absorbed into the dark-energy density parameter \(\Omega_{\rm DE,0}\)​.

First Friedmann Equation, Dimensionless Parameterization

\[\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2=H(a)^2=H_0^2\,E(a)^2,\qquad E(a)^2=\Omega_{m0}a^{-3}+\Omega_{r0}a^{-4}+\Omega_{k0}a^{-2}+\Omega_{\rm DE,0}\,g(a).\]

This is the equation the program integrates, written as \(da/dt=aH_0E(a)\). Neither \(G\) nor \(c\) needs to appear: both are absorbed into the definitions of \(H_0\) and the critical density \(\rho_{\rm crit}=3H_0^2/8\pi G\), and every \(\Omega_i\equiv\rho_i/\rho_{\rm crit}\) is dimensionless by construction.

Relative to their present values, matter density varies as \(a^{-3}\), radiation density as \(a^{-4}\), and dark-energy density by the factor \(g(a)\). Matter is diluted by the expanding volume; radiation also loses energy through redshift. The curvature contribution to \(E(a)^2\) varies as \(a^{-2}\).

Einstein-de Sitter space

In 1932, Albert Einstein and Willem de Sitter proposed a simplified model by applying three specific conditions to the general Friedmann equation: (a) The universe is spatially flat \(k = 0\); (b) the cosmological constant \(\Lambda\) is zero; (c) the energy density is entirely made up of non-relativistic matter.

For decades, the Einstein-de Sitter model served as the standard "textbook" model of cosmology because of its mathematical elegance and because early observational data could not easily distinguish it from other geometries.

However, beginning in the 1970s and especially in the 1980s and 1990s, measurements of the Hubble constant tended to give values around \(H_{\rm 0} \approx 70 \, \text{--} \, 100 \, \rm{ km \, s^{−1} \, Mpc^{−1} }\), creating serious issues. For example, the Einstein–de Sitter relation \(t_0=2/(3H_0)\) gives an age for the universe of about 6.52 Gyr if \(H_0=100\ {\rm km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}\), or 9.31 Gyr if \(H_0=70\ {\rm km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}\). Such ages could be smaller than the estimated ages of the oldest globular clusters: the historical age problem.

We first explore the consequences of the Einstein-de Sitter model: 
# Einstein-de Sitter: flat, matter only -- the historical "age crisis" case
python main.py --mode evolve --omega_m 1 --omega_r 0 --omega_de 0

In the Scale-factor evolution panel in the upper left, we see that the universe has been continuously expanding from a singularity, but that the rate of expansion has been decelerating (The semi-log plot obscures this fact. Use your cursor to read off the scale factor versus time and plot on plain graph paper to see this.), the current age of the universe is about 9.3123 Gyr. Early in the universe's history, the scale factor expanded rapidly, but at late time, the growth becomes extremely sluggish as the matter density dilutes.

The Hubble parameter panel tells us that initially, H(t) was immensely huge, but dropped off rapidly over the first few billion years. Using the zoom feature of Matplotlib, you can read off today's Hubble constant \(\approx 70\) at the current age of the universe \(\approx 9.3 \, \rm{Gyr} \). As time progresses, the Hubble parameter steadily drops towards zero.

The Fractional energy density panel tells us that the matter fraction is one throughout this idealized model; radiation and dark energy are absent and spatial curvature is zero.

The Deceleration \(\to\) acceleration panel plots \(q\) from the Second Friedmann Equation:

\[\frac{\ddot a}{a}=-\frac{4\pi G}{3}\sum_i\left(\rho_i+\frac{3P_i}{c^2}\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad q(a)\equiv-\frac{\ddot a\,a}{\dot a^2}=\frac12\sum_{i=m,r,{\rm DE}}\Omega_i(a)\big(1+3w_i(a)\big).\]

\(q>0\) is decelerating, \(q<0\) is accelerating. Matter alone gives \(q=1/2\); radiation alone gives \(q=1\); a cosmological constant alone gives \(q=-1\) (de Sitter expansion).

This panel tells us that the deceleration parameter is \(q=1/2\) throughout. Constant q does not mean constant physical acceleration. Here \(a(t)\propto t^{2/3}\): the scale factor grows without bound, while its slope \(\dot a\) decreases. The dimensionless deceleration parameter is constant even though \(\ddot a\) is not.

The constant deceleration parameter of \( q = 0.5 \) is the theoretical hallmark of a flat, matter-dominated universe.

The concordance cosmology

The flat \(\Lambda\)CDM (Lambda Cold Dark Matter) model is the current standard model of Big Bang cosmology. The model emerged as a consensus interpretation of unexpected observational data that forced physicists to abandon simpler models like Einstein-de Sitter in favor of a universe driven by dark energy.

  • Until 1998, the Einstein-de Sitter model was favored due to its theoretical simplicity and adaptability to observations consistent with cosmic inflation.
  • That year, however, two competing teams of researchers stunned the scientific community with data showing that the universe's expansion is accelerating, not decelerating. Within General Relativity and the FLRW framework, ordinary matter and radiation cannot account for that acceleration. A cosmological constant is the simplest explanation, but the program also explores alternative dark-energy equations of state.
  • Multiple independent observations, including datasets collected by the WMAP (Wilkinson Microwave Anisotropy Probe) satellite launched in 2001, combined with large-scale galaxy surveys and CMB balloon experiments, led to a consensus view of a spatially flat universe comprised of roughly 70% dark energy, ~25% cold dark matter, and ~5% ordinary matter.
  • The European Space Agency's Planck satellite provided high-precision mapping of the Cosmic Microwave Background. The Planck 2018 data release reinforced \(\Lambda\)CDM as the current best cosmological model.

In the following, we explore the \(\Lambda\)CDM model: 

# The concordance cosmology (Planck-2018-like flat LCDM). Omitting
# --omega_de forces flatness exactly; passing --omega_de 0.685 explicitly
# here instead would leave a stray Omega_k0 = -9.24e-5 from the default
# radiation density.

python main.py --mode evolve --H0 67.4 --omega_m 0.315

In the Scale-factor evolution panel, the vertical dashed line marks today at about 13.79 Gyr. The annotation gives an acceleration-transition redshift of \(z\approx0.632\). The console gives \(a\approx0.613\) for this transition; reading the sampled trajectory places it at a cosmic time of about 7.69 Gyr. We can verify these from the Matplotlib graph by first zooming in on cosmic time \(0 \le t \le 10\) Gyr with scale factor \(0.1 \le a \le 1 \), then cosmic time \(10 \le t \le 40\) Gyr with scale factor \(0.5 \le a \le 10 \), and plotting the points that you read on plain graph paper or using a spreadsheet. The expansion decelerates before the transition and accelerates afterward. Matter–dark-energy equality occurs later, at \(a\approx0.772\), or about 10.29 Gyr. These events differ because acceleration depends on pressure as well as density.

As with the Einstein-de Sitter model, the Hubble parameter history in the flat \(\Lambda\)CDM model shows a rapid decline from immensely high values early in the universe history to its present value of about \(H_0 = 67.4\text{ km s}^{-1}\text{ Mpc}^{-1}\) today. However, unlike the Einstein-de Sitter model, this curve flattens out into a nearly constant value rather than continuing to drop toward zero. This is the signature of dark energy, which drives an accelerating expansion and maintains a constant Hubble parameter into the distant future.

The Fractional energy-density content panel shows the changing relative contributions of the universe's different components across cosmic history plotted as a function of the scale factor \(a\). Radiation dominates the early universe (\(a \lesssim 10^{-4}\)). As the universe expands past \(a \approx 10^{-4}\), matter takes over, dominating the universe's energy density for billions of years until "shortly" before the present day, the crossover occurring at \(a \approx 0.77\), or \(\approx 10 \, \text{Gyr} \) after the big bang. At late times, dark energy (\(\Omega_{DE} \)) rises sharply to dominate the energy density of the universe. The horizontal dotted line illustrates that the sum of all fractional energy densities equals exactly 1 at every point in cosmic history.

The Deceleration \(\to\) acceleration panel shows that early in the universe's history, the attractive force of matter caused the expansion to decelerate. Where \(q\) crosses from positive to negative marks where cosmic expansion transitioned from slowing down to speeding up. In the present day, (\(t_0 = 13.8\text{ Gyr}\)), the curve is well below zero, indicating that the universe is currently in a state of accelerated expansion. At late times, the curve is predicted to flatten out, approaching \(-1.0\) as a limit, which is the signature of a dark energy-dominated universe expanding exponentially.

Speculative alternative: The Big Rip

The "Big Rip" is a hypothetical scenario for the ultimate fate of the universe, driven by a specialized type of dark energy known as phantom energy. Alternative theories have proposed that dark energy need not be a cosmological constant but varies with \(a,\) this variability characterized by the parameterized dark energy equation of state:

\[w(a)=w_0+w_a(1-a).\]

In standard \(\Lambda\)CDM cosmology, \(w_0 = -1\) and \(w_a = 0\), yielding a constant \(w(a) = -1\), meaning the dark energy density remains constant as the universe expands. In contrast, phantom energy represents a scenario where the equation of state falls below \(-1\) (such as having \(w_0 < -1\)), causing \(w(a) < -1\) across cosmic time and leading to a growing energy density as space expands.

This growing energy density results in a compounding feedback loop where more expansion leads to higher dark energy density, which causes faster expansion. The expansion rate accelerates so aggressively that the scale factor reaches infinity in a finite amount of time. Extension of some idealized phantom models suggests that this runaway expansion could even overpower all fundamental forces holding matter together, so that everything from galaxy clusters to stars, planets, molecules and even subatomic particles become torn apart.

The following formula estimates the time until the Big Rip:
\[w_0<-1,\ w_a=0:\qquad t_{\rm rip}-t_0\approx\frac{1}{-\tfrac32(1+w_0)\,H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{\rm DE,0}}}.\]

\(w_0 < -1\) does not automatically guarantee a Big Rip. Geometry is also important! If a universe is closed (meaning it has a curved, spherical shape where \(\Omega_{k0} < 0\)), gravity can pull everything back together. A closed universe might actually recollapse (a Big Crunch) before phantom energy has time to grow strong enough to rip it apart. Because of this, just knowing \(w_0 < -1\) isn't enough; the computer program has to check whether gravity or phantom energy wins the race.

Current observations do not establish a Big Rip as the fate of our universe. The standard flat \(\Lambda\)CDM model remains an important baseline, although some combinations of observations hint that dark energy may evolve. Those hints are not yet conclusive. If dark energy remains a positive cosmological constant, the expansion approaches an exponential form and distant galaxies eventually drift out of view.

In the following, we explore a Big Rip scenario. Here \(w_a=0\), so \(w(a)=w_0=-1.2\) throughout the model: 

# Phantom dark energy: w0 < -1 can lead to a future Big Rip, if no
# earlier recollapse intervenes first
python main.py --mode evolve --w0 -1.2 --a_max 1000

The solid curve ends at the requested \(a_{\max}\), marked by a dot. The dashed curve continues the same Friedmann model for display, and the dotted red line marks its finite-time limit as \(a\to\infty\). The CSV still ends at \(a_{\max}\). The plotted time can differ from the console's DE-only estimate because the continuation retains the other components. Omit --t_max to enable this display: supplying any explicit --t_max disables the continuation, even if that time lies beyond the predicted Rip.

We will not provide a detailed analysis of the output, but you should have sufficient skills from our previous explorations to thoroughly understand these plots.

Historically important alternative: The recollapsing universe

For much of the mid-to-late 20th century (long before dark energy was discovered), most researchers pursued three primary scenarios for the fate of the universe:
  • The Open Universe (\(\Omega_m < 1, k \lt 0\)): Expands forever while decelerating, approaching coasting at late times.
  • The Flat/Critical Universe (\(\Omega_m = 1, k = 0\)): The Einstein-de Sitter model. Expands forever with \(a(t)\) proportional to \(t^{2/3}\); its expansion rate \(da/dt\) tends toward zero, while \(a\) itself remains unbounded .
  • The Closed Universe (\(\Omega_m > 1, k \gt 0\)): The density of matter is high enough that space is positively curved (spherical) and gravity eventually overpowers the expansion, pulling everything back together into a Big Crunch.

Other models that were pursued included a cyclic universe where each Big Crunch would bounce into a new Big Bang.

These were all \(\Lambda=0,\) matter-only models. Before precision measurements in the late 1990s showed that the universe is flat and dominated by dark energy, cosmologists actively tried to measure whether \(\Omega_m\) was greater than or less than 1 to determine if our universe was destined to suffer this closed, recollapsing fate or expand forever.

Here we explore the consequences of a recollapsing universe:

# A closed universe that recollapses; mirror the contracting branch back
# down to a_i (not literally to the a=0 singularity -- see --continue_collapse)
python main.py --mode evolve --omega_m 1.5 --omega_de 0.0 --continue_collapse

As with the Big Rip scenario, we will not provide a detailed analysis of the output, but you should have sufficient skills from our previous explorations to thoroughly understand most of these plots. The exception might be the Fractional energy-density panel, which shows peculiar behavior toward the right-hand side.

The “fractions” shown here need not lie between zero and one: today, \(\Omega_m=1.5\) and \(\Omega_k\approx-0.5\), while their sum, including radiation, remains one. As the turnaround at \(a\approx3\) approaches, the critical density, proportional to \(H^2\), tends to zero. The normalized matter and curvature terms therefore grow without bound in opposite directions, although the physical densities remain finite. The plot clips these curves well before turnaround; the small radiation contribution becomes visible as the narrow orange spike. At turnaround itself, the normalized quantities are undefined. Because the horizontal axis is \(a\), contraction retraces the same curves.

Beat 1 · Compare Models

The dark-energy fraction plot shows dark energy sharply taking over around \(a \sim 1\).
  3. Open (Open Universe Model)
    • Key Metrics: Age = \(11.29\text{ Gyr}\) | \(H_0 t_0 = 0.8085\) | \(q_0 = 0.15\) | \(z_{\text{accel}} = \text{n/a}\)

Beat 2 · Historical Age Crisis

The plots generated by the following command provide a visualization of the historical Einstein-de Sitter (EdS) Age Crisis and how altering cosmological parameters attempted to resolve it.

# The historical age problem: age of the universe vs Omega_m, flat by construction
python main.py --mode age --scan_param omega_m --scan_lo 0.05 --scan_hi 1.5

What the plots show:

The program scans flat universes (\(\Omega_k=0\)) while varying \(\Omega_{m0}\). The left panel shows the age of the universe today, and the right panel shows the dimensionless quantity \(H_0 t_0 .\) Two horizontal reference lines are drawn on the right:

  • \(H_0 t_0 = 1\) (the Milne empty universe)
  • \(H_0 t_0 = \frac23\) (the Einstein-de Sitter universe)
The curve passes almost exactly through \(H_0t_0=2/3\) at \(\Omega_{m0}=1,\) which is exactly what it should do.

Connecting this to the historical age crisis:

Suppose observers determine \(H_0 \approx 70\ {\rm km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}.\) The Hubble time is then \(H_0^{-1}\approx 14\ {\rm Gyr}.\)

The Einstein-de Sitter model predicts \(t_0=\frac23 H_0^{-1} \approx 9.3\ {\rm Gyr}.\) Looking at your left panel, the point near \(\Omega_{m0}=1\) is indeed around \(9.2-9.3\ {\rm Gyr},\) exactly matching the analytic formula.

Historically, however, astronomers were finding globular cluster ages around \(12-15\ {\rm Gyr}\) and sometimes even higher. Thus: \(t_{\rm stars} > t_{\rm universe}\) which is impossible.

That is the age crisis.

An important thing that the plot shows:

Notice where the dashed 13 Gyr line intersects the curve. By eye it occurs around \(\Omega_{m0}\approx 0.34.\) That is extremely interesting because it says:

  • For a fixed \(H_0,\) a flat universe reaches an age of roughly 13 Gyr only when the matter density is much smaller than the Einstein-de Sitter value \(\Omega_{m0}=1.\)

In other words, if the measured \(H_0\) is kept fixed, reducing the matter density makes the universe older. The plot therefore visually demonstrates why cosmologists became uncomfortable with the \(\Omega_m=1\) model.

At the high-density end of this flat scan, \(\Omega_{\Lambda0}=1-\Omega_{m0}-\Omega_{r0}\) becomes negative. The orange triangles mark models that reach today’s size but subsequently recollapse within the scan’s \(a\le5\) search range. Flatness alone therefore does not determine their fate.

Why \(\Lambda\) helps:

The plot imposes \(\Omega_{\Lambda0}=1-\Omega_{m0}-\Omega_{r0}\) to preserve flatness. As \(\Omega_m\) decreases, \(\Omega_\Lambda\) increases. This enables the age to climb dramatically. The values following come from separate evolutions at \(H_0=70\), with default radiation and flatness imposed; the selected matter densities are not the plotted scan’s grid points:

\(\Omega_m\)Age today(Gyr)\(H_0t_0\)
1.5 8.10497 0.580233
1.0 9.31118 0.666586
0.5 11.60444 0.830760
0.3 13.46127 0.963690
0.1 17.82569 1.276138

This is essentially the historical solution to the age crisis:

  • Einstein-de Sitter \(\Omega_m=1,\Lambda=0)\) gives too small an age.
  • Introducing a cosmological constant increases \(H_0 t_0.\)
  • A universe with roughly \(\Omega_m \approx 0.3, \, \Omega_\Lambda \approx 0.7\) achieves \(H_0t_0 \approx 0.96,\)
which for \(H_0\approx70\) gives an age around 13.4 Gyr.

The right-hand plot makes this especially clear: the age crisis was fundamentally a crisis in the value of \(H_0 t_0.\)

  • Einstein-de Sitter is fixed at \(H_0 t_0=\frac23.\)
  • The observed universe is much closer to \(H_0 t_0\approx0.95-1.\)
The entire historical debate can almost be read directly from the right-hand panel. The age crisis arose because the \(\Omega_m=1\) point sits far below the age required by the oldest stars, while moving toward lower matter density (and correspondingly higher \(\Lambda\) in a flat universe) naturally raises \(H_0 t_0\) into the observationally acceptable range.

Suggested Experiments

The exercises are divided into a core sequence, arranged from reading the output through controlled comparisons to numerical work, and a set of optional projects that connect to open questions in cosmology. Four synthesis exercises close the set. Most exercises use the program and a spreadsheet; optional research projects also call for outside sources.

Useful definitions for these exercises:

\[ \begin{flalign*} w_i={P_i\over\rho_i c^2},\qquad \rho_{{\rm crit},0}={3H_0^2\over8\pi G},\qquad \Omega_{i0}={\rho_{i0}\over\rho_{{\rm crit},0}}. && \end{flalign*} \]

\[ \begin{flalign*} \dot\rho_i+3H\left(\rho_i+{P_i\over c^2}\right)=0,\qquad g(a)=a^{-3(1+w_0+w_a)}\exp[-3w_a(1-a)]. && \end{flalign*} \]

\[ \begin{flalign*} \Omega_{k0}=1-\Omega_{m0}-\Omega_{r0}-\Omega_{{\rm DE},0}, \qquad z={1\over a}-1. && \end{flalign*} \]

\[ \begin{flalign*} \Omega_m(a)={\Omega_{m0}a^{-3}\over E(a)^2},\quad \Omega_r(a)={\Omega_{r0}a^{-4}\over E(a)^2},\quad \Omega_k(a)={\Omega_{k0}a^{-2}\over E(a)^2},\quad \Omega_{\rm DE}(a)={\Omega_{{\rm DE},0}g(a)\over E(a)^2}. && \end{flalign*} \]

These equations assume that the material components are separately conserved. Here \(g(a)=\rho_{\rm DE}(a)/\rho_{{\rm DE},0}\). The plotted fractions use the instantaneous critical density \(3H(a)^2/(8\pi G)\); curvature is a geometric contribution to the bookkeeping, rather than a material fluid.

Numerical method and time origin

The expanding branch uses \(da/dt=aH_0E(a)\), with fourth-order Runge–Kutta steps sized approximately as \(\text{step\_frac}/H(a).\) This is a step-size rule, not an error-controlled adaptive method. The code finds a genuine turnaround from \(E(a)^2=0\) and handles the nearby time integral separately. The forward run ends at the first of a_max, t_max and a turnaround; --continue_collapse adds the mirrored contracting branch, subject to a time limit on the returned data.

Integration begins at \(a_i>0\). Where a finite Big Bang and a dominant early component can be established, an asymptotic offset estimates the preceding elapsed time. If \(E(a)^2 \approx C a^p\) with \(C \gt 0\), the integral to \(a=0\) converges only for \(p \lt 0\). \[ {dt\over da}\approx {1\over H_0\sqrt C}\,a^{-1-p/2}. \] The integral from zero to a positive scale factor is finite only when the power of \(a\) exceeds \(-1\), which here means \(p<0\). At \(p=0\) it diverges logarithmically; for \(p>0\) it diverges as a power. In the dark-energy-only CPL model, \(p=-3(1+w_0+w_a)\) is the leading exponent as \(a\to0\). A past-eternal model has no finite age from a Big Bang; its elapsed time from \(a_i\) is separately labeled bookkeeping.

EXP-1 · introductory

Read the Concordance Universe

Run python main.py --mode evolve --H0 67.4 --omega_m 0.315 (omit --omega_de so the program forces flatness exactly; passing --omega_de 0.685 here would leave a stray \(\Omega_{k0}=-9.24\times10^{-5}\) from the default radiation density). Record the age of the universe, \(q_0\), the matter-radiation and matter-dark-energy equality redshifts, and the acceleration-transition redshift. Which happened more recently: matter-dark-energy equality, or the onset of accelerated expansion? Should those two necessarily coincide, given the formula for \(q(a)\)? For a bonus, derive \(a_{m\Lambda}=(\Omega_{m0}/\Omega_{\Lambda0})^{1/3}\) and \(a_{\rm accel}=(\Omega_{m0}/2\Omega_{\Lambda0})^{1/3}\) for flat matter+\(\Lambda\) with negligible radiation, verify both against the printed values, and explain the factor of two between them.

EXP-2 · introductory

The Historical Age Crisis

Run python main.py --mode evolve --omega_m 1 --omega_r 0 --omega_de 0 (Einstein-de Sitter). Record the age. The oldest globular clusters in the Milky Way are reliably dated to roughly 12-13 Gyr. Is this matter-only, flat universe old enough to contain them? This was a real crisis in cosmology before 1998 -- explain, in one sentence using only the formula \(t_0=(2/3)/H_0\), why raising \(H_0\) cannot solve the problem. Now run --mode evolve --H0 70 --omega_m 0.3 --omega_de 0.0 (an open, low-density, still matter-only universe -- no \(\Lambda\) at all) and compare its age. This is the historically important point: Dropping flatness can lengthen the age without adding dark energy. The \(\Omega_{m0}=0.3\) example is older than EdS but still younger than the reference cluster ages; lower matter density or a lower \(H_0\) can lengthen it further. What finally made dark energy hard to avoid was a separate observation (EXP-6), not the age alone.

EXP-3 · introductory

Three Eras

Using the closed-form \(a_{\rm eq}=\Omega_{r0}/\Omega_{m0}\), predict the matter-radiation equality scale factor before checking it against the printed summary.

EXP-4 · intermediate

An Analytic Validation Case (Milne)

Run --mode evolve --omega_m 0 --omega_r 0 --omega_de 0 (the Milne universe: pure curvature, nothing else). The exact solution is \(a=H_0t\) for all time, where \(H_0\) must first be converted from km/s/Mpc to Gyr\(^{-1}\) (the program does this once, internally; do it yourself by hand here). To compare at the level of the integration error, use the program's own constants: \(1\ \mathrm{Mpc} = 3.08567758149×10^{22}\ \mathrm{m} \) and \(1\ \mathrm{Gyr} = 10^9\ \mathrm{Julian\ years} = 3.15576\times 10^{16}\ \mathrm{s}\) (exactly), so that \(H_0 = 70\ \mathrm{km/s/Mpc} = 0.0715898515532304\ \mathrm{Gyr}^{-1}\). With rounder constants your 'error' would measure your unit conversion, not the integrator. Alternatively, for Milne \(t_0 = 1/H_0\) exactly, so the printed 17-digit 'Age today' is itself \(1/H_0\) in Gyr. Using the CSV output, compute the relative error \(|a_{\rm program}(t)-H_0t|/(H_0t)\) at several rows spanning the run; the run starts at a small but nonzero \(t\) set by the early-time offset (there is no literal \(t=0\) row), but skip the first row or two anyway if \(t\) there is so small that ordinary floating-point round-off in \(a_{\rm program}(t)\) dominates the relative error you are trying to measure. Why does the constant right-hand side \(da/dt=H_0\) make this a clean test? What additional behavior would require a model with nonconstant \(aH(a)\) or a genuine turnaround? For Milne, \(\Omega_{k0}=1\), so \(E(a)^2=a^{-2}\). Substitute this into \(dt/da=1/(aH_0E(a))\) and integrate from \(a=0\) to a positive scale factor. Is the elapsed cosmic time finite? How can that be consistent with Milne's \(a=0\) being a boundary of the expanding coordinate patch of Minkowski spacetime, rather than a curvature singularity?

EXP-5 · introductory/intermediate

The Bookkeeping Identity

Using the evolve-mode CSV for any cosmology of your choice, verify numerically that \(\Omega_m(a)+\Omega_r(a)+\Omega_k(a)+\Omega_{\rm DE}(a)=1\) at every row where the quantities are defined, to within floating-point round-off (skip any row flagged as a turnaround/undefined row, where a vanishing denominator makes the individual \(\Omega_i\) not meaningfully defined even though the identity's derivation below still explains why it would hold in the limit). Then derive this identity algebraically in one line, starting from the definition \(\Omega_i(a)\equiv\rho_i(a)/\rho_{\rm crit}(a)\) and the first Friedmann equation. Because every term shares the same denominator, this identity holds by construction regardless of whether \(a(t)\) was integrated correctly -- it checks that the program's internal bookkeeping and CSV export are self-consistent, not that the numerical integration of \(a(t)\) is right (EXP-11 builds that independent check).

EXP-6 · introductory/intermediate

1998 in One Plot

Run the concordance cosmology and read the sign of \(q(t)\) at early times versus today. Using only the formula \(q=\tfrac12\sum\Omega_i(1+3w_i)\), explain algebraically why a universe with only matter and radiation can never have \(q<0\), and why, within General Relativity and the FLRW model, accelerating expansion requires a component with negative effective \(\rho+3p\) -- \(w<-1/3\) in the fluid picture this program implements. A cosmological constant is one realization of that component (it can be written either geometrically, on the left side of Einstein's equation, or as an exactly equivalent \(w=-1\) fluid on the right side, as this program treats it); modified gravity, which changes the left side of Einstein's equation instead of adding a fluid, is a genuinely different route to \(q<0\) and lies outside what this program can model.

EXP-7 · intermediate

Open and Closed, Side by Side

Run --mode compare --presets open,closed first: these two share zero dark energy but differ in both \(\Omega_{m0}\) and the derived \(\Omega_{k0}\) (curvature makes up whatever matter does not, under flatness being dropped), so their very different fates can be explained using only the matter+curvature first Friedmann equation \(E(a)^2=\Omega_{m0}a^{-3}+\Omega_{k0}a^{-2}\), without reference to dark energy at all -- do that, tracking both terms. Then add lambdaCDM to the comparison (--presets EdS,lambdaCDM,open,closed) and note that it differs from the other three in \(H_0\), \(\Omega_{m0}\), and the presence of dark energy simultaneously, so any single difference you see there cannot be attributed to curvature alone -- fate follows from the full \(E(a)^2\) budget, not from \(\Omega_{k0}\) in isolation once dark energy is present.

EXP-9 · intermediate

Evolving Dark Energy, Phantom, and the Cosmological Constant

Run --mode compare --presets lambdaCDM,evolving_de,phantom,desi_like. All four share the same \(H_0\) and \(\Omega_{m0}\); only \(w_0\) and \(w_a\) differ, so any difference you see is attributable to the dark-energy sector alone. Compare \(\Lambda\)CDM and evolving_de over \(0.2\le a\le1\), moving backward from today. Find where their \(H(a)\) values first differ by more than 1%, relative to \(\Lambda\)CDM, and quote the redshift. Interpolate to common scale factors before comparing. The compare CSV contains \(H\) for each model. This compares background histories; it does not calculate the precision required of a real survey. Caution: despite its name, evolving_de (\(w_0=-0.8\), \(w_a=0.3\)) is not pure quintessence over the whole range this program can show you -- using \(w(a)=w_0+w_a(1-a)\), verify for yourself that it crosses \(w=-1\) at \(a=5/3\) (since \(dw/da=-w_a\ne0\), it genuinely crosses rather than merely touching). In the future interval \(1\le a<5/3\), the model is quintessence-like; at \(a=5/3\) it crosses \(w=-1\), and beyond that it is phantom-like. Separately, using \(w(a)=w_0+w_a(1-a)\), explain why desi_like (\(w_0=-0.7,\ w_a=-1.0\)) moves in the opposite direction into the past from evolving_de.

EXP-10 · intermediate

The Flatness Problem, Quantitatively

Run --mode evolve with \(\Omega_{m0}=0.3\), \(\Omega_{\rm de}=0.6999\) (so \(\Omega_{k0}=1-0.3-9.24\times10^{-5}-0.6999\approx7.6\times10^{-6}\), already tiny). With the default \(\Omega_{r0}=9.24\times10^{-5}\), matter-radiation equality is at \(a_{\rm eq}=\Omega_{r0}/\Omega_{m0}\approx3.08\times10^{-4}\). Using the CSV output, sample two separate era pairs: \(a=0.1\) and \(0.01\), where matter dominates, and \(a=10^{-6}\) and \(10^{-8}\), deep in radiation domination. With the default \(a_i=10^{-8}\), \(a=10^{-8}\) is exactly the first row of the CSV; other scale factors will not generally land on the grid exactly—state whether you used nearest rows or interpolation. Read off \(\Omega_k(a)\) at each point and estimate the log–log slope of \(|\Omega_k(a)|\) separately for each pair. Explain from the \(a^{-2}\) (curvature) vs. \(a^{-3}\) (matter) vs. \(a^{-4}\) (radiation) scalings in \(E(a)^2\) why the slope is approximately one in the matter era and approximately two in the radiation era. Note that these are dominance-limit scalings, so small departures from exact integers are expected.

EXP-11 · advanced

Verify the Second Friedmann Equation Independently

Using the evolve-mode CSV for the concordance cosmology, numerically differentiate \(a(t)\) twice to estimate \(q(t)=-\ddot a\,a/\dot a^2\) directly from the trajectory, at several times of your choosing, and compare with the program's own \(q(t)\) (built on the algebraic second Friedmann equation, not on differentiating the first). The output time grid is not uniform in \(t\) (the step size is Hubble-time-scaled), so a simple equal-spacing second-difference formula is not directly valid -- either use the general three-point finite-difference formula for unevenly spaced points, or first resample \(a(t)\) onto a uniform grid by interpolation before differentiating. Exclude rows right at a checkpoint or turnaround. Define precisely what you mean by "coarser cadence" (e.g., use only every \(k\)-th CSV row) before asking whether agreement gets better or worse with it.

EXP-12 · intermediate/advanced

A Universe That Recollapses

Run --mode evolve --omega_m 1.5 --omega_de 0.0 --continue_collapse. Record \(a_{\rm turn}\), \(t_{\rm turn}\), and the total lifetime. First derive \(a_{\rm turn}\) algebraically by setting \(E(a)^2=0\) with only matter and curvature (neglecting the tiny radiation term, so your hand value will be approximately, not exactly, 3) and compare with the program's value, which does include that small radiation term. Then explain, using only the fact that \(E(a)^2\) depends on \(a\) but never on the sign of \(\dot a\), why the total lifetime must be exactly twice the time to turnaround -- with no need to re-integrate the contracting half. Note that the mirrored data in the CSV run back down only to \(a_i\), not to \(a=0\); the quoted total-lifetime figure extrapolates the missing interval near each end through the analytic early-time offset, not through additional integrated points. For a convergence check, repeat at a few values of --a_i and --step_frac and see how much \(t_{\rm turn}\) moves.

EXP-14 · advanced

Numerical Convergence

Repeat the concordance run with \(H_0=67.4\) and \(\Omega_{m0}=0.315\), using --step_frac 0.04, 0.02, 0.01 and 0.005 in separate runs. Keep the full printed ages. Compare with a finer reference run, such as --step_frac 0.0001, and quantify the residual differences. Estimate the empirical convergence order only where the differences are clearly above floating-point noise. Extend to smaller steps and investigate where that estimate ceases to be useful. Then repeat for the closed-universe turnaround in EXP-12. This is not the same convergence question as the ordinary trajectory: \(a_{\rm turn}\) is found by algebraic bisection (converges to bisection tolerance, essentially independent of step_frac), while \(t_{\rm turn}\) additionally depends on the RK4-integrated time accumulated before the handoff to that root-finder plus a separate Gauss-Legendre quadrature -- so ask separately how \(a_{\rm turn}\) and \(t_{\rm turn}\) each behave as step_frac is refined, rather than expecting one single RK4 convergence order to describe both.

EXP-16 · advanced

The Dark-Energy Degeneracy Problem

Find a pair \((w_0,w_a)\) distinct from \((-1,0)\), with \(|w_a|\ge0.05\), that reproduces the concordance cosmology's age to within 0.1% and \(q_0\) to within 0.01, holding its other parameters fixed. At fixed present densities, \(q_0\) depends on \(w_0\), but not on \(w_a\), because \(w(1)=w_0\). First choose \(w_0\) close enough to match \(q_0\), then adjust \(w_a\) to match the age. Now compare the two models' \(\Omega_{\rm DE}(a)\) curves at \(a=0.3\) (a past epoch actually reachable by real surveys) and at \(a=3\) (a CPL extrapolation into the future, not a prediction -- CPL has no guarantee of remaining valid that far from \(a=1\); label it as such). This near-degeneracy -- similar today, different in the past -- is exactly why real dark-energy surveys need data spanning a wide range of redshift, not just a single-epoch measurement, to break it.

EXP-17d · advanced (numerical-methods project)

The Closed-Matter-Only Universe, Exactly

The flat matter-only case has a closed form (\(a\propto t^{2/3}\)), but so does the closed matter-only case (\(\Omega_{r0}=\Omega_{\rm DE,0}=0\), \(\Omega_{m0}>1\)): it is the classic cycloid, \(a(\eta)=\tfrac{\Omega_{m0}}{2(\Omega_{m0}-1)}(1-\cos\eta)\), \(H_0t(\eta)=\tfrac{\Omega_{m0}}{2(\Omega_{m0}-1)^{3/2}}(\eta-\sin\eta)\), parametrized by the development angle \(\eta\in[0,2\pi]\) (\(\eta=\pi\) is turnaround). Derive this yourself from \(E(a)^2=\Omega_{m0}a^{-3}+(1-\Omega_{m0})a^{-2}\), or look up the standard derivation, and implement it (a short script, or even a spreadsheet) to compute \(a_{\rm turn}\) and \(t_{\rm turn}\) for \(\Omega_{m0}=2\) directly from these closed-form expressions, with no numerical integration at all. Compare against this program's python main.py --mode evolve --omega_m 2 --omega_r 0 --omega_de 0 output. Then repeat EXP-17c's experiment using this exact solution as the reference instead of the program's own first run: place --a_max and --t_max progressively closer to the exact \(a_{\rm turn}\)/\(t_{\rm turn}\) (say, at 90%, 99%, 99.9%, and 99.99% of the exact value) and track how the program's reported stopping time and scale factor converge to the exact cycloid values as the boundary approaches the turnaround. This is the most demanding test of the event-ordering and turnaround machinery in the program, because it checks it against an independent solution with no integration error of its own, not just against the program's own self-consistency.

For a supported constant-\(w\) Big Rip model, a dashed curve can continue beyond the numerical endpoint toward a dotted red finite-time limit. This display continuation adds no CSV samples and is disabled by any explicit --t_max.
evolve — \(H(t)\)\(H\) falls by many orders of magnitude from the radiation era to today. Its future behavior depends on which dark-energy case you ran: it flattens toward a constant only for an exact cosmological constant / de Sitter domination (\(w_0=-1\), \(w_a=0\)); for constant \(w_0>-1\) it instead falls toward zero; for phantom (\(w_0<-1\)) it grows without bound toward the Big Rip; for evolving CPL (\(w_a\neq0\)) the future trend can do any of these depending on the parameters. Matter-only (no dark energy) models keep falling.
evolve — \(\Omega_i(a)\)For the default/concordance-like model: three eras in sequence — radiation, then matter, then dark energy — with the sum-check line at 1 at every regular row (NaN at an exact turnaround, where every \(\Omega_i\) formally diverges). A pure-radiation, Milne, matter-only, negative-\(\Omega_{\rm DE}\), or recollapsing model will NOT show this three-era sequence; what you see depends on the energy content you actually ran. Every detected matter–dark-energy equality crossing is marked; an evolving dark-energy model can have more than one. Separately, matter–radiation equality occurs at \(a_{\rm eq}=\Omega_{r0}/\Omega_{m0}\).
evolve — \(q(t)\)Starts near \(+1\) (radiation domination), settles near \(+1/2\) (matter domination), crosses zero at the acceleration transition, and approaches \(-1\) if a cosmological constant eventually dominates. For a recollapsing model, \(q\) diverges as the turnaround is approached, since \(H\to0\) while the densities stay finite.
compare — \(a(t)\) overlayEach cosmology's history, with a dot marking where it crosses \(a=1\); reading the dots' horizontal positions directly compares their ages.
compare — \(q(t)\) overlayThe deceleration axis is deliberately clipped (\(-1.6\) to \(3.0\)): a recollapsing model's \(q\) diverges near its turnaround and would otherwise swamp the comparison, which is the point of the panel.
compare — age bar chart A ranking of finite ages. A cross at the baseline with a reason label replaces an unavailable age: a past-eternal model has no finite age, while a run ending before \(a=1\) has not reached today. The cross does not represent an age of zero.
compare — \(\Omega_{\rm DE}(a)\) overlayModels with no dark energy sit at zero throughout; the phantom and evolving_de presets separate visibly from a cosmological constant only close to and after \(a=1\), which is exactly why distinguishing them observationally is hard.
age — age vs. parameterThe classic "age crisis" plot: age falls monotonically as \(\Omega_{m0}\) rises (flat case), crossing the reference globular-cluster age near \(\Omega_{m0}\approx0.3\)-\(0.4\) — which is close to, but not identical to, the concordance value, and figuring out why is a good exercise in itself.
age — \(H_0t_0\) vs. parameterThe dimensionless version of the same plot, with reference lines at the Einstein-de Sitter value \(2/3\) and the Milne value \(1\).
Run summary — evolve

The terminal reports why the returned trajectory ended under "Run stopped because". When the model's fate has been classified, a separate "Model fate" line reports it. The corresponding machine-readable fields are stop_reason and fate_status in the summary and CSV metadata. A "Notes on this run" block appears whenever the model has been pushed outside typical bounds (extreme curvature, a decelerating "dark energy," an unresolved early-time offset, a t_max stop before reaching \(a=1\), an impending recollapse or Big Rip). For a past_status="past_eternal" model, the "Age today" line explicitly says no finite age exists rather than printing a number, and any additional matter-dark-energy-equality or acceleration-transition crossings beyond the first are listed alongside the primary one. Read it.

Run summary — compareA single compact table, one row per cosmology: age (or an explicit past-eternal marker in place of a number), \(H_0t_0\), \(q_0\), the acceleration-transition redshift, and a short fate note ("recollapses", "Big Rip ahead", "future recollapse", "past-eternal (no finite age)", or "fate unresolved") where applicable -- not the full evolve-style block, since it would be repeated once per cosmology. The compare-mode CSV carries the full machine-readable detail behind that note in its own columns (fate_status, past_status, elapsed_ai_to_today_gyr, a_turn, t_turn_gyr, total_lifetime_gyr, big_rip_gyr, big_rip_remaining_gyr, future_turnaround_a, fate_search_limit_a, and stop_reason) rather than only the human-readable note.
Run summary — ageScan extrema (oldest/youngest age and where they occur) and a count of how many scan points recollapse within the finite \(a=5\) look-ahead horizon, plus a separate count of how many scanned points are past-eternal (no finite age to include in the extrema at all) -- a single summary of the whole scan, not a per-point evolve-style block.
CSV files With --csvdir PATH, evolve mode writes trajectory samples with columns t_Gyr, a, z, H_km_s_Mpc, Omega_m, Omega_r, Omega_k, Omega_DE, q, w_DE. The commented header records provenance, parameters and summary results. If more than one matter–dark-energy equality or acceleration-transition crossing is found, that header also records a numbered list of the crossings. These are metadata fields, not extra table columns. Compare mode writes an age-comparison table and a long-format table of the cosmologies' curves.
PNG fileWith --outdir PATH: a timestamped copy of the figure, saved in addition to the on-screen display. Use --no_plot --csvdir PATH for a data-only sweep.

Model limits

This is a homogeneous-and-isotropic background calculation — it has no perturbations, no structure formation, no photons decoupling to make a cosmic microwave background, no primordial nucleosynthesis chemistry, and no inflationary epoch. \(a\to0\) is treated as a mathematical limit of the classical Friedmann equation, not a claim about what actually happened at the Big Bang, where quantum gravity is expected to take over. Use the program to build intuition for how the geometry and material content of an FLRW spacetime control its expansion history and age — not as a cosmological-parameter-fitting pipeline.
