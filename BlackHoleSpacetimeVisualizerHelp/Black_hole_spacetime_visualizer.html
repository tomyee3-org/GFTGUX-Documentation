Description

Black_hole_spacetime_visualizer takes the single most striking prediction of general relativity — the black hole — and gives a student four different, quantitative ways to look at it. One program covers four calculations, selected with --mode: embed draws the embedding diagram of the curved spatial geometry around the hole; tidal computes the stretching and compressing tidal acceleration a body feels at a given radius, and compares that across black-hole masses; infall follows a test particle dropped from rest all the way to the horizon, in both its own time and a distant observer's; and horizons makes visible a distinction that is usually only stated in words — the difference between the local, instantaneous apparent horizon and the global, teleological event horizon of a black hole that is actively gaining mass.

The default run (--mode embed) draws Flamm's paraboloid for a ten solar-mass black hole: a Schwarzschild radius of 29.5 km, and a throat with a vertical tangent exactly at the horizon. Switching to tidal for the same hole gives a radial tidal acceleration of 1.9×10⁷ g at the horizon — solidly lethal — while the supermassive black hole at the centre of the Milky Way, Sgr A* at 4.31×10⁶ solar masses, manages only about 10⁻⁴ g there: gentle enough that, by this criterion alone, you would not notice crossing its horizon. Switching to infall, the same ten solar-mass hole shows a particle released from rest at six Schwarzschild radii crossing the horizon after 2.26 milliseconds of its own proper time, while the Schwarzschild coordinate time used by a distant observer is already diverging logarithmically at that point and never catches up. And horizons, for a hole doubling its mass by accretion, shows the event horizon rising measurably before the accretion begins — it has to anticipate mass that has not arrived yet — while the apparent horizon only starts moving once the infalling matter is actually there.

What this program is, and what it is not. Every number in this program is an exact consequence of the Schwarzschild geometry, or, for horizons, its simplest time-dependent generalisation, the ingoing Vaidya metric. There is no equation of state, no fitted closure, and no calibration to real astrophysical data anywhere in this module — unlike the stellar-structure programs in this suite. What is idealised is the physical scenario: point test particles and test rods with no mass or extent of their own, purely radial motion with zero angular momentum, and, in horizons, a mass function chosen by the student rather than one derived from a real accretion flow. No rotation (a real astrophysical black hole is a Kerr hole, not a Schwarzschild one), no radiation reaction, and no back-reaction of the infalling matter on the geometry beyond the prescribed mass function.
What is exact and what is numerical: a teal EXACT tag marks a formula that is an exact closed-form consequence of the metric, a green ODE tag marks a quantity obtained by numerically integrating a stated differential equation, and a blue DERIVED tag marks an algebraic result derived from the exact formulae. There are no fitted or calibrated quantities anywhere in this module — a deliberate contrast with the stellar-structure programs, worth pointing out explicitly the first time a student meets both.

Physical Background

Curved Space You Can Draw

The full Schwarzschild spacetime is four-dimensional and cannot be embedded in ordinary 3-D space at all. But a single instant of it — the spatial geometry alone, at fixed Schwarzschild time t, restricted to the equatorial plane — is a 2-D curved surface, and that can be embedded exactly in flat 3-D Euclidean space. The resulting picture, Flamm's paraboloid, is not a metaphor or an artist's impression: every distance measured along the drawn surface equals the corresponding proper distance computed from the Schwarzschild metric. It is the one genuinely quantitative "rubber sheet" picture of a black hole, and it is worth being precise about what it does and does not depict: it says nothing about time, nothing about gravity as a force, and the popular image of a ball rolling toward the centre to represent gravitational attraction is not part of what the embedding actually shows.

Why Tidal Forces Are the Honest Danger

Crossing a black hole's event horizon is not, by itself, locally violent: for a large enough hole nothing dramatic happens right at the horizon. What can kill you is the tidal force — the difference in gravitational acceleration between your head and your feet — and that difference depends on the size of the hole in a way most people find surprising. Because the Schwarzschild radius grows linearly with mass while the tidal acceleration at that radius falls as the inverse square of the mass, a bigger black hole is gentler at its horizon, not fiercer. This program lets a student verify that scaling directly rather than take it on faith.

Two Clocks, One Infall

A particle falling into a Schwarzschild black hole experiences nothing pathological at the horizon: its proper time runs smoothly and it crosses in a finite, ordinary interval. A distant observer watching it fall sees something entirely different. The Schwarzschild t coordinate — which is also the time kept by a clock very far from the hole — takes longer and longer to advance the closer the particle gets to the horizon, formally diverging as the particle approaches it, so that a distant observer never actually sees the crossing happen. The two clocks are not in conflict: they are simply measuring different things, one the particle's own elapsed time, the other a coordinate defined by, and only meaningful to, observers who stay far away. Accompanying the coordinate-time divergence is a genuine, observable effect: light sent outward by the falling particle is progressively redshifted and, in the strict mathematical limit, never quite finishes arriving — which is the precise sense in which an object falling into a black hole is said to "freeze and fade" as seen from outside, even though nothing freezes from the particle's own point of view.

Apparent Horizon and Event Horizon: Local vs. Global

In an unchanging (static) black hole spacetime, "the horizon" is unambiguous, and the two definitions in this section's title agree exactly. Once the hole is allowed to change — by swallowing matter, or, in principle, by evaporating — they come apart, and the distinction is one of the more important ideas in modern black-hole physics.

The apparent horizon is defined locally, at a single instant: it is the outermost surface where outward-pointing light rays instantaneously fail to increase their radius. You can find it from the geometry at that moment alone, with no knowledge of the future. The event horizon is defined globally: it is the boundary of the set of events from which a light ray can never escape to arbitrarily large distance, no matter how long you wait. Answering "can it ever escape" requires knowing the entire future of the spacetime — which is exactly why the event horizon is called teleological, and why, in a dynamical spacetime, it can begin moving in response to infalling matter that has not arrived yet. This program makes that abstract statement into a picture: for a black hole that gains mass over some interval of time, the located event horizon is demonstrably larger than the apparent horizon throughout the accretion, and it starts rising perceptibly before the accretion begins.

Governing Equations

The Schwarzschild Metric

Schwarzschild line element EXACT
\[ds^2 = -\left(1-\frac{r_s}{r}\right)c^2dt^2 + \frac{dr^2}{1-r_s/r} + r^2\left(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right), \qquad r_s = \frac{2GM}{c^2}.\]

Every formula in this program follows from this line element (or, for horizons, from its ingoing-null-coordinate generalisation below), together with the geodesic equation for a freely falling test particle or photon. Nothing here is fitted to data or calibrated against a more detailed model.

Mode embed: Flamm's Paraboloid

Restrict the metric to \(t=\text{const}\), \(\theta=\pi/2\): \(ds^2 = dr^2/(1-r_s/r) + r^2 d\phi^2\). Embed this as a surface of revolution \(z(r)\) in flat 3-D space, where \(ds^2=dz^2+dr^2+r^2d\phi^2\):

Embedding profile DERIVED
\[\left(\frac{dz}{dr}\right)^2 = \frac{r_s}{r-r_s} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad z(r) = 2\sqrt{r_s(r-r_s)}, \qquad r\ge r_s.\]

The derivative diverges as \(r\to r_s\): the throat has a vertical tangent, a real geometric feature, not a numerical artefact. The embedding is undefined for \(r

Mode tidal: Geodesic Deviation

Two nearby free-falling test particles separated by proper distance \(\delta\ell\) accelerate relative to each other according to the geodesic deviation equation, governed by the Riemann tensor. For Schwarzschild, in the local orthonormal frame of an observer at radius \(r\) (static or radially infalling — the radial components turn out to be the same for both):

Tidal acceleration EXACT
\[a_{\rm radial} = \frac{2GM}{r^3}\,\delta r \quad(\text{stretching: the two ends pull apart}), \qquad a_{\rm tangential} = \frac{GM}{r^3}\,\delta\ell \quad(\text{compressing: the two ends push together, each transverse direction}).\]

Both quantities are quoted, printed, plotted and written to CSV as magnitudes; the physical sense of each — one stretching the body along the radial line, the other squeezing it in the two transverse directions — is carried by the words "radial/stretching" and "tangential/compressing," not by a sign on the number. These are exactly the tidal-acceleration formulae of Newtonian gravity for a point mass \(M\) — general relativity adds no correction to the radial or tangential tidal terms of Schwarzschild at any order in this frame. What differs from Newtonian intuition is only where you are allowed to evaluate them: general relativity insists the formula holds however close to \(r_s\) you go, all the way to (and through) the horizon.

Illustrative survival radius DERIVED
\[r_{\rm crit} = \left(\frac{2GM\,\delta\ell}{a_{\rm limit}}\right)^{1/3}, \qquad a_{\rm limit} = (\text{limit\_g})\times g.\]

Solving \(a_{\rm radial}=a_{\rm limit}\) for \(r\) gives the radius at which a body of length \(\delta\ell\) first experiences a chosen illustrative tidal-acceleration threshold. Comparing \(r_{\rm crit}\) with \(r_s\) tells you whether that threshold is reached outside the horizon (the body is destroyed before crossing) or only inside it (the body crosses intact, by this criterion). --limit_g is a deliberately round illustrative number, not a precise physiological claim, and one of the exercises asks what changes if you adopt a different one.

Mode infall: Radial Free Fall

For a particle released from rest at \(r_0\), the conserved specific energy is \(E=\sqrt{1-r_s/r_0}\) (E \(\to 1\) as \(r_0\to\infty\)). The exact radial equations of motion are:

Radial geodesic equations EXACT, integrated ODE
\[\left(\frac{dr}{d\tau}\right)^2 = c^2\left(\frac{r_s}{r}-\frac{r_s}{r_0}\right), \qquad \frac{dt}{d\tau} = \frac{E}{1-r_s/r}.\]

\(dr/d\tau\) is finite and smooth all the way through \(r=r_s\): nothing pathological happens to the particle there. \(dt/d\tau\) diverges as \(r\to r_s\): this is what makes the distant observer's coordinate-time coordinate run away. Both are integrated numerically with RK4 in the particle's own proper time \(\tau\), with the step size capped at a fraction of the remaining distance to the stopping radius and at a fraction of the distance already fallen from \(r_0\) — exactly as the structure integrators of the other GFtGU programs cap their step by a fraction of the current integration variable. The second cap matters here specifically: as a function of \(r\), \(dr/d\tau\) behaves like \(-\text{const}\times\sqrt{r_0-r}\) close to release (the particle starts from rest, so its speed grows like the square root of the distance fallen), an infinite-slope branch point that would otherwise quietly knock RK4's accuracy down from fourth order to first order for the whole run, no matter how small --step_frac is set.

Local speed and redshift DERIVED
\[v_{\rm local}(r) = \frac{1}{E}\sqrt{\frac{r_s}{r}-\frac{r_s}{r_0}}\ \longrightarrow\ 1 \text{ as } r\to r_s, \] \[\frac{\nu_{\rm obs}}{\nu_{\rm emit}} = \sqrt{1-\frac{r_s}{r}}\ \sqrt{\frac{1-v_{\rm local}}{1+v_{\rm local}}}\ \longrightarrow\ 0 \text{ as } r\to r_s.\]

The redshift factor is the product of the ordinary gravitational redshift for a static source at \(r\) and the special-relativistic Doppler factor for that source's local motion relative to a static observer it is passing. For a particle released from rest at infinity (\(E=1\)) this collapses to the well-known closed form \(\nu_{\rm obs}/\nu_{\rm emit}=1-\sqrt{r_s/r}\); Exercise EXP-7 asks you to verify that limit directly from the program's output.

Mode horizons: the Ingoing Vaidya Metric

To model a hole that gains mass, replace the constant \(M\) in the Schwarzschild metric with a prescribed, non-decreasing function \(M(v)\) of the ingoing null (advanced-time) coordinate \(v\):

Ingoing Vaidya line element EXACT (as a solution of the Einstein field equations for infalling null dust)
\[ds^2 = -\left(1-\frac{2M(v)}{r}\right)dv^2 + 2\,dv\,dr + r^2d\Omega^2 \qquad (G=c=1 \text{ in this line}).\]

This program uses a mass function that ramps linearly from \(M_0\) to \(M_1\) between advanced times \(v_1\) and \(v_2=v_1+\)--duration_rs0, and is constant before and after — a simple, explicit, student-chosen stand-in for an accretion episode, not a result of an accretion-physics calculation.

Apparent horizon DERIVED (algebraic, from the mass function alone)
\[r_{AH}(v) = 2M(v).\]
Outgoing radial null geodesics EXACT, integrated ODE
\[\frac{dr}{dv} = \frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{2M(v)}{r}\right).\]

The event horizon is the one outgoing null geodesic that neither escapes to \(r\to\infty\) nor falls to \(r=0\): it is located by firing trial geodesics from deep in the static region before \(v_1\) (where the true horizon generator sits exponentially close to \(r=2M_0\)) and bisecting the initial radius until the trajectory neither clearly escapes nor clearly plunges by a late time \(v_{\rm end}\). "Escapes" and "plunges" are themselves finite-time, finite-radius numerical verdicts — a trial geodesic is called plunged once \(r\) drops to a small floor, and unambiguously escaped once \(r\) grows past a large ceiling, both comfortably outside the region that matters physically but neither literally \(r\to 0\) or \(r\to\infty\) — not a compromise specific to this program, since no computation can literally run to infinite \(r\) or infinite \(v\) either. See the algorithm section for the numerical details, for exactly what "exponentially close" costs you in practice, and for a note on an alternative, non-shooting way to construct the same event horizon.

Algorithm

Mode embed

  1. Validate the mass and the outer radius (must exceed \(r_s\)); compute \(r_s\) and evaluate \(z(r)=2\sqrt{r_s(r-r_s)}\) on a linear grid from \(r_s\) to --r_max_rs\(\times r_s\).
  2. Also evaluate the proper radial distance \(\int_{r_s}^r dr'/\sqrt{1-r_s/r'}\) by a singularity-removing substitution \(r'=r_s+u^2\), for the exercises that compare it with the naive coordinate difference \(r-r_s\).
  3. Hand the profile to the plotting layer, which also constructs the 3-D surface of revolution for display.

Mode tidal

  1. Validate the mass, the innermost radius (must exceed \(r_s\)) and the test-rod separation; evaluate the exact tidal-acceleration formulae on a logarithmic radial grid.
  2. If --compare_masses is supplied, repeat the horizon-value calculation and the survival-radius inversion for each mass in the list and tabulate the results.

Mode infall

  1. Validate the mass, the release radius \(r_0\) (must exceed \(r_s\)) and the stopping radius \(r_{\rm stop}\) (must lie strictly between \(r_s\) and \(r_0\)); compute \(E=\sqrt{1-r_s/r_0}\).
  2. Start the integration an infinitesimal distance inside \(r_0\): \(r_0\) itself is an exact fixed point of \(dr/d\tau\) (the particle is released from rest there), so beginning exactly at \(r_0\) would never move.
  3. Advance \(r\) and \(t\) together with RK4 in \(\tau\), with the step \(d\tau\) capped at a fraction (--step_frac) of the remaining proper-time-equivalent distance to \(r_{\rm stop}\) and of the distance already fallen from \(r_0\), so the step shrinks geometrically near both ends of the track — near \(r_{\rm stop}\) because \(t\) and the redshift vary rapidly there, and near \(r_0\) because \(dr/d\tau\) has an infinite slope in \(r\) at the release point, which would otherwise silently degrade the integrator's accuracy. Interpolate the final partial step so the recorded track ends exactly at \(r_{\rm stop}\).
  4. At each recorded point, evaluate the local speed and the outward redshift factor from the closed-form expressions above.
  5. The integration deliberately stops just outside the horizon, at \(r_{\rm stop}=\)--r_stop_rs\(\times r_s\) (default 1.0005), because the Schwarzschild \(t\) coordinate used for the distant observer's clock diverges there; the particle's own \(\tau\) does not, and nothing in the underlying physics is singular at the horizon itself — only this particular coordinate choice is.

Mode horizons

  1. Validate \(M_0\), \(M_1\) (must satisfy \(M_1\ge M_0\): this Vaidya mass function models only accretion, never mass loss), and the accretion-window and margin parameters. Build the piecewise-linear mass function \(M(v)\) and the corresponding apparent horizon \(r_{AH}(v)=2M(v)\) directly.
  2. Integrate a trial outgoing null geodesic \(dr/dv=\tfrac12(1-2M(v)/r)\) with RK4, from a chosen starting radius at \(v_{\rm start}=v_1-\)--v_start_margin_rs0\(\times r_{s0}\), out to \(v_{\rm end}=v_2+\)--v_end_margin_rs0\(\times r_{s0}\). The step in \(v\) is adaptive, capped at a fraction of the current radius: a fixed step overshoots straight through \(r=0\) as the derivative diverges there, which makes a plunging geodesic appear, spuriously, to bounce back out and escape. A trial is classified numerically, on one of three explicit finite criteria: it is called plunged the moment \(r\) first drops to or below a small floor (\(10^{-4}\) of \(m_0+m_1\)), called unambiguously escaped the moment \(r\) first exceeds a large ceiling (\(10^4\) of \(m_0+m_1\)), and otherwise — if it simply reaches \(v_{\rm end}\) without doing either — classified by comparing its radius there against \(r_{s1}\). All three are numerical stand-ins for the literal \(r\to 0\) and \(r\to\infty\) of the true definition, chosen far enough from the horizon not to matter physically for any reasonable mass.
  3. Bracket the starting radius between a value that clearly falls in and one that clearly escapes, starting near \([0.5,\,1.5]\times r_{s0}\) and, if that does not already bracket the answer, widening geometrically. A fixed bracket near \(r_{s0}\) is not always enough: for a large mass ratio \(M_1/M_0\) the true critical radius at \(v_{\rm start}\) sits well above \(r_{s0}\) — teleologically, the horizon has to have "grown into" most of its eventual size early to end up tangent to \(r_{s1}\) once accretion finishes — so the search widens until it succeeds before bisection begins. Bisect for --bisect_iters iterations (default 60, i.e. a bracket narrowed by a factor of order \(2^{-60}\)). The midpoint of the final bracket is the located event-horizon generator's starting radius; integrating it forward gives \(r_{EH}(v)\).
  4. Two distinct sources of error attach to the located horizon, and it is worth not conflating them. The bisection bracket width — printed as residual_rs0 — measures only how many times that bracket was halved; it is driven to floating-point noise by the default 60 iterations and says nothing about how close the bracket sits to the true answer, and increasing --bisect_iters further buys essentially nothing once each trial geodesic's own RK4 integration error, and eventually double-precision round-off itself, dominates. Separately, because the true event-horizon generator only sits exactly at \(r=2M_0\) in the limit \(v_{\rm start}\to-\infty\), starting the shooting method at any finite \(v_{\rm start}\) leaves the located horizon systematically offset from the true one near \(v_{\rm start}\) — a finite-\(v_{\rm start}\) truncation error, generally much larger than the bisection residual, that decays (empirically, exponentially) as \(v_{\rm start}\) is moved earlier rather than as --bisect_iters is increased. This offset is not merely a numerical inconvenience: \(r_{\rm crit}/r_{s0}-1\) printed in the summary is the physical (finite-\(v_{\rm start}\)) separation between the located event horizon and the static horizon \(r=2M_0\) — a genuine measurement of how far in advance the horizon "knows" accretion is coming, limited only by how early you choose to start looking. Both quantities are genuine, controllable features of the calculation, not embarrassments; Exercise EXP-10 asks you to demonstrate the second one directly and connect it to the exponential instability of a geodesic sitting near an unstable fixed point.
  5. Finally, integrate a small family of geodesics with starting radii spread around the located critical radius, for the panel that shows directly which ones escape and which plunge.
Design note: shooting vs. backward integration. The event horizon can also be constructed by integrating a single outgoing null geodesic backward in \(v\) from the exactly known boundary condition \(r=2M_1\) at \(v=v_2\) (once the mass stops growing, the event and apparent horizons coincide there), rather than forward from a guessed starting radius before \(v_1\). Backward integration has no bracket to search for and no finite-\(v_{\rm start}\) truncation error at all — arguably the numerically cleaner construction. It is not what this program does, by choice: forward shooting turns the event horizon into something you can watch happen — the separatrix between a family of light rays that escape and a family that plunge, which is exactly the picture in the "how the shooting method finds it" panel and the basis of Exercises EXP-10 and EXP-13 — and that visualisation is judged to have the greater tutorial value for a first encounter with a teleological horizon. Both constructions are legitimate; this is a pedagogical choice, not a numerical necessity, and an instructor is welcome to prefer the other.
Input and runtime safeguards: masses, radii and separations must be finite and positive; embedding and tidal radii must exceed \(r_s\); an infall release radius must exceed \(r_s\) and the stopping radius must lie strictly between \(r_s\) and the release radius; --step_frac must lie in \((10^{-5}, 0.2]\); a Vaidya run requires \(M_1\ge M_0\); the null-geodesic integrator caps its own step size against the current radius specifically to prevent the horizon-shooting bisection from being corrupted by an overshoot instability; and --no_plot is refused unless a CSV directory is given, so a run always produces something. Masses far outside the observed black-hole mass range are still computed exactly — general relativity supplies no natural mass scale — but are flagged, since the point of running one is usually precisely to see the numbers become extreme.

Module Overview

Physics Layer
physics_bh.py

Constants, the Schwarzschild radius, Flamm's paraboloid, the exact tidal-acceleration formulae and survival-radius inversion, the radial-infall RK4 integrator with its local-speed and redshift formulae, the Vaidya mass function, the adaptive-step outgoing-null-geodesic integrator, and the bisection shooting method that locates the event horizon. All validation of physical inputs lives here.

Driver Layer
driver_bh.py

Validates presentation-level inputs, parses the comma-separated comparison-mass list, dispatches on --mode, prints the run summary, writes timestamped CSV files (including secondary files for the tidal cross-mass comparison and the horizon geodesic family), and calls the plotter.

Plot Layer
plot_bh.py

Four figure layouts, one per mode — including a 3-D surface of revolution for the embedding diagram and a two-colour escapes/plunges geodesic-family panel for the horizons mode — with shared save-or-display logic.

Entry Point
main.py

Defines the student-facing command-line options, grouped by mode, and converts validation or runtime failures into concise command-line error messages.

Parameters

Run python main.py --help for the current command summary, and python main.py --version for the model version, which is also written into every CSV header. Options that do not apply to the selected mode are ignored silently — --mode embed --r0_rs 6 will not change anything — so check the printed summary, or the parameter block at the top of the CSV file, to confirm which values the run actually used.

--n_steps6000—Output-grid resolution in advanced time, for the plot and the CSV file (the integration itself is adaptively stepped, not fixed to this count).
--bisect_iters60—Bisection iterations used to locate the event horizon.
--n_family9—Number of nearby geodesics drawn around the located event-horizon generator, for the "how the shooting method finds it" panel.

Output and Plot Panels

OutputWhat to Look For
embed — radial profileA vertical tangent exactly at \(r=r_s\): the throat. The dashed lower branch is a plotting convenience (a mirror image), not a second physical sheet — the surface does not continue anywhere the way a wormhole picture might suggest.
embed — 3-D surfaceThe same profile revolved about the vertical axis. Notice how quickly the curvature (the bend of the surface) flattens out beyond a few \(r_s\): far from the hole, space is nearly flat, exactly as it must be.
tidal — acceleration vs. radiusBoth curves fall off as \(1/r^3\): a factor of 2 in radius is a factor of 8 in tidal force. The radial (stretching) curve is always exactly twice the tangential (compressing) one.
tidal — cross-mass panelsThe tidal acceleration at the horizon falls as \(M^{-2}\): a straight line of slope \(-2\) on log–log axes. The survival-radius panel shows directly, for each mass, whether the illustrative threshold is reached outside the horizon (destroyed before crossing) or only inside it.
infall — r(τ)A smooth curve reaching the stopping radius (default \(r_{\rm stop}=1.0005\,r_s\), just outside the horizon) at a finite, unremarkable proper time. The run stops there deliberately — see the algorithm section — and does not itself compute or plot the moment of crossing \(r_s\); nothing about the curve's shape signals anything eventful happening from the particle's own point of view.
infall — t(τ)Pulls away from the \(t=\tau\) reference line and would formally diverge as \(\tau\) approaches the (finite) horizon-crossing proper time; the plotted curve only reaches the point where the integration was deliberately stopped, just outside the horizon.
infall — local speedClimbs toward \(v=c\) as \(r\to r_s\), for every release radius \(r_0\) — even a particle dropped from just above the horizon is measured, by the local static observers it passes, to be moving at very nearly the speed of light there.
infall — redshiftFalls toward zero (log scale) as \(r\to r_s\): a distant observer receives light from the infall ever more redshifted and ever more delayed, which is the precise, quantitative version of "you never see it fall in." The plotted quantity is the frequency ratio \(\nu_{\rm obs}/\nu_{\rm emit}\) itself; each redshifted photon also carries less energy, so a real detector would additionally see the source fade, but that inference depends on the emission and detection model and is not separately computed here.
horizons — apparent vs. eventWhen \(M_1>M_0\): the event horizon lies at or above the apparent horizon everywhere, coincides with it once the mass has stopped changing, and — the most important feature of the whole mode — begins rising measurably before the shaded accretion window starts. When \(M_0=M_1\) (no accretion supplied), there is nothing to anticipate: the two horizons coincide at \(r_{s0}\) for the entire run, and the summary and plot say so directly rather than describing a rise that is not there.
horizons — geodesic familyGreen trajectories (starting just outside the located event-horizon generator) escape to large radius; red ones (starting just inside it) plunge to \(r=0\). The event-horizon curve is literally the boundary between the two families.
Run summaryPrinted to the terminal for every mode: inputs, derived quantities, and the key results. A notes on this run block appears whenever a mass lies outside the astrophysically known range, or, for horizons, always — to report the bisection bracket width explicitly (a separate figure from the finite-\(v_{\rm start}\) truncation error; see the algorithm section). Read it.
CSV filesWith --csvdir PATH: the full numerical table for the run. The commented header records the model version, the run time, and every parameter the selected mode actually used. tidal additionally writes a comparison-table CSV when --compare_masses is given, and horizons additionally writes the geodesic-family CSV.

Suggested Experiments

The exercises are divided into a core sequence, arranged from reading the output through controlled comparisons to numerical work, and a set of optional projects that ask for a somewhat larger investigation. Two synthesis exercises close the set. Nothing here needs anything beyond the program, a spreadsheet or short script, and the printed summary.

Core sequence · introductory

EXP-1 · introductory

Read the Throat

Run python main.py --mode embed --M 10. Identify the throat in both panels. Why does the profile have a vertical tangent exactly at \(r=r_s\), and why is that not a numerical mistake? The dashed lower branch is a mirror image drawn for visual symmetry only — state, in your own words, what would and would not be true if you instead interpreted it as a tunnel through to "the other side."

EXP-2 · introductory

How Gentle Is the Tide?

Run python main.py --mode tidal --M 10 --compare_masses 10,4.31e6. Record the radial tidal acceleration at the horizon, in g, for both masses. 4.31×10⁶ solar masses is (very nearly) the mass of Sgr A*, the black hole at the centre of our galaxy. For which of the two does the program's illustrative 100 g threshold get reached only inside the horizon (by this criterion, crossed intact) rather than outside it (destroyed first)? Which scales as you might have naively guessed — bigger hole, stronger tide, or the reverse — and why?

EXP-3 · introductory

Falling In

Run python main.py --mode infall --M 10 --r0_rs 6. Record the proper time and the Schwarzschild coordinate time at which the integration stops, just outside the horizon. Which one is "how long it took," from the falling observer's own point of view? At roughly what value of \(r/r_s\) does the local infall speed first exceed half the speed of light?

EXP-4 · introductory

Two Kinds of Horizon

Run python main.py --mode horizons --M0 5 --M1 10. In your own words, state the difference between how the apparent horizon and the event horizon are each defined — one sentence for each, using the words "instantaneous" and "future" somewhere. At what value of \(v/r_{s0}\) do the two curves become indistinguishable in the left panel, and how does that compare with the end of the shaded accretion window?

Core sequence · introductory to intermediate

EXP-5 · introductory/intermediate

Coordinate Distance Is Not Proper Distance

Using --mode embed --csvdir ./data, compare the proper_radial_distance_m column with the naive coordinate difference \(r-r_s\) at a few radii out to 8 \(r_s\). By what percentage do they differ at \(r=2r_s\)? At \(r=8r_s\)? State, in one sentence, why the two are not equal even though both are "distance from the horizon."

EXP-6 · introductory/intermediate

Where Is the Chosen Tidal Threshold?

Run --mode tidal --compare_masses 3,10,100,1e4,1e6,4.31e6,1e9 and study the fourth panel. Find, by trial and error or by inverting the \(M^{-2}\) scaling algebraically, the approximate mass at which \(r_{\rm crit}=r_s\) exactly for the default 1.8 m separation and 100 g limit. Then repeat with --limit_g 10 and again with --limit_g 1000: how much does the crossover mass move, and why does an order-of-magnitude change in your assumed survival threshold move it by so much less than an order of magnitude?

EXP-7 · introductory/intermediate

The Rain-Frame Limit

Run --mode infall --M 10 --r0_rs 1000 --csvdir ./data (a release radius large enough to approximate "from rest at infinity"). From the CSV, compare the local speed at a few radii with the closed-form \(v=\sqrt{r_s/r}\), and the redshift factor with the closed form \(1-\sqrt{r_s/r}\) quoted in the equations section. How small is the discrepancy, and why does it shrink as --r0_rs grows?

Core sequence · intermediate

EXP-8 · intermediate

Verify the \(M^{-2}\) Scaling

Run --mode tidal --compare_masses 1,3,10,30,100,300,1000 and, from the CSV comparison table, fit \(\log(a_{\rm radial,\ horizon})\) against \(\log M\). Confirm the slope is \(-2\) to within your fitting uncertainty, and derive that exponent yourself algebraically from \(a_{\rm radial}=2GM\delta r/r^3\) together with \(r_s\propto M\).

EXP-9 · intermediate

Compressing and Stretching Are Locked Together

Using the tidal CSV for any mass, verify numerically that a_tangential is exactly half of a_radial at every radius. Prove algebraically, from the two formulae in the equations section, that this ratio holds at every radius for every mass, not just as an approximation. What property of the Schwarzschild Riemann tensor is responsible — specifically, why must the sum of all three principal tidal accelerations (one radial, two tangential) vanish for vacuum, and how does that alone force the factor of 2?

EXP-10 · intermediate

The Event Horizon's Memory

Run --mode horizons --M0 5 --M1 10 --v_start_margin_rs0 5, then repeat with 10, 15, 20 and 30, holding --bisect_iters 60 fixed each time, and record r_crit_over_rs0 and residual_rs0 from the printed summary. Plot \(\log(r_{\rm crit}/r_{s0}-1)\) against --v_start_margin_rs0. Is the decay consistent with an exponential? Then check residual_rs0 across the same five runs: it should barely move at all, because it tracks only the bisection bracket width, which depends on --bisect_iters (held fixed here), not on --v_start_margin_rs0. The quantity that actually moves, \(r_{\rm crit}/r_{s0}\), is the numerical face of the event horizon's teleological character: the earlier you have to "know the answer" (start the shooting method), the more precisely you must aim.

EXP-11 · intermediate

Checking the Infall Integration Against a Closed Form

The radial free-fall equation \((dr/d\tau)^2=c^2(r_s/r-r_s/r_0)\) happens to have an exact closed-form solution, the same "cycloid" parametrisation used for Newtonian radial (zero-angular-momentum) Kepler infall: with \(\eta\) running from 0 (at \(r=r_0\)) to \(\eta_{\rm stop}=\arccos(2r_{\rm stop}/r_0-1)\), \[r(\eta)=\tfrac{r_0}{2}(1+\cos\eta), \qquad \tau(\eta)=\sqrt{\tfrac{r_0^3}{8GM}}\,(\eta+\sin\eta).\] Run --mode infall --M 10 --r0_rs 6 (default --r_stop_rs 1.0005) and compare the printed tau_total_ms with \(\tau(\eta_{\rm stop})\) from this formula. You should find agreement to better than one part in \(10^5\) even at the coarsest allowed --step_frac 0.2. Now repeat with --step_frac at 0.1, 0.05, 0.02 and 0.01: does the agreement improve much further, or does it plateau? Explain the plateau in terms of the two-sided geometric step refinement described in the algorithm section — refining near \(r_0\) as well as near \(r_{\rm stop}\) is what makes a deliberately coarse --step_frac already highly accurate here, unlike a naive fixed-step RK4 applied to the same equation (which you can verify separately: a fixed step of comparable size, with no refinement near \(r_0\), reproduces the total proper time to only about two significant figures).

Core sequence · intermediate to advanced

EXP-12 · intermediate/advanced

Sudden vs. Gradual Accretion

Run --mode horizons --M0 5 --M1 10 --duration_rs0 0.5 (a near-instantaneous mass jump) and compare the left-panel event-horizon curve with the default --duration_rs0 10 run. Does the sudden case still rise smoothly before \(v_1\), or does its shape change qualitatively? Increase --n_steps if the sudden case looks under-resolved. What would you expect to happen to the "anticipation" lead-time (how far before \(v_1\) the event horizon becomes measurably distinct from the apparent horizon) as the accretion becomes more sudden?

EXP-13 · intermediate/advanced

How Many Bisections Are Enough?

Run --mode horizons --M0 5 --M1 10 with --bisect_iters set to 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60 and 70, recording residual_rs0 each time. Confirm the residual halves (roughly) with each additional iteration, consistent with bisection's linear convergence — and then confirm it stops doing so: at some point the individual trial geodesics' own RK4 integration error, and eventually double-precision floating-point round-off, dominates, and further iterations buy essentially nothing. Where does that happen for you? Separately, explain why a shooting method built on bisection is a reasonable choice here even though far faster root-finders exist — think about what "escapes" vs. "plunges" costs to evaluate, and what smoothness properties the underlying function does or does not have near the critical value.

EXP-14 · advanced

A Black Hole That Barely Grows, and One That Grows a Lot

Run --mode horizons --M0 10 --M1 10.5 and --mode horizons --M0 10 --M1 100. Compare the shapes of the two \(r_{EH}(v)\) curves, in particular how far in advance of \(v_1\) each event horizon becomes distinguishable from its apparent horizon, and how sharply each one turns over near \(v_2\). Connect what you see to the fact that a black hole's horizon area can never decrease (the area theorem): check, from your CSV output, that both \(r_{AH}\) and \(r_{EH}\) are non-decreasing functions of \(v\) in both runs.

Optional projects · advanced

EXP-15 · advanced

Newtonian Tides, Relativistic Reach

By hand, derive the Newtonian tidal acceleration between two points separated by \(\delta r\) along the line to a point mass \(M\) at distance \(r\), starting from \(g(r)=GM/r^2\) and a first-order Taylor expansion. Compare your result term-for-term with the program's a_radial formula. Given that they agree exactly, what precisely does general relativity add to this particular calculation? (Consider what it means that the formula is asserted to hold arbitrarily close to, and inside, \(r_s\) — a regime where the Newtonian derivation's own assumptions no longer straightforwardly apply, even though the formula survives.)

EXP-16 · advanced

Proper Distance Near the Throat and Far Away

Using the embed CSV out to a large --r_max_rs (try 50), fit the excess proper distance \(\Delta = (\text{proper\_radial\_distance\_m}) - (r-r_s)\) as a function of \(r/r_s\) at large radius. Does \(\Delta\) approach a constant, grow, or shrink as \(r\to\infty\)? Separately, examine the near-horizon behaviour: does your fit, or a direct look at the embedding formula, suggest \(\Delta\) diverges, vanishes, or stays finite as \(r\to r_s^+\)? (Careful: it is the embedding's slope \(dz/dr\) that diverges at the throat, not the proper distance itself, which shrinks to zero there along with \(r-r_s\) — do not assume the answer before you compute it.) Support your answer with the closed form for \(z(r)\) and its relationship to proper distance along the embedded surface.

EXP-17 · advanced

Beyond This Program: Rotation

This is a short reading-and-reasoning project, not a coding one. Every real astrophysical black hole is expected to rotate, and is therefore better described by the Kerr metric than by Schwarzschild. Research (from Chapter 21 of Gravity from the Ground Up, or another source) how the Kerr event horizon and ergosphere differ from their Schwarzschild counterparts, and identify, specifically, which of this program's four calculations would need the most substantial revision for a rotating hole and which would need the least. Justify each answer in two or three sentences grounded in what actually changes in the metric.

Synthesis

EXP-18 · synthesis

One Black Hole, Four Views

Choose a single mass, say 10 solar masses, and run all four modes for it (for horizons, use --M0 10 --M1 10, the no-accretion case, so the horizon is unambiguous and matches the other three). Write a short, connected paragraph — not four separate answers — that uses the Schwarzschild radius from one run to make sense of a number from each of the other three: the throat's curvature scale from embed, the tidal acceleration at the horizon from tidal, the proper infall time from infall, and the (trivial, in this no-accretion case) horizon radius from horizons. The point of the exercise is the connections, not the individual numbers.

EXP-19 · synthesis

Exact, Numerical, and Idealised: Map the Whole Program

Make a three-column table of every calculation in this program — Flamm's paraboloid, the tidal-acceleration formulae, the survival-radius inversion, the radial geodesic equations, the local-speed and redshift formulae, the Vaidya apparent horizon, the outgoing-null-geodesic integration, and the event-horizon bisection — classified as an exact closed-form result, a numerically integrated (and therefore step-size- or iteration-dependent) result, or an idealisation of the physical scenario rather than of the mathematics. Then name the three physical effects you judge most significant among those this program leaves out entirely — candidates include rotation (Kerr geometry), Hawking radiation, radiation reaction / self-force on the infalling particle, the finite strength and self-gravity of a real body in the tidal calculation, and realistic (rather than prescribed) accretion-flow physics — and say, for each, which of the four modes it would change the most.

Domain of Validity

This program is a teaching instrument built entirely from exact general-relativistic formulae. Unlike the stellar-structure programs in this suite, there is no fitted closure anywhere in it to be wary of — but there is a physical scenario being idealised, and a numerical method in horizons whose limits are worth stating plainly.

QuantityAcceptedWhat is idealised
black-hole massany positive valuemasses outside about 2 to 5×10¹⁰ \(M_\odot\) — the observed range of known black holes — are flagged, since general relativity itself supplies no mass scale
tidal test bodyany positive separationa point-like rod with no mass, no internal structure and no finite strength of its own; a real body's own self-gravity and material strength are absent
infall release radiusany \(r_0>r_s\)purely radial free fall from rest, zero angular momentum; no radiation reaction, so the particle does not lose energy to gravitational waves as it falls, unlike a real infalling mass
infall stopping radius\(1the integration is stopped just outside the horizon because the Schwarzschild t coordinate diverges there; this is a coordinate limitation of the calculation, not a physical one
Vaidya mass functionany non-decreasing piecewise-linear rampprescribed by the student, not derived from an accretion-disk or infall calculation; models only accretion (mass increase), never evaporation or mass loss
event-horizon locationcontrolled by --v_start_margin_rs0 and --bisect_itersa numerical shooting-method approximation with a quantified, exercisable residual error; never exact at any finite setting

What is absent

  • Rotation. Every calculation here uses the non-rotating Schwarzschild (or Vaidya) geometry. A real astrophysical black hole is expected to be spinning and is better described by the Kerr metric, whose horizon, ergosphere and innermost stable orbit all differ from their Schwarzschild counterparts.
  • Charge. Astrophysical black holes are expected to be very nearly electrically neutral, so this is a minor omission physically, but it is worth naming: the general static, spherical solution is Reissner-Nordström, of which Schwarzschild is the uncharged special case used throughout this program.
  • Radiation reaction and self-force. The infalling test particle in infall does not lose energy to gravitational radiation as it falls, and does not back-react on the geometry at all; a real infalling mass, especially a comparable-mass one, would do both.
  • Hawking radiation and quantum effects. Entirely classical general relativity throughout; nothing here evaporates.
  • Realistic accretion physics. The mass function in horizons is a simple, explicit ramp chosen by the student. A real accretion flow has its own hydrodynamics, radiative transfer, and angular momentum transport, none of which is modelled; the Vaidya metric used here idealises the infalling matter as a pressureless, purely radial null dust.
  • Finite-size and material effects in the tidal calculation. The tidal formulae are for a point-like separation; a real extended body has its own self-gravity, elasticity and material strength, all of which affect whether and how it is actually torn apart, and none of which this program attempts to model.

What the program captures well is the logic: why an embedding diagram can be quantitatively exact while depicting only an instant of space and nothing about time; why bigger black holes are gentler at the horizon; why a falling observer's own clock and a distant observer's coordinate time tell two consistent but very different stories about the same infall; and why, once a black hole is allowed to change, "the horizon" splits into a local notion you can compute from the here-and-now and a global one that requires knowing the whole future.
