Description

Black_hole_spacetime_visualizer takes the single most striking prediction of general relativity — the black hole — and gives a student four different, quantitative ways to look at it. One program covers four calculations, selected with --mode: embed draws the embedding diagram of the curved spatial geometry around the hole; tidal computes the stretching and compressing tidal acceleration a body feels at a given radius, and compares that across black-hole masses; infall follows a test particle dropped from rest all the way to the horizon, in both its own time and a distant observer's; and horizons makes visible a distinction that is usually only stated in words — the difference between the local, instantaneous apparent horizon and the global, teleological event horizon of a black hole that is actively gaining mass.

The default run (--mode embed) draws Flamm's paraboloid for a ten solar-mass black hole: a Schwarzschild radius of 29.5 km, and a throat with a vertical tangent exactly at the horizon. Switching to tidal for the same hole gives a radial tidal acceleration of 1.9×10⁷ g at the horizon — solidly lethal — while the supermassive black hole at the centre of the Milky Way, Sgr A* at 4.31×10⁶ solar masses, manages only about 10⁻⁴ g there: gentle enough that, by this criterion alone, you would not notice crossing its horizon. Switching to infall, the same ten solar-mass hole shows a particle released from rest at six Schwarzschild radii reaching a stopping radius just outside the horizon (\(r_{\rm stop}=1.0005\,r_s\), by default — the program deliberately does not integrate all the way to \(r_s\) itself; see the algorithm section) after about 2.2048 milliseconds of its own proper time (the exact closed-form value at the horizon itself, quoted here only as an analytic extrapolation the run does not compute, is about 2.2049 ms — indistinguishable at this level of rounding), while the Schwarzschild coordinate time used by a distant observer is already diverging logarithmically at that point and never catches up. And horizons, for a hole doubling its mass by accretion, shows the event horizon rising measurably before the accretion begins — it has to anticipate mass that has not arrived yet — while the apparent horizon only starts moving once the infalling matter is actually there.

What this program is, and what it is not. Every number in this program is an exact consequence of the Schwarzschild geometry, or, for horizons, its simplest time-dependent generalisation, the ingoing Vaidya metric. There is no equation of state, no fitted closure, and no calibration to real astrophysical data anywhere in this module — unlike the stellar-structure programs in this suite. What is idealised is the physical scenario: point test particles and test rods with no mass or extent of their own, purely radial motion with zero angular momentum, and, in horizons, a mass function chosen by the student rather than one derived from a real accretion flow. No rotation (a real astrophysical black hole is a Kerr hole, not a Schwarzschild one), no radiation reaction, and no back-reaction of the infalling matter on the geometry beyond the prescribed mass function.
What is exact and what is numerical: a teal EXACT tag marks a formula that is an exact closed-form consequence of the metric, a green ODE tag marks a quantity obtained by numerically integrating a stated differential equation, and a blue DERIVED tag marks an algebraic result derived from the exact formulae. There are no fitted or calibrated quantities anywhere in this module — a deliberate contrast with the stellar-structure programs, worth pointing out explicitly the first time a student meets both.

Physical Background

Curved Space You Can Draw

The full Schwarzschild spacetime is four-dimensional and cannot be embedded in ordinary 3-D space at all. But a single instant of it — the spatial geometry alone, at fixed Schwarzschild time t, restricted to the equatorial plane — is a 2-D curved surface, and that can be embedded exactly in flat 3-D Euclidean space. The resulting picture, Flamm's paraboloid, is not a metaphor or an artist's impression: every distance measured along the drawn surface equals the corresponding proper distance computed from the Schwarzschild metric. It is the one genuinely quantitative "rubber sheet" picture of a black hole, and it is worth being precise about what it does and does not depict: it says nothing about time, nothing about gravity as a force, and the popular image of a ball rolling toward the centre to represent gravitational attraction is not part of what the embedding actually shows.

Why Tidal Forces Are the Honest Danger

Crossing a black hole's event horizon is not, by itself, locally violent: for a large enough hole nothing dramatic happens right at the horizon. What can kill you is the tidal force — the difference in gravitational acceleration between your head and your feet — and that difference depends on the size of the hole in a way most people find surprising. Because the Schwarzschild radius grows linearly with mass while the tidal acceleration at that radius falls as the inverse square of the mass, a bigger black hole is gentler at its horizon, not fiercer. This program lets a student verify that scaling directly rather than take it on faith.

Two Clocks, One Infall

A particle falling into a Schwarzschild black hole experiences nothing pathological at the horizon: its proper time runs smoothly and it crosses in a finite, ordinary interval. A distant observer watching it fall sees something entirely different. The Schwarzschild t coordinate — which is also the time kept by a clock very far from the hole — takes longer and longer to advance the closer the particle gets to the horizon, formally diverging as the particle approaches it, so that a distant observer never actually sees the crossing happen. The two clocks are not in conflict: they are simply measuring different things, one the particle's own elapsed time, the other a coordinate defined by, and only meaningful to, observers who stay far away. Accompanying the coordinate-time divergence is a genuine, observable effect: light sent outward by the falling particle is progressively redshifted and, in the strict mathematical limit, never quite finishes arriving — which is the precise sense in which an object falling into a black hole is said to "freeze and fade" as seen from outside, even though nothing freezes from the particle's own point of view.

Apparent Horizon and Event Horizon: Local vs. Global

In an unchanging (static) black hole spacetime, "the horizon" is unambiguous, and the two definitions in this section's title agree exactly. Once the hole is allowed to change — by swallowing matter, or, in principle, by evaporating — they come apart, and the distinction is one of the more important ideas in modern black-hole physics.

The apparent horizon is defined locally, at a single instant: it is the outermost surface where outward-pointing light rays instantaneously fail to increase their radius. You can find it from the geometry at that moment alone, with no knowledge of the future. The event horizon is defined globally: it is the boundary of the set of events from which a light ray can never escape to arbitrarily large distance, no matter how long you wait. Answering "can it ever escape" requires knowing the entire future of the spacetime — which is exactly why the event horizon is called teleological, and why, in a dynamical spacetime, it can begin moving in response to infalling matter that has not arrived yet. This program makes that abstract statement into a picture: for a black hole that gains mass over some interval of time, the located event horizon is demonstrably larger than the apparent horizon throughout the accretion, and it starts rising perceptibly before the accretion begins.

Governing Equations

The Schwarzschild Metric

Schwarzschild line element EXACT
\[ds^2 = -\left(1-\frac{r_s}{r}\right)c^2dt^2 + \frac{dr^2}{1-r_s/r} + r^2\left(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right), \qquad r_s = \frac{2GM}{c^2}.\]

Every formula in this program follows from this line element (or, for horizons, from its ingoing-null-coordinate generalisation below), together with the geodesic equation for a freely falling test particle or photon. Nothing here is fitted to data or calibrated against a more detailed model.

Mode embed: Flamm's Paraboloid

Restrict the metric to \(t=\text{const}\), \(\theta=\pi/2\): \(ds^2 = dr^2/(1-r_s/r) + r^2 d\phi^2\). Embed this as a surface of revolution \(z(r)\) in flat 3-D space, where \(ds^2=dz^2+dr^2+r^2d\phi^2\):

Embedding profile DERIVED
\[\left(\frac{dz}{dr}\right)^2 = \frac{r_s}{r-r_s} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad z(r) = 2\sqrt{r_s(r-r_s)}, \qquad r\ge r_s.\]

Mode tidal: Geodesic Deviation

Two nearby free-falling test particles separated by proper distance \(\delta\ell\) accelerate relative to each other according to the geodesic deviation equation, governed by the Riemann tensor. For Schwarzschild, in the local orthonormal frame of an observer at radius \(r\) (static or radially infalling — the radial components turn out to be the same for both):

Tidal acceleration EXACT
\[a_{\rm radial} = \frac{2GM}{r^3}\,\delta r \quad(\text{stretching: the two ends pull apart}), \qquad a_{\rm tangential} = \frac{GM}{r^3}\,\delta\ell \quad(\text{compressing: the two ends push together, each transverse direction}).\]

Both quantities are quoted, printed, plotted and written to CSV as magnitudes; the physical sense of each — one stretching the body along the radial line, the other squeezing it in the two transverse directions — is carried by the words "radial/stretching" and "tangential/compressing," not by a sign on the number. It is worth being precise about exactly what is, and is not, exact here. The curvature coefficients \(2GM/r^3\) and \(GM/r^3\) are exact Schwarzschild results, and are numerically identical, term for term, to the Newtonian tidal-acceleration formula for a point mass \(M\) — what differs from Newtonian intuition is only where you are allowed to evaluate them: general relativity insists the coefficients hold however close to \(r_s\) you go, all the way to (and through) the horizon. But multiplying an exact local coefficient by a finite separation \(\delta r\) or \(\delta\ell\), as this program does to report an acceleration in physical units, is the linearized (infinitesimal-separation) geodesic-deviation approximation, not an exact finite-body result "at any order": it silently assumes the curvature does not change appreciably across the body. That assumption is outstanding for every astrophysical example this program is built to illustrate (a person-scale separation is utterly negligible next to any stellar or supermassive Schwarzschild radius), but the program's own accepted mass and separation ranges do not by themselves guarantee it — at the smallest masses --compare_masses accepts, \(r_s\) can be only millimetres across, making a 1.8 m separation hundreds of horizon radii, nowhere near "infinitesimal." The program now warns explicitly (in the printed summary and CSV) whenever the separation is not small compared with the sampled radius; see Exercise EXP-15.

Illustrative survival radius DERIVED
\[r_{\rm crit} = \left(\frac{2GM\,\delta\ell}{a_{\rm limit}}\right)^{1/3}, \qquad a_{\rm limit} = (\text{limit\_g})\times g.\]

Solving \(a_{\rm radial}=a_{\rm limit}\) for \(r\) gives the radius at which a body of length \(\delta\ell\) first experiences a chosen illustrative tidal-acceleration threshold. Comparing \(r_{\rm crit}\) with \(r_s\) tells you whether that threshold is reached outside the horizon (the body is destroyed before crossing) or only inside it (the body crosses intact, by this criterion). --limit_g is a deliberately round illustrative number, not a precise physiological claim, and one of the exercises asks what changes if you adopt a different one.

Mode infall: Radial Free Fall

For a particle released from rest at \(r_0\), the conserved specific energy is \(E=\sqrt{1-r_s/r_0}\) (E \(\to 1\) as \(r_0\to\infty\)). The exact radial equations of motion are:

Radial geodesic equations EXACT, integrated ODE
\[\left(\frac{dr}{d\tau}\right)^2 = c^2\left(\frac{r_s}{r}-\frac{r_s}{r_0}\right), \qquad \frac{dt}{d\tau} = \frac{E}{1-r_s/r}.\]

\(dr/d\tau\) is finite and smooth all the way through \(r=r_s\): nothing pathological happens to the particle there. \(dt/d\tau\) diverges as \(r\to r_s\): this is what makes the distant observer's coordinate-time coordinate run away. Both are integrated numerically with RK4 in the particle's own proper time \(\tau\), with the step size capped at a fraction of the remaining distance to the stopping radius and at a fraction of the distance already fallen from \(r_0\) — exactly as the structure integrators of the other GFtGU programs cap their step by a fraction of the current integration variable. The second cap matters here specifically: as a function of \(r\), \(dr/d\tau\) behaves like \(-\text{const}\times\sqrt{r_0-r}\) close to release (the particle starts from rest, so its speed grows like the square root of the distance fallen), an infinite-slope branch point that would otherwise quietly knock RK4's accuracy down from fourth order to first order for the whole run, no matter how small --step_frac is set.

Local speed and redshift DERIVED
\[v_{\rm local}(r) = \frac{1}{E}\sqrt{\frac{r_s}{r}-\frac{r_s}{r_0}}\ \longrightarrow\ 1 \text{ as } r\to r_s, \] \[\frac{\nu_{\rm obs}}{\nu_{\rm emit}} = \sqrt{1-\frac{r_s}{r}}\ \sqrt{\frac{1-v_{\rm local}}{1+v_{\rm local}}}\ \longrightarrow\ 0 \text{ as } r\to r_s.\]

The redshift factor is the product of the ordinary gravitational redshift for a static source at \(r\) and the special-relativistic Doppler factor for that source's local motion relative to a static observer it is passing. For a particle released from rest at infinity (\(E=1\)) this collapses to the well-known closed form \(\nu_{\rm obs}/\nu_{\rm emit}=1-\sqrt{r_s/r}\); Exercise EXP-7 asks you to verify that limit directly from the program's output.

Mode horizons: the Ingoing Vaidya Metric

To model a hole that gains mass, replace the constant \(M\) in the Schwarzschild metric with a prescribed, non-decreasing function \(M(v)\) of the ingoing null (advanced-time) coordinate \(v\):

Ingoing Vaidya line element EXACT (as a solution of the Einstein field equations for infalling null dust)
\[ds^2 = -\left(1-\frac{2M(v)}{r}\right)dv^2 + 2\,dv\,dr + r^2d\Omega^2 \qquad (G=c=1 \text{ in this line}).\]

This program uses a mass function that ramps linearly from \(M_0\) to \(M_1\) between advanced times \(v_1\) and \(v_2=v_1+\)--duration_rs0, and is constant before and after — a simple, explicit, student-chosen stand-in for an accretion episode, not a result of an accretion-physics calculation.

Apparent horizon DERIVED (algebraic, from the mass function alone)
\[r_{AH}(v) = 2M(v).\]
Outgoing radial null geodesics EXACT, integrated ODE
\[\frac{dr}{dv} = \frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{2M(v)}{r}\right).\]

The event horizon is the one outgoing null geodesic that neither escapes to \(r\to\infty\) nor falls to \(r=0\). It has an exact boundary condition at late times: once accretion finishes at \(v_2\), the spacetime is exactly static Schwarzschild with mass \(M_1\), so the event and apparent horizons coincide there exactly, \(r=2M_1\), with no search needed at all. This program's primary construction integrates that one geodesic backward in \(v\) from \(r(v_2)=2M_1\); see the algorithm section for why this is not merely a numerically convenient alternative but the numerically well-conditioned way to locate this particular geodesic. A second, forward construction — firing trial geodesics from deep in the static region before \(v_1\) and bisecting the initial radius until a trajectory neither clearly escapes nor clearly plunges by a late time \(v_{\rm end}\) — is also computed, and is retained for two purposes: it drives the "family of nearby geodesics" panel that makes the event horizon's role as a separatrix visible, and it supplies a secondary, genuinely independent accuracy check against the backward construction. "Escapes" and "plunges," used by that forward construction, are themselves finite-time, finite-radius numerical verdicts — a trial geodesic is called plunged once \(r\) drops to a small floor, and unambiguously escaped once \(r\) grows past a large ceiling, both comfortably outside the region that matters physically but neither literally \(r\to 0\) or \(r\to\infty\) — not a compromise specific to this program, since no computation can literally run to infinite \(r\) or infinite \(v\) either.

Algorithm

Mode embed

  1. Validate the mass and the outer radius (must exceed \(r_s\)); compute \(r_s\) and evaluate \(z(r)=2\sqrt{r_s(r-r_s)}\) on a linear grid from \(r_s\) to --r_max_rs\(\times r_s\).
  2. Also evaluate the proper radial distance \(\int_{r_s}^r dr'/\sqrt{1-r_s/r'}\) by a singularity-removing substitution \(r'=r_s+u^2\), for the exercises that compare it with the naive coordinate difference \(r-r_s\).
  3. Hand the profile to the plotting layer, which also constructs the 3-D surface of revolution for display.

Mode tidal

  1. Validate the mass, the innermost radius (must exceed \(r_s\)) and the test-rod separation; evaluate the exact tidal-acceleration formulae on a logarithmic radial grid.
  2. If --compare_masses is supplied, repeat the horizon-value calculation and the survival-radius inversion for each mass in the list and tabulate the results.

Mode infall

  1. Validate the mass, the release radius \(r_0\) (must exceed \(r_s\)) and the stopping radius \(r_{\rm stop}\) (must lie strictly between \(r_s\) and \(r_0\)); compute \(E=\sqrt{1-r_s/r_0}\).
  2. Record the exact release event \((\tau=0, t=0, r=r_0)\) as the first sample. \(r_0\) itself is not a physical equilibrium: a particle released from rest there has a perfectly ordinary, nonzero inward proper acceleration, and moves immediately. What is singular at \(r_0\) is only this integrator's first-order formulation, \((dr/d\tau)^2=c^2(r_s/r-r_s/r_0)\): it is non-Lipschitz there (infinite \(dr/d\tau\)-vs.-\(r\) slope), a numerical-startup issue with this particular equation, not a physical fixed point. The numerical integration below therefore begins an infinitesimal distance inside \(r_0\) — but that second sample is seeded with its own exact proper and coordinate time, taken from the closed-form cycloid solution quoted in Exercise EXP-11, rather than being mislabeled \(\tau=0\).
  3. Advance \(r\) and \(t\) together with RK4 in \(\tau\), with the step \(d\tau\) capped at a fraction (--step_frac) of the remaining proper-time-equivalent distance to \(r_{\rm stop}\) and of the distance already fallen from \(r_0\), so the step shrinks geometrically near both ends of the track — near \(r_{\rm stop}\) because \(t\) and the redshift vary rapidly there, and near \(r_0\) because \(dr/d\tau\) has an infinite slope in \(r\) at the release point, which would otherwise silently degrade the integrator's accuracy. Interpolate the final partial step so the recorded track ends exactly at \(r_{\rm stop}\).
  4. At each recorded point, evaluate the local speed and the outward redshift factor from the closed-form expressions above.
  5. The integration deliberately stops just outside the horizon, at \(r_{\rm stop}=\)--r_stop_rs\(\times r_s\) (default 1.0005), because the Schwarzschild \(t\) coordinate used for the distant observer's clock diverges there; the particle's own \(\tau\) does not, and nothing in the underlying physics is singular at the horizon itself — only this particular coordinate choice is.

Mode horizons

Why backward, not forward. Firing trial geodesics from before accretion begins and bisecting the starting radius -- the seemingly obvious approach -- has a structural weakness explained in step 3 below: it can return a curve that violates the program's own documented invariant \(r_{EH}\ge r_{AH}\), not only at unusual settings but at fully default parameters for a mild mass-growth case (\(M_0=10\to M_1=10.5\;M_\odot\)). The event horizon reported by this program is instead constructed by integrating backward in \(v\) from the exact, search-free boundary condition at the end of accretion. Forward shooting is still computed, but only in a secondary role -- driving the geodesic-family visualisation panel and providing an independent accuracy diagnostic -- never supplying the reported \(r_{EH}(v)\) itself.
  1. Validate \(M_0\), \(M_1\) (must satisfy \(M_1\ge M_0\): this Vaidya mass function models only accretion, never mass loss), and the accretion-window and margin parameters (\(v_1\) itself may be any finite value, including zero or negative — the advanced-time origin is arbitrary; only --duration_rs0 and the two margins have a physically required positive sign). Build the piecewise-linear mass function \(M(v)\) and the corresponding apparent horizon \(r_{AH}(v)=2M(v)\) directly.
  2. Primary construction. Once accretion finishes at \(v_2\), the spacetime is exactly static Schwarzschild with mass \(M_1\), so \(r=2M_1\) at \(v_2\) is the event horizon's boundary condition — exact, with no search needed. Integrate the outgoing-null-geodesic equation \(dr/dv=\tfrac12(1-2M(v)/r)\) backward in \(v\) from that point down to \(v_{\rm start}=v_1-\)--v_start_margin_rs0\(\times r_{s0}\), with RK4 and the same adaptive, current-radius-capped step used for the forward construction below. (When \(M_0=M_1\) exactly, this reduces to the trivial exact answer \(r_{EH}(v)=r_{s0}\) for the whole run, computed directly without integration.) The result, interpolated onto the output grid together with the exact post-\(v_2\) segment \(r=r_{s1}\), is what is plotted and exported as \(r_{EH}(v)\). A postcondition check confirms \(r_{EH}(v)\ge r_{AH}(v)\) everywhere on the output grid (to a small numerical tolerance) before the run is allowed to complete; if it does not hold — which should not happen for any physically reasonable input — the run fails loudly with a clear error rather than exporting a curve that contradicts its own documented invariant.
  3. Why backward, not forward: \(r=2M(v)\) is a fixed point of the outgoing-null-geodesic equation whenever \(M\) is locally constant (true both before \(v_1\) and after \(v_2\)). Linearising a small offset \(\epsilon=r-2M\) gives \(d\epsilon/dv=\epsilon/(2M)\): the fixed point is unstable running forward in \(v\) and stable running backward — the same equation, opposite direction. Forward shooting inherits that instability directly: any offset from the true horizon generator, even one at the level of a single floating-point ULP (the smallest a bisection search can ever remove), is amplified exponentially over however much \(v\)-range --v_start_margin_rs0/--v_end_margin_rs0 requests. Backward integration, starting from an exact boundary condition rather than a search, is self-correcting in exactly the same sense the forward construction is self-uncorrecting — which is why the reported event horizon is built this way, rather than by forward shooting.
  4. Secondary construction (diagnostic and visualisation only). A trial outgoing null geodesic is also integrated forward, from a chosen starting radius at \(v_{\rm start}\) out to \(v_{\rm end}=v_2+\)--v_end_margin_rs0\(\times r_{s0}\). A trial is classified numerically on one of three explicit finite criteria: plunged the moment \(r\) first drops to or below a small floor (\(10^{-4}\) of \(m_0+m_1\)), unambiguously escaped the moment \(r\) first exceeds a large ceiling (\(10^4\) of \(m_0+m_1\)), and otherwise — if it simply reaches \(v_{\rm end}\) without doing either — classified by comparing its radius there against \(r_{s1}\). The starting radius is bracketed between a value that clearly falls in and one that clearly escapes (widened geometrically from near \(r_{s0}\) if needed) and bisected for --bisect_iters iterations. This secondary result, and the bracket width that produced it (residual_rs0), are reported purely as diagnostics: r_crit_shooting_over_rs0 is where forward shooting alone would have placed the horizon at \(v_{\rm start}\), and shooting_vs_backward_rs0 is the disagreement between the two constructions' STARTING radius only. That single-radius comparison can look reassuringly small even when the forward trial has already gone badly wrong further along its own track — including plunging before ever reaching \(v_2\) — so three further fields report the actual curve-wise behaviour instead: shooting_reached_v2 (did the trial reach \(v_2\) at all, rather than plunging or escaping first), shooting_v2_boundary_residual_rs0 (its own error against \(r=2M_1\) there, if it did), and shooting_vs_backward_curve_max_rs0 (the largest pointwise difference between the forward and backward curves over whatever portion of \([v_{\rm start},v_2]\) the forward trial actually covers). residual_rs0 itself measures only how many times the bracket was halved, driven to floating-point noise by --bisect_iters alone, independent of how accurate the located geodesic itself is (see Exercise EXP-13, which walks through exactly this distinction).
  5. Finally, integrate a small family of geodesics with starting radii spread around the (backward-derived) critical radius at \(v_{\rm start}\), for the panel that shows directly which nearby trajectories escape and which plunge. With --n_family 1 this reduces to just the event-horizon generator itself, presented without the escape/plunge legend, since a single trajectory is not a bracketing family; --n_family 2 is rejected outright for the same reason (it can be neither a genuine bracket nor the single-trajectory case).
Input and runtime safeguards: masses, radii and separations must be finite and positive; embedding and tidal radii must exceed \(r_s\); an infall release radius must exceed \(r_s\) and the stopping radius must lie strictly between \(r_s\) and the release radius; --step_frac must lie in \((10^{-5}, 0.2]\); a Vaidya run requires \(M_1\ge M_0\); the null-geodesic integrator caps its own step size against the current radius specifically to prevent the horizon-shooting bisection from being corrupted by an overshoot instability; and --no_plot is refused unless a CSV directory is given, so a run always produces something. Masses far outside the observed black-hole mass range are still computed exactly — general relativity supplies no natural mass scale — but are flagged, since the point of running one is usually precisely to see the numbers become extreme. Finite inputs that would still overflow a computation (an astronomically large mass or radius ratio, or a CLI integer with hundreds of digits) are now explicitly checked and rejected with a concise message, rather than silently returning infinity or a raw Python traceback. tidal and infall now also cap --r_max_rs and --r0_rs respectively at large but generous finite values for the same reason. CSV output filenames now use microsecond-resolution timestamps with a collision-safe fallback, so two fast successive runs into the same directory (as in a shell-loop parameter sweep) can no longer silently overwrite one another.
A packaging note on equation rendering: this file is otherwise entirely self-contained, but the equations are rendered by loading MathJax 3 from a remote CDN (cdn.jsdelivr.net) at the top of the page. This is a deliberate, acknowledged packaging choice, not an oversight: it keeps this HTML file small and lets MathJax stay current, at the cost of requiring network access the first time the page renders equations. If fully offline use is a requirement for your setup, vendor a local copy of MathJax (or a lighter equation renderer) and change the <script src="..."> line near the top of this file's source accordingly.

Constants (including the IAU 2015 nominal solar mass parameter, GM_SUN_NOMINAL), the Schwarzschild radius, Flamm's paraboloid and the Gaussian curvature \(K(r)\) of its slice, the exact tidal-acceleration formulae and survival-radius inversion, the radial-infall RK4 integrator (with a startup seeded from the exact cycloid closed form) and its local-speed and redshift formulae, the Vaidya mass function, the adaptive-step outgoing-null-geodesic integrator (forward and backward), and the backward-integration construction that locates the event horizon, with a secondary forward-shooting search kept for diagnostics and the geodesic-family panel. All validation of physical inputs lives here.

Driver Layer
driver_bh.py

Validates presentation-level inputs, parses and numerically sorts the comma-separated comparison-mass list (so the cross-mass plot panels trace a clean curve regardless of input order), dispatches on --mode, prints the run summary, writes full-precision, collision-safe timestamped CSV files (including secondary files for the tidal cross-mass comparison and the horizon geodesic family), and calls the plotter.

Plot Layer
plot_bh.py

Four figure layouts, one per mode — including a 3-D surface of revolution for the embedding diagram and a two-colour escapes/plunges geodesic-family panel for the horizons mode — with shared save-or-display logic.

Entry Point
main.py

Defines the student-facing command-line options, grouped by mode, and converts validation or runtime failures into concise command-line error messages.

Parameters

Run python main.py --help for the current command summary, and python main.py --version for the model version, which is also written into every CSV header. Options that do not apply to the selected mode are ignored silently — --mode embed --r0_rs 6 will not change anything — so check the printed summary, or the parameter block at the top of the CSV file, to confirm which values the run actually used.

Suggested Experiments

The exercises are divided into a core sequence, arranged from reading the output through controlled comparisons to numerical work, and a set of optional projects that ask for a somewhat larger investigation. Two synthesis exercises close the set. Nothing here needs anything beyond the program, a spreadsheet or short script, and the printed summary.

Core sequence · introductory

EXP-1 · introductory

Read the Throat

Run python main.py --mode embed --M 10. Identify the throat in both panels. Why does the profile have a vertical tangent exactly at \(r=r_s\), and why is that not a numerical mistake? The dashed lower branch is a mirror image drawn for visual symmetry only — state, in your own words, what would and would not be true if you instead interpreted it as a tunnel through to "the other side."

EXP-2 · introductory

How Gentle Is the Tide?

Run python main.py --mode tidal --M 10 --compare_masses 10,4.31e6. Record the radial tidal acceleration at the horizon, in g, for both masses. 4.31×10⁶ solar masses is (very nearly) the mass of Sgr A*, the black hole at the centre of our galaxy. For which of the two does the program's illustrative 100 g threshold get reached only inside the horizon (by this criterion, crossed intact) rather than outside it (destroyed first)? Which scales as you might have naively guessed — bigger hole, stronger tide, or the reverse — and why?

EXP-3 · introductory

Falling In

Run python main.py --mode infall --M 10 --r0_rs 6. Record the proper time and the Schwarzschild coordinate time at which the integration stops, just outside the horizon. Which one is "how long it took," from the falling observer's own point of view? At roughly what value of \(r/r_s\) does the local infall speed first exceed half the speed of light?

EXP-4 · introductory

Two Kinds of Horizon

Run python main.py --mode horizons --M0 5 --M1 10 --csvdir ./data. In your own words, state the difference between how the apparent horizon and the event horizon are each defined — one sentence for each, using the words "instantaneous" and "future" somewhere. From the CSV, restricting your search to \(v\ge v_1\) (before \(v_1\) the gap is already far below any reasonable threshold, so an unrestricted search would just return the very first row and miss the point of the exercise), find the first value of \(v/r_{s0}\) at which \(|r_{EH}-r_{AH}|/r_{s0} < 10^{-3}\) — a concrete, quantitative stand-in for "become indistinguishable" — and how does that compare with the end of the shaded accretion window? Separately: after \(v_2\) the two curves coincide exactly, by construction, not merely to within your \(10^{-3}\) tolerance — explain in one sentence why that region is a genuinely different case from the numerical-coincidence question you just answered.

EXP-5 · introductory/intermediate

Coordinate Distance Is Not Proper Distance

Using --mode embed --csvdir ./data, compare the proper_radial_distance_m column with the naive coordinate difference \(r-r_s\) at a few radii out to 8 \(r_s\). Report the excess as a percentage of the coordinate difference, i.e. \(100\times[(\text{proper\_radial\_distance\_m})-(r-r_s)]/(r-r_s)\) — state explicitly which denominator you used, since "by what percentage do they differ" has more than one defensible reading otherwise. What is that percentage at \(r=2r_s\)? At \(r=8r_s\)? (The default 400-point linear grid happens to land exactly on \(r=2r_s\) and \(r=8r_s\) for --r_max_rs 8; if you change --n_r or --r_max_rs, use the CSV row nearest your target radius, or interpolate, and say which you did.) State, in one sentence, why the two are not equal even though both are "distance from the horizon."

EXP-6 · introductory/intermediate

Where Is the Chosen Tidal Threshold?

Find, by trial and error or by inverting the \(M^{-2}\) scaling algebraically, the approximate mass at which \(r_{\rm crit}=r_s\) exactly for the default 1.8 m separation and 100 g limit. Then repeat with --limit_g 10 and again with --limit_g 1000: how much does the crossover mass move, and why does an order-of-magnitude change in your assumed survival threshold move it by so much less than an order of magnitude?

The Rain-Frame Limit

Core sequence · intermediate

EXP-8 · intermediate

Verify the \(M^{-2}\) Scaling

Run --mode tidal --compare_masses 1,3,10,30,100,300,1000 and, from the CSV comparison table, fit \(\log(a_{\rm radial,\ horizon})\) against \(\log M\). This data is deterministic, not measured — every point lies exactly on the underlying formula up to double-precision round-off — so "fitting uncertainty" is not the right way to think about the residual; report instead the fitted slope itself and its deviation from exactly \(-2\) (which should be tiny, limited only by floating-point precision and how many points you fit). Then derive that exponent yourself algebraically from \(a_{\rm radial}=2GM\delta r/r^3\) together with \(r_s\propto M\).

EXP-9a · intermediate

Compressing and Stretching Are Locked Together

Using the tidal CSV for any mass, verify numerically that a_tangential is exactly half of a_radial at every radius. Prove algebraically, from the two formulae in the equations section, that this ratio holds at every radius for every mass, not just as an approximation.

EXP-10 · intermediate

The Event Horizon's Memory

Run --mode horizons --M0 5 --M1 10 --v_start_margin_rs0 5, then repeat with 10, 15, 20 and 30, and record r_crit_over_rs0 from the printed summary each time. Plot \(\log(r_{\rm crit}/r_{s0}-1)\) against --v_start_margin_rs0. This is not a numerical-error convergence study — \(r_{\rm crit}/r_{s0}-1\) at each margin is the genuine, physical displacement of the true event horizon above the static radius \(r_{s0}\) at that specific, earlier moment in advanced time (the event horizon equals \(r_{s0}\) only in the strict \(v\to-\infty\) limit); the five runs are five different physical questions, not five successive refinements of one answer. Is the decay with increasing margin consistent with an exponential? That exponential decay is the numerical face of the event horizon's teleological character: how precisely the horizon "anticipates" accretion that has not happened yet, arbitrarily far in advance, at an exponentially shrinking but never literally zero displacement. Separately, to see a genuine numerical-accuracy comparison instead: hold --v_start_margin_rs0 fixed and instead compare r_crit_over_rs0 (the primary, backward-integrated result) against r_crit_shooting_over_rs0 (the secondary, forward-shooting result) via the reported shooting_vs_backward_rs0 — that is where a genuine numerical-method error lives in this calculation (see Exercise EXP-13).

EXP-11 · intermediate

Checking the Infall Integration Against a Closed Form

The radial free-fall equation \((dr/d\tau)^2=c^2(r_s/r-r_s/r_0)\) happens to have an exact closed-form solution, the same "cycloid" parametrisation used for Newtonian radial (zero-angular-momentum) Kepler infall: with \(\eta\) running from 0 (at \(r=r_0\)) to \(\eta_{\rm stop}=\arccos(2r_{\rm stop}/r_0-1)\), \[r(\eta)=\tfrac{r_0}{2}(1+\cos\eta), \qquad \tau(\eta)=\sqrt{\tfrac{r_0^3}{8GM}}\,(\eta+\sin\eta).\] Run --mode infall --M 10 --r0_rs 6 (default --r_stop_rs 1.0005) and compare the printed tau_total_ms with \(\tau(\eta_{\rm stop})\) from this formula. You should find agreement to better than one part in \(10^5\) even at the coarsest allowed --step_frac 0.2. Now repeat with --step_frac at 0.1, 0.05, 0.02, 0.01 and 0.005, and plot the relative error on a log scale against --step_frac (also on a log scale). You should see strong convergence as --step_frac is refined — roughly third-to-fourth order, consistent with (though not an exact multiple of) RK4's fourth-order accuracy — down to about --step_frac 0.005, where the shrinking RK4 truncation error and the accumulated floating-point round-off from many more, individually tinier steps become comparable in size. Now push further, to --step_frac 0.001, 0.0001 and 0.00002 (near the smallest accepted value, just above \(10^{-5}\)): the relative error does not settle into a floor that it stays at or below forever. It reaches its smallest values (order \(10^{-10}\) to \(10^{-11}\)) somewhere around --step_frac 0.005-0.0002, then climbs measurably back up as --step_frac keeps shrinking — order \(10^{-8}\) to \(10^{-7}\) by the time you reach the smallest accepted values — as accumulated round-off from ever more, ever tinier floating-point steps comes to dominate the (by then already tiny) RK4 truncation error. Confirm this climb-back-out empirically from your own data: it is real, not noise, and it is the actual, honest limiting behaviour of this integrator, not an idealised endless improvement that a small enough step size could reach. The claim that does hold throughout, and is worth confirming directly: across the entire accepted --step_frac range, the relative error never exceeds its own value at the coarsest allowed step size (--step_frac 0.2) — refining --step_frac below about 0.005 is never necessary for accuracy here, and can occasionally cost a little of it back, but it never costs enough to violate the one-part-in-\(10^5\) tolerance this exercise opened with. Separately: confirm that the very first row of the infall CSV is exactly \(\tau=0\), \(t=0\), \(r=r_0\) — the actual release event, not a numerically displaced stand-in for it.

EXP-12 · intermediate/advanced

Sudden vs. Gradual Accretion

Run --mode horizons --M0 5 --M1 10 --duration_rs0 0.5 (a near-instantaneous mass jump) and compare the left-panel event-horizon curve with the default --duration_rs0 10 run. Does the sudden case still rise smoothly before \(v_1\), or does its shape change qualitatively? --n_steps controls only the output sampling/plot grid, not the underlying geodesic integration (which uses its own adaptive step regardless), so raising it can make a plotted curve look smoother but cannot itself resolve a genuinely under-integrated feature. The backward integrator's own internal step size is not a setting this program's CLI exposes, so treat what you see here as a question about the physical sharpness of the transition, not about integration resolution: shooting_vs_backward_rs0 and its curve-wise companions (see EXP-13) compare the secondary, intentionally unstable forward-shooting construction against the primary backward one, which answers a different question — how far the forward trial has drifted from the primary curve — not whether the primary backward integration itself is well-resolved. Using \(|r_{EH}-r_{AH}|/r_{s0}\ge10^{-3}\) as your quantitative criterion for "measurably distinct" (the complement of the indistinguishability threshold used in EXP-4), what would you expect to happen to the anticipation lead-time (how far before \(v_1\) the event horizon first becomes distinct from the apparent horizon by that criterion) as the accretion becomes more sudden? Check your prediction against both runs.

EXP-13 · intermediate/advanced

Bracket Width Is Not Horizon Accuracy

This exercise is in three parts: bracket-width convergence, the starting-radius diagnostic, and the actual curve-wise error are three genuinely different questions, and it is easy to mistake a small answer to one of them for a small answer to another. Part (a): Run --mode horizons --M0 5 --M1 10 with --bisect_iters set to 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60 and 70, recording residual_rs0 each time. Confirm it decreases roughly by a factor of \(2^{10}\approx1000\) for each block of 10 additional iterations, consistent with bisection's exponential (not merely linear) bracket-halving — and then confirm it eventually stops decreasing, once you reach double-precision floating-point bracket collapse. This is a purely algorithmic property of the bisection loop itself; it says nothing yet about whether the geodesic inside that bracket is close to the true event horizon. Part (b): At the same settings, also record shooting_vs_backward_rs0 — the difference between the two constructions' STARTING radius only, at \(v_{\rm start}\). You will find it already tiny by 20 iterations and minuscule by 30 — which looks reassuring, but measures only where the forward trial began, not what it did afterward. Part (c): Now record the three fields that actually answer that question: shooting_reached_v2, shooting_v2_boundary_residual_rs0, and shooting_vs_backward_curve_max_rs0 (the maximum pointwise difference between the forward-shooting curve and the primary backward curve, over whatever portion of \([v_{\rm start},v_2]\) the forward trial actually reached). For the default \(M_0=5\), \(M_1=10\) case you should find something like: at 10 and 20 iterations, shooting_reached_v2 is False — the trial plunged before ever reaching \(v_2\) — and shooting_vs_backward_curve_max_rs0 is of order 1-2 (i.e. order \(r_{s0}\), not a small correction), even though Part (b)'s shooting_vs_backward_rs0 read only about \(7\times10^{-4}\) and \(7\times10^{-7}\) there. By 30 iterations shooting_reached_v2 becomes True and the curve-wise disagreement collapses to order \(10^{-4}\), falling further at 40 and 60. Explain, using the exponential-instability argument in the algorithm section, why a forward-shooting search can have an excellent starting-radius match yet a badly wrong curve — and why shooting_vs_backward_rs0 alone cannot be used to certify the forward construction's accuracy, only shooting_vs_backward_curve_max_rs0 (together with shooting_reached_v2) can.

EXP-14 · advanced

A Black Hole That Barely Grows, and One That Grows a Lot

Run --mode horizons --M0 10 --M1 10.5 and --mode horizons --M0 10 --M1 100. Compare the shapes of the two \(r_{EH}(v)\) curves, in particular how far in advance of \(v_1\) each event horizon becomes distinguishable from its apparent horizon, and how sharply each one turns over near \(v_2\). Connect what you see to the fact that a black hole's horizon area can never decrease (the area theorem): check, from your CSV output, that both \(r_{AH}\) and \(r_{EH}\) are non-decreasing functions of \(v\) in both runs. The two checks are not equally deep: \(r_{AH}=2M(v)\) is monotonic simply because this program only ever supplies a non-decreasing mass function \(M(v)\) by construction — an algebraic consequence of your input, not itself a test of general relativity. The event-horizon result is the more instructive one, since nothing in the backward-integration construction was told to produce a monotonic curve; that it does so anyway is the actual content of the area theorem being verified.

Optional projects · advanced

EXP-9b · advanced

Why the Factor of 2: Vacuum Tracelessness

This continues EXP-9a and is more advanced: it asks you to explain why the ratio you just verified has to be exactly 2, not merely confirm that it is. In vacuum, the Ricci tensor vanishes, and one consequence is that the three principal tidal-acceleration eigenvalues along the radial and the two transverse directions must sum to zero. Watch the signs carefully: this program exports \(a_{\rm radial}\) and \(a_{\rm tangential}\) as positive magnitudes, with the physical sense (stretching vs. compressing) carried only by their names, so the algebraic statement is not "the three exported magnitudes sum to zero" (they manifestly do not — they are all positive). It is that the signed eigenvalues \(+2GM/r^3\) (radial, stretching) and \(-GM/r^3\), \(-GM/r^3\) (the two transverse directions, compressing) sum to zero: \(2GM/r^3-GM/r^3-GM/r^3=0\). Write out that cancellation explicitly, and explain in your own words why it alone — together with the two transverse directions being equal to each other by the spherical symmetry of the problem — forces the transverse magnitude to be exactly half the radial one.

EXP-15 · advanced

Newtonian Tides, Relativistic Reach — and Where "Exact" Stops Applying

By hand, derive the Newtonian tidal acceleration between two points separated by \(\delta r\) along the line to a point mass \(M\) at distance \(r\), starting from \(g(r)=GM/r^2\) and a first-order Taylor expansion. Compare your result term-for-term with the program's a_radial formula: the coefficient \(2GM/r^3\) agrees exactly, term for term, with the corresponding Schwarzschild curvature component. Given that agreement, what precisely does general relativity add to this particular calculation? (Consider what it means that the coefficient is asserted to hold arbitrarily close to, and inside, \(r_s\) — a regime where the Newtonian derivation's own assumptions no longer straightforwardly apply, even though the coefficient survives.) Then go a step further than the coefficient: both your first-order Taylor expansion and the program's use of it to report an acceleration in physical units multiply that exact local coefficient by a finite \(\delta r\) or \(\delta\ell\) — the linearized, infinitesimal-separation geodesic-deviation approximation. Neither a first-order finite-difference derivation nor the infinitesimal-separation limit of the geodesic deviation equation establishes an exact result for a finite-length body "at any order"; both are leading-order approximations that happen to be excellent whenever the body is small compared with the local curvature scale. Using the program's linearization_ratio diagnostic (separation divided by the smallest sampled radius), find a mass/separation combination within the program's accepted ranges where that ratio is no longer small, and explain in your own words why the reported tidal acceleration should no longer be trusted as more than indicative there.

EXP-16 · advanced

Proper Distance Near the Throat and Far Away

Using the embed CSV out to a large --r_max_rs (try 50), fit the excess proper distance \(\Delta = (\text{proper\_radial\_distance\_m}) - (r-r_s)\) as a function of \(r/r_s\) at large radius. Does \(\Delta\) approach a constant, grow, or shrink as \(r\to\infty\)? Separately, examine the near-horizon behaviour: does your fit, or a direct look at the embedding formula, suggest \(\Delta\) diverges, vanishes, or stays finite as \(r\to r_s^+\)? (Careful: it is the embedding's slope \(dz/dr\) that diverges at the throat, not the proper distance itself, which shrinks to zero there along with \(r-r_s\) — do not assume the answer before you compute it.) Support your answer with the closed form for \(z(r)\) and its relationship to proper distance along the embedded surface.

EXP-17 · advanced

Beyond This Program: Rotation

This is a short reading-and-reasoning project, not a coding one. Every real astrophysical black hole is expected to rotate, and is therefore better described by the Kerr metric than by Schwarzschild. Research (from Chapter 21 of Gravity from the Ground Up, or another source) how the Kerr event horizon and ergosphere differ from their Schwarzschild counterparts, and identify, specifically, which of this program's four calculations would need the most substantial revision for a rotating hole and which would need the least. Justify each answer in two or three sentences grounded in what actually changes in the metric.

Synthesis

EXP-18 · synthesis

One Black Hole, Four Views

Choose a single mass, say 10 solar masses, and run all four modes for it (for horizons, use --M0 10 --M1 10, the no-accretion case, so the horizon is unambiguous and matches the other three). Write a short, connected paragraph — not four separate answers — that uses the Schwarzschild radius from one run to make sense of a number from each of the other three: from embed, the intrinsic Gaussian curvature \(K(r_s)=-1/(2r_s^2)\) at the throat — a genuine output of the program, printed in the summary and written to the CSV's gaussian_curvature_K_per_m2 column, not merely a quantity discussed in prose — the tidal acceleration at the horizon from tidal, the proper infall time from infall, and the (trivial, in this no-accretion case) horizon radius from horizons. The point of the exercise is the connections, not the individual numbers: in particular, both \(K(r_s)\) and the horizon tidal acceleration scale with mass in a definite way — work out how each one scales with \(M\), and connect that scaling to the shared underlying fact that a bigger black hole is a "flatter," gentler one at its horizon.

EXP-19 · synthesis

Two Independent Axes: Method and Idealisation

A single three-way classification — "exact," "numerical," or "idealised" — might seem like enough to describe any one calculation, but it is not: a calculation cannot be squeezed into just one of the three. method/solution (is the formula a closed form, or does it depend on a numerical integration or root-finding setting?) and physical idealisation (does the calculation model a simplified scenario, regardless of how exactly that scenario's mathematics is solved?) are two independent questions that apply simultaneously to nearly every calculation here. Make a table with two separate axis-columns instead of one: for each of Flamm's paraboloid, the tidal-acceleration formulae, the survival-radius inversion, the radial geodesic equations, the local-speed and redshift formulae, the Vaidya apparent horizon, the outgoing-null-geodesic integration (both the primary backward construction and the secondary forward-shooting one), and the located event horizon itself, record (1) its method/solution axis — exact closed form, or numerically integrated and therefore step-size- or setting-dependent — and (2) its physical-idealisation axis — what about the physical scenario, not the mathematics, is simplified (point particles, no rotation, a prescribed rather than derived mass function, an infinitesimal-separation tidal approximation, and so on). You should find several rows are exact by method and still physically idealised (the Vaidya apparent horizon \(r_{AH}=2M(v)\): pure algebra from your chosen mass function, with no integration or root-finding anywhere, yet idealised because that mass function is prescribed by you rather than derived from real accretion physics) — and that the located event horizon is a genuinely mixed case even on the method axis alone: exact exactly at and after \(v_2\), the boundary condition itself, but RK4-integrated, and therefore step-size- and setting-dependent, everywhere before that (do not classify it as simply "exact"). Separately, you should find at least one row that is idealised on both axes at once (the tidal formula, exact in its curvature coefficient but a linearized, not exact-at-any-order, statement about a finite body — see Exercise EXP-15). Then name the three physical effects you judge most significant among those this program leaves out entirely — candidates include rotation (Kerr geometry), Hawking radiation, radiation reaction / self-force on the infalling particle, the finite strength and self-gravity of a real body in the tidal calculation, and realistic (rather than prescribed) accretion-flow physics — and say, for each, which of the four modes it would change the most.

Domain of Validity

This program is a teaching instrument built entirely from exact general-relativistic formulae. Unlike the stellar-structure programs in this suite, there is no fitted closure anywhere in it to be wary of — but there is a physical scenario being idealised, and a numerical method in horizons whose limits are worth stating plainly.

What is absent

  • Rotation. Every calculation here uses the non-rotating Schwarzschild (or Vaidya) geometry. A real astrophysical black hole is expected to be spinning and is better described by the Kerr metric, whose horizon, ergosphere and innermost stable orbit all differ from their Schwarzschild counterparts.
  • Charge. Astrophysical black holes are expected to be very nearly electrically neutral, so this is a minor omission physically, but it is worth naming: the general static, spherical solution is Reissner-Nordström, of which Schwarzschild is the uncharged special case used throughout this program.
  • Radiation reaction and self-force. The infalling test particle in infall does not lose energy to gravitational radiation as it falls, and does not back-react on the geometry at all; a real infalling mass, especially a comparable-mass one, would do both.
  • Hawking radiation and quantum effects. Entirely classical general relativity throughout; nothing here evaporates.
  • Realistic accretion physics. The mass function in horizons is a simple, explicit ramp chosen by the student. A real accretion flow has its own hydrodynamics, radiative transfer, and angular momentum transport, none of which is modelled; the Vaidya metric used here idealises the infalling matter as a pressureless, purely radial null dust.
  • Finite-size and material effects in the tidal calculation. The tidal formulae are for a point-like separation; a real extended body has its own self-gravity, elasticity and material strength, all of which affect whether and how it is actually torn apart, and none of which this program attempts to model.

What the program captures well is the logic: why an embedding diagram can be quantitatively exact while depicting only an instant of space and nothing about time; why bigger black holes are gentler at the horizon; why a falling observer's own clock and a distant observer's coordinate time tell two consistent but very different stories about the same infall; and why, once a black hole is allowed to change, "the horizon" splits into a local notion you can compute from the here-and-now and a global one that requires knowing the whole future.
