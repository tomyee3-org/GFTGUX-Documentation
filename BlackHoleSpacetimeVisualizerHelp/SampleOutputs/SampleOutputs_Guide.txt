--------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT THIS IS, IN ONE PARAGRAPH
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Black_hole_spacetime_visualizer is a teaching program for "Gravity from
the Ground Up," built entirely from exact Schwarzschild (and, for one
mode, Vaidya) formulae -- no fitted model, no calibration to data. It
gives a student four different quantitative pictures of a black hole,
selected with --mode: the curved space around it (embed), the tidal
forces that would actually harm you (tidal), what falling in looks like
from two different clocks (infall), and the difference between the
"instantaneous" and "eventual" horizon of a black hole that is actively
growing (horizons). This folder contains one sample figure from each mode,
generated directly by the actual program (not mocked up), plus the exact
console text each run printed.

Everything below is organized as: what the figure shows, what to look
for, and why it is scientifically honest -- so Prof. Schutz can assess
the physics and Mr. Higgs can assess the pedagogy and presentation
without needing to read source code.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------
FILE 1: bh_embed_10.00Msun_....png
Command:  python main.py --mode embed --M 10
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT IT SHOWS
  Flamm's paraboloid: the curved spatial geometry around a 10-solar-mass
  black hole, drawn two ways -- a 2-D radial cross-section (left) and the
  actual 3-D surface of revolution (right). This is the one "rubber sheet"
  picture of a black hole that is quantitatively exact, not an artist's
  impression: every distance measured along the drawn surface is the
  true proper distance from the Schwarzschild metric.

WHAT TO LOOK FOR
  The vertical tangent where the surface meets r = r_s (the horizon) --
  labelled directly on the plot as "the throat, not a numerical
  artefact." That flare is real geometry, not a rendering glitch. Note
  what is explicitly NOT claimed: this is a single instant of space, not
  spacetime, and it says nothing about gravity as a force or about the
  interior of the hole.

WHY IT'S HONEST
  The console output (see run_logs.txt) reports the exact Schwarzschild
  radius for this mass (29.54 km) and states plainly that the throat's
  vertical tangent is a feature of the *embedding's slope*, not of the
  slice's intrinsic curvature, which stays finite everywhere plotted.
  This distinction -- the thing that most popular explanations get wrong
  -- is stated directly in the program's own documentation, not left for
  a student to misread from the picture.

FILE 2: bh_tidal_10.00Msun_....png
Command:  python main.py --mode tidal --M 10 --compare_masses 10,4.31e6
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT IT SHOWS
  Four panels. Top two: tidal (stretching/compressing) acceleration
  falling off as 1/r^3 near a 10-solar-mass hole, in physical units and
  in multiples of g. Bottom two: the same tidal acceleration compared
  across black-hole masses -- specifically a stellar-mass hole (10 Msun)
  against Sgr A*, the 4.31-million-solar-mass hole at the centre of our
  own galaxy.

WHAT TO LOOK FOR
  The bottom-left panel's clean power law: tidal acceleration at the
  horizon falls as M^-2, so bigger holes are gentler, not fiercer, at
  their horizon -- the exact opposite of most people's intuition. The
  printed comparison table (run_logs.txt, lines 31-34) makes the point
  concretely: a 10-solar-mass hole subjects a 1.8 m body to 1.9x10^7 g at
  the horizon (destroyed long before crossing, by the program's
  illustrative 100 g threshold), while Sgr A* manages only about
  1.0x10^-4 g there -- gentle enough that this criterion alone would not
  tell you that you had crossed.

WHY IT'S HONEST
  The 100 g line is labelled "illustrative survival limit" everywhere it
  appears -- in the plot legend, the console table header, and the help
  file -- never presented as a real physiological threshold. The program
  also flags, automatically, that 4.31x10^6 Msun sits inside the
  observationally known black-hole mass range while making clear that
  general relativity itself places no bound on the mass at all.

FILE 3: bh_infall_10.00Msun_....png
Command:  python main.py --mode infall --M 10 --r0_rs 6
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT IT SHOWS
  A test particle released from rest at six Schwarzschild radii and
  dropped straight in, followed on four panels: its radius against its
  own proper time; the distant (Schwarzschild-coordinate) time against
  that same proper time; its local infall speed; and the redshift of
  light it sends outward, as received far away.

WHAT TO LOOK FOR
  Panel 1 (top-left): the particle reaches 1.0005 r_s -- just outside the
  horizon, where this run deliberately stops -- after a finite, entirely
  unremarkable 2.205 ms of its own time. Panel 2 (top-right): over that
  same interval, the distant Schwarzschild coordinate time has already
  raced ahead to 3.388 ms and is visibly steepening -- it diverges as the
  horizon is approached, which is why the run stops just short of it.
  Panel 4 (bottom-right, log scale): the redshift factor has fallen to
  about 2.7x10^-4 by the same point. Together, these four panels are the
  clearest way to show a class that "falling in" means two entirely
  different, non-contradictory things depending on whose clock you ask.

WHY IT'S HONEST
  The annotation on panel 1 says explicitly that the run reaches
  r = 1.0005 r_s, not the horizon itself, and that the true horizon
  crossing happens at a slightly larger, finite proper time that this
  particular run does not compute (a companion exercise checks that
  omitted number against an exact closed-form solution). This numerical
  integrator was independently re-benchmarked against that exact
  closed-form solution and agrees to a few parts in 10^7 -- see the
  accompanying audit report for the verification.

FILE 4: bh_horizons_5.00to10.00Msun_....png
Command:  python main.py --mode horizons --M0 5 --M1 10
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT IT SHOWS
  This is the program's signature calculation: a black hole doubling its
  mass by swallowing an infalling shell (the ingoing Vaidya spacetime),
  showing the difference between the apparent horizon (orange dashed --
  read directly off the mass at each instant) and the event horizon
  (black solid -- the true, teleological horizon, located by firing
  trial light rays and finding the one that neither escapes nor falls
  in). The right panel shows that shooting method directly: green rays
  escape, red rays plunge, and the black curve is the boundary between
  them.

WHAT TO LOOK FOR
  In the left panel, the event horizon is already visibly rising before
  the shaded accretion window even begins -- it has to anticipate mass
  that has not arrived yet, because it is defined by the entire future of
  the spacetime, not by conditions at that instant. This is the single
  most concrete way to show a class why "the event horizon" and "the
  apparent horizon" are different objects the moment a black hole is
  allowed to change, and it is normally taught with words alone.

WHY IT'S HONEST
  The console output is unusually careful to separate two different
  numbers that are easy to conflate: the bisection's own precision
  (1.2x10^-16 r_s0 here -- essentially floating-point noise) versus how
  far in advance of the accretion the shooting method was started, which
  is the number that actually controls how trustworthy the located
  horizon is. The program states outright that the first number is not a
  measure of the second, and a companion exercise has students
  demonstrate that directly.

FOR PROF. SCHUTZ -- scientific integrity at a glance
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Every formula behind these four figures is an exact consequence of the
Schwarzschild metric (embed, tidal) or, for horizons, its ingoing-Vaidya
generalisation -- there is no fitted closure or calibration anywhere in
this module, unlike the stellar-structure programs in this same suite.
What is idealised is only the physical scenario: point test particles and
rods, purely radial free fall, and, in horizons, a mass function chosen
by the student rather than derived from a real accretion calculation. The
full help file (Black_hole_spacetime_visualizer.html, delivered
separately) tags every individual result as either an exact closed form,
a numerically integrated quantity with a stated and checkable error, or
an algebraic derivation -- so a student is always told which kind of
number they are looking at. This program went through two independent
critique passes (one from ChatGPT, one from Microsoft Copilot) followed
by a further audit and repair pass; the full audit report is available on
request and documents every issue found and how each was resolved,
including two numerical-honesty defects (a mislabelled stopping point in
the infall plot, and an overclaimed "fainter" from a redshift-only
calculation) that were caught only by rendering and inspecting the actual
output images, not by reading the code.

FOR MR. HIGGS -- what this adds to the book's program suite
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
This is one program covering four of the most requested "show me a black
hole" topics in a single, consistent interface and visual style (matching
the existing StellarEvolutionTracks help file already in the suite). A
student runs one command per mode and gets a publication-quality multi-
panel figure, a printed plain-language summary, and optional CSV data for
further analysis -- all three outputs happen from the same run, and none
is required. The accompanying help file includes 19 tiered exercises
(introductory through synthesis) plus worked example commands, so it is
classroom-ready, not just a code sample. The four sample images in this
folder are exactly what a student would see on their own screen from the
example commands already quoted in the help file's "Four Modes" section
-- nothing here was staged differently for this guide.
