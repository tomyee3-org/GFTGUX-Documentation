Description

GravitationalWaveSources is a numerical investigation of the leading gravitational-wave signal from a quasi-circular compact binary: the orbit is treated as circular at each instant while it shrinks gradually under gravitational-wave emission. It integrates the quadrupole-order frequency evolution from a chosen starting frequency to the Schwarzschild innermost-stable-circular-orbit (ISCO) cutoff and plots an illustrative strain waveform, its amplitude scale, and the instantaneous gravitational-wave frequency.

The default run uses two 1.4-solar-mass components at 400 Mpc, beginning at 20 Hz. In the leading-order model this gives about 158 seconds of inspiral before the ISCO cutoff. The code deliberately stops the physical inspiral model there; it does not claim to model the highly relativistic merger itself.

Optional ringdown: --ringdown appends a deliberately simplified Schwarzschild quasi-normal-mode (QNM) signal after the ISCO cutoff. This is useful for illustrating damped ringing, but it is not a general merger model. In particular, a binary-neutron-star merger need not promptly form a black hole, and a realistic binary-black-hole remnant generally rotates, so its ringdown differs from the Schwarzschild approximation used here. To keep this visible on the plot itself and not only in this Help file, the figure's annotation box carries the same reminder whenever --ringdown is used: "illustrative ringdown -- not a physical merger."

Physical Background

What the Plotted Strain Represents

A passing gravitational wave produces a fractional change in separation of freely falling test masses. The program plots a dimensionless strain scale. A strain of \(10^{-21}\), for example, corresponds to a fractional length change of one part in \(10^{21}\).

The amplitude used here is the standard Newtonian quadrupole face-on amplitude scale. The plotted \(h(t)\) is a representative dimensionless sinusoidal strain scale, not the predicted strain in a particular detector channel. It is not a sky-and-polarisation average and does not include a detector antenna pattern, source inclination, cosmological redshift, higher harmonics, spins, tidal effects, or higher-order post-Newtonian corrections to the inspiral. Those corrections become increasingly important as the binary approaches the strong-field regime, especially for heavier or more unequal-mass systems. The waveform should therefore be interpreted as a pedagogical signal, not as a matched-filter template for LIGO, Virgo, or KAGRA data.

Chirp Mass

The leading frequency evolution depends especially strongly on the chirp mass

Chirp mass
\[\mathcal{M}=\frac{(m_1m_2)^{3/5}}{(m_1+m_2)^{1/5}}.\]

At a fixed gravitational-wave frequency, increasing \(\mathcal{M}\) increases both the rate at which the frequency rises and the leading-order strain amplitude.

ISCO Cutoff and Optional Ringdown

The inspiral is terminated when the gravitational-wave frequency reaches the Schwarzschild ISCO value computed from the total mass. Calling this point an ISCO cutoff, rather than the merger time, is important: the simple quadrupole inspiral is no longer reliable in the true merger regime.

When --ringdown is requested, the program assumes for illustration that the remnant is a nonrotating black hole with 95% of the original total mass and appends its fundamental quadrupolar QNM. This option is most physically suggestive for black-hole-binary examples, not for the default binary-neutron-star case. The 5% mass loss and zero-spin assumptions are intentionally simple teaching choices, not general predictions for arbitrary binaries. The ringdown is attached continuously in strain and phase at the cutoff, but it is not a physical inspiral-merger-ringdown match in derivative or radiated energy flux.

Governing Equations

Frequency Sweep

Leading quadrupole chirp
\[\frac{df}{dt}=\frac{96}{5}\pi^{8/3}\left(\frac{G\mathcal{M}}{c^3}\right)^{5/3}f^{11/3}.\]

The steep \(f^{11/3}\) dependence causes the familiar chirp: the cycles become progressively shorter as the binary evolves.

Strain-Amplitude Scale

Newtonian face-on amplitude scale
\[A(f)=\frac{4(G\mathcal{M})^{5/3}(\pi f)^{2/3}}{c^4 d}.\]

The program plots \(h(t)=A(t)\cos\Phi(t)\), where the gravitational-wave phase obeys \(d\Phi/dt=2\pi f\). The frequency and phase are advanced together with RK4. Because the late-time phase is sensitive to timestep, numerical convergence should be checked with smaller \(\Delta t\) when the final cycles are important.

ISCO Frequency

Schwarzschild ISCO cutoff
\[f_{\rm ISCO}=\frac{c^3}{6^{3/2}\pi GM},\qquad M=m_1+m_2.\]

Leading-Order Inspiral Time

Time between two GW frequencies
\[\Delta t=\frac{5}{256\pi^{8/3}}\left(\frac{G\mathcal{M}}{c^3}\right)^{-5/3}\left(f_1^{-8/3}-f_2^{-8/3}\right).\]

The program uses this analytic result to estimate the number of required integration steps and to reject impractically large runs before allocating large arrays.

Illustrative Schwarzschild QNM

For the optional ringdown, the fundamental gravitational \(\ell=2\) Schwarzschild mode is approximated by \(M\omega\simeq0.3737-0.0890i\). The real part gives the oscillation frequency and the imaginary part gives the exponential decay time.

Physical constants used. \(G=6.674\,30\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}\), \(c=2.997\,924\,58\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) (exact by SI definition), \(1\,\mathrm{Mpc}=3.085\,677\,581\,49\times10^{22}\,\mathrm{m}\), and \(M_\odot=1.988\,92\times10^{30}\,\mathrm{kg}\). This program multiplies that independently adopted \(M_\odot\) by \(G\) wherever a geometrized solar mass is needed, rather than using the IAU 2015 Resolution B3 nominal solar-mass parameter \(GM_\odot^{\rm N}=1.327\,124\,4\times10^{20}\,\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}\) directly; the two differ by about \(0.026\%\), small enough to leave every conclusion in this tutorial unchanged but large enough to shift the last displayed digit of quantities such as \(f_{\rm ISCO}\). The value is named explicitly here, and referenced by variable name (physics_gw.M_sun) in EXP-7, so a value a student computes by hand with a different published \(M_\odot\) can be understood rather than mistaken for an error.

Algorithm

  1. Validate masses, distance, starting frequency, and timestep bounds; ringdown-only settings (--n_tau, --rd_pts) are validated only when --ringdown is requested, so an unused or out-of-range ringdown setting never affects a pure-inspiral run.
  2. Convert masses and distance to SI units and compute total mass, chirp mass, and the Schwarzschild ISCO frequency.
  3. If --ringdown is requested, compute the illustrative remnant mass and QNM parameters from the total mass and validate that --n_tau/--rd_pts can resolve the QNM oscillation -- deliberately before estimating or performing the inspiral integration below, so an invalid ringdown request is rejected immediately rather than after the (potentially long) inspiral has already been computed.
  4. Require the starting frequency to lie below the ISCO cutoff and estimate the requested number of inspiral steps.
  5. Integrate the coupled equations for \(f(t)\) and phase \(\Phi(t)\) with fourth-order Runge–Kutta.
  6. Evaluate the leading-order amplitude scale and strain at every stored inspiral point.
  7. After an RK4 trial step crosses the cutoff, linearly interpolate within that final step so the stored inspiral terminates at the ISCO frequency rather than overshooting it. This is a pedagogical cutoff, not a high-precision event finder: away from the cutoff the RK4 stepper itself converges at the expected fourth order, but the single linearly-interpolated final step means the reported time to ISCO converges more slowly than fourth order as --dt is reduced. A convergence study aimed at demonstrating RK4's fourth-order accuracy should compare a quantity sampled well before the cutoff (for example the frequency or phase at a fixed early time) rather than the time to ISCO itself.
  8. If --ringdown is requested, append the illustrative damped Schwarzschild QNM continuously in strain and phase at the cutoff, using the QNM parameters already computed and validated in step 3.
  9. Print diagnostics; then, after confirming the requested zoom window (--t_before/--t_after) is not empty for the computed data, save a timestamped CSV copy of the numerical arrays if --csvdir is given. Checking the zoom window before writing keeps output all-or-nothing: a run that will go on to fail the zoom check below does not leave a CSV file behind.
  10. Render the three-panel figure on screen; if --outdir is given, additionally save a timestamped PNG copy to that folder.
Input and runtime safeguards: masses, distance, timestep, and starting frequency must be positive and finite; f_start must be below the computed ISCO frequency; the timestep must at least satisfy the Nyquist sampling requirement at the ISCO cutoff; grossly under-resolved inspirals with fewer than about 50 estimated steps are rejected; zoom widths must be non-negative; when ringdown is requested, ringdown counts must be valid integers, ringdown sampling is capped at 500,000 points, and --n_tau together with --rd_pts must resolve at least 8 samples per QNM oscillation cycle, all checked before the inspiral is integrated; --dpi is capped at 600; and requests exceeding five million inspiral steps are rejected with an explanatory error. A run approaching that five-million-step ceiling can require substantial memory (order of 1 GiB) in addition to whatever else is running on your machine, so prefer starting from smaller step counts unless you know your machine has ample RAM to spare. Saved CSV and PNG filenames carry a microsecond timestamp and are claimed atomically before being written, so two rapid saves to the same folder cannot silently overwrite one another. Passing these checks does not replace a convergence test.

Module Overview

Physics Layer
physics_gw.py

Defines constants and the leading-order inspiral equations, advances frequency and phase with RK4, estimates inspiral duration, performs physical/numerical validation, and optionally appends the illustrative Schwarzschild QNM.

Driver Layer
driver_gw.py

Validates plotting inputs, runs the physics calculation, prints the summary, optionally writes the numerical arrays to a timestamped CSV file, and passes the result to the plotter.

Plot Layer
plot_gw.py

Produces the waveform, amplitude-scale, and frequency panels; marks the ISCO cutoff; shows the optional QNM separately; always displays the figure on screen; and, when --outdir is given, additionally saves a timestamped PNG.

Entry Point
main.py

Defines the student-facing command-line options and converts validation or runtime failures into concise command-line error messages.

Requires Python 3.10 or later. See the GFTGU Python Setup Guide if you need to install or update Python.
Build identifier provenance: BUILD_ID is computed at import time from the UTF-8 source text of the four core modules listed above (physics_gw.py, driver_gw.py, main.py, plot_gw.py), after normalizing CRLF/CR line endings to LF and framing each file by name and normalized length -- so two otherwise-identical source trees that differ only in line-ending convention intentionally produce the same BUILD_ID. Editing the Help file or the test suite never changes it. If the program is run from a frozen, zipped, or otherwise relocated distribution where those four files cannot be read from beside physics_gw.py on disk, BUILD_ID reports the literal string unknown instead of failing to run. MODEL_VERSION is unaffected and remains reliable in that situation.

Suggested Experiments

The exercises below are arranged in approximate order of difficulty. The first few emphasize direct observation and simple proportionalities; later exercises require controlled comparisons, numerical convergence, or interpretation of model limitations.

EXP-1 · introductory

Identify the Chirp

Run python main.py. Record the chirp mass, time to ISCO, and ISCO frequency. Identify which panel shows the increasing frequency directly and which panel isolates the increasing amplitude scale. Then explain how both changes appear together in the waveform panel as the cutoff approaches.

EXP-2 · introductory

Verify Inverse-Distance Scaling

Repeat the default calculation with --d 100, 200, 400, and 800. Record the strain scale at ISCO and test quantitatively whether doubling the distance halves it. Confirm that the chirp time and ISCO frequency do not change.

EXP-3 · introductory/intermediate

Starting Frequency and Time in Band

Compare --f_start 20, 30, and 40 for the default masses. Record the time to ISCO, compare the ratios of the times as well as their absolute differences, and explain why raising the low-frequency cutoff removes a disproportionately large fraction of the signal duration.

EXP-4 · intermediate

Chirp Mass Controls the Sweep

Compare 1.4+1.4, 10+10, and 36+29 solar-mass systems at the same distance and starting frequency. Record chirp mass, total mass, ISCO frequency, and time to ISCO. Because both chirp mass and total mass change in this comparison, distinguish the faster frequency sweep associated with larger chirp mass from the lower ISCO cutoff associated with larger total mass.

EXP-5 · intermediate

Mass Ratio at Fixed Total Mass

Compare 20+20, 25+15, and 30+10 solar masses at the same distance and start frequency. Make a table of total mass, chirp mass, ISCO frequency, time to ISCO, and strain scale at ISCO. Verify that \(f_{\rm ISCO}\) stays fixed because the total mass is fixed, while the chirp time and amplitude change with chirp mass.

EXP-6 · intermediate

Test the Inspiral-Time Power Law

For the default binary, compare several starting frequencies such as 20, 25, 30, and 40 Hz. Using the printed times, test the prediction \(T\propto f_{\rm start}^{-8/3}\) by comparing time ratios with the corresponding \(f_{\rm start}^{-8/3}\) ratios (or by finding the slope of a log-log plot). Explain why the finite ISCO-frequency term causes a small departure from an exact power law.

EXP-7 · intermediate

Extract Chirp Mass from the Frequency Sweep

This is the exercise the underlying physics is named for: recovering \(\mathcal{M}\) from an observed frequency sweep, using the same leading-order relation LIGO/Virgo used to estimate the chirp mass of GW150914 from its measured \(f\) and \(df/dt\), rather than computing \(\mathcal{M}\) forward from already-known component masses. Note what this exercise is not: it is a noiseless self-consistency check using two points generated by the exact model being tested, not a reproduction of the real observational pipeline, which must additionally handle detector noise, calibration, detector response, and higher-order waveform systematics.

Run python main.py --csvdir ./runs for the default binary and open the resulting CSV (data rows follow the commented # metadata lines and the t_s,f_hz,A,h,phase_rad header). Take the first data row and a row about 0.002 s later, and run this exact snippet with their t_s/f_hz values filled in:

import physics_gw as gw

t1, f1 = 0.0, 20.0                       # first row: t_s, f_hz
t2, f2 = 0.0020000000000000005, 20.000095056743174   # a later row

fdot_est = (f2 - f1) / (t2 - t1)
f_mid = 0.5 * (f1 + f2)
Mc_kg = gw.chirp_mass_from_fdot(f_mid, fdot_est)
print(f"{Mc_kg / gw.M_sun:.4f}")   # gw.M_sun is this program's adopted M_sun --
                                     # see "Physical constants used" under Governing Equations

For the unmodified default run this prints 1.2188, matching the chirp mass already in the run's own summary to 4 decimal places -- note the explicit division by gw.M_sun, since a different published solar-mass value can shift the last displayed digit. Repeat using a wider time separation between the two rows and explain why the estimate changes slightly; this is the same finite-difference-accuracy trade-off explored more formally in EXP-8.

EXP-8 · intermediate/advanced

Numerical Convergence

Use the 36+29 \(M_\odot\) example for a short run and repeat it with --dt 8e-4, 4e-4, 2e-4, and 1e-4, using the same --t_before 0.15 zoom. Compare the printed time to ISCO and the locations of the final waveform peaks relative to the cutoff, and confirm the differences shrink as --dt is reduced. Because the printed time to ISCO is set by a single linearly-interpolated final step rather than the RK4 stepper alone, do not expect it to shrink by a clean factor of 16 each time --dt is halved; that classic fourth-order reduction is a property of the RK4 stepper itself and is best seen by comparing the frequency at a fixed early time (well before the cutoff) across the same --dt values instead. Add --csvdir ./runs to each of the four runs and read off \(f\) at the same fixed early time from each CSV (interpolating between the two rows nearest that time if it does not fall exactly on a stored sample) to get the precise numbers this comparison needs, rather than reading them off the plot. Each exported file records its own dt_s (along with the other run parameters) in its commented metadata block, so the four files remain individually identifiable even after being renamed, moved, or separated from the terminal session that produced them -- open one on its own and it still says which timestep it came from. Decide when further timestep reduction ceases to make a meaningful difference. Do not use plot smoothness alone as the accuracy test.

EXP-9 · advanced

Illustrative Black-Hole Ringdown

For the black-hole-binary example, run python main.py --m1 36 --m2 29 --d 440 --ringdown --t_before 0.2 --t_after 0.05. Measure the time between neighboring ringdown peaks (preferably averaging over several cycles), convert it to a frequency, and compare with the printed QNM frequency. Then explain why the transition at ISCO should not be interpreted as a realistic merger waveform.

EXP-10 · advanced

QNM Scaling with Remnant Mass

Enable ringdown for equal-mass black-hole binaries such as 20+20, 40+40, and 80+80 \(M_\odot\), keeping the mass ratio fixed while scaling the total mass. Compare the reported QNM frequencies and decay times. Test the Schwarzschild-model prediction that frequency scales as \(1/M_f\) while the decay time scales as \(M_f\).

EXP-11 · synthesis

Map the Model's Domain of Validity

Make a table listing which ingredients of the displayed signal are included at leading order and which are omitted or idealized. Classify at least spins, orbital eccentricity, source orientation, detector response, cosmological redshift, neutron-star tides, merger dynamics, and remnant spin. Explain which omissions matter most for early inspiral, late inspiral, and ringdown.
