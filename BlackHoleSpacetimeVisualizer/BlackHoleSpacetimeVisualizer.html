Description

BlackHoleSpacetimeVisualizer takes the single most striking prediction of general relativity — the black hole — and gives a student four different, quantitative ways to look at it. One program covers four calculations, selected with --mode: embed draws the embedding diagram of the curved spatial geometry around the hole; tidal computes the stretching and compressing tidal acceleration a body feels at a given radius, and compares that across black-hole masses; infall follows a test particle dropped from rest to just outside the horizon, in both its own time and a distant observer's; and horizons makes visible a distinction that is usually only stated in words — the difference between the local, instantaneous apparent horizon and the global, teleological event horizon of a black hole that is actively gaining mass.

The following Beats answer four questions: what space looks like on a chosen slice; how strongly nearby worldlines pull apart; whose clock measures a fall; and which outgoing light ray separates escape from capture after a black hole grows.

The Schwarzschild line element:
\[ds^2 = -\left(1-\frac{r_s}{r}\right)c^2dt^2 + \frac{dr^2}{1-r_s/r} + r^2\left(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right), \qquad r_s = \frac{2GM}{c^2}.\]

Every formula in this program follows from this line element (or, for horizons, from its ingoing-null-coordinate generalisation below), together with the geodesic equation for a freely falling test particle or photon. Nothing here is fitted to data or calibrated against a more detailed model.

Before the first run

From the folder with main.py, run python main.py --version and compare with the stamp above. Install NumPy and Matplotlib if needed. A run displays its figure; --outdir ./figures additionally saves a PNG. For a numerical table add --csvdir ./data. A CSV-only run requires both --no_plot --csvdir ./data.

Beat 0 · Space

The full Schwarzschild spacetime is four-dimensional and cannot be embedded in ordinary 3-D space at all. But a single instant of it — the spatial geometry alone, at fixed Schwarzschild time \(t\), restricted to the equatorial plane — is a 2-D curved surface, and that can be embedded exactly in flat 3-D Euclidean space. The resulting picture, Flamm's paraboloid, is not a metaphor or an artist's impression: every distance measured along the drawn surface equals the corresponding proper distance computed from the Schwarzschild metric. It is the one genuinely quantitative "rubber sheet" picture of a black hole, and it is worth being precise about what it does and does not depict: it says nothing about time, nothing about gravity as a force, and the popular image of a ball rolling toward the centre to represent gravitational attraction is not part of what the embedding actually shows.

Embedding profile:

Restrict the metric to \(t=\text{const}\), \(\theta=\pi/2\): \(ds^2 = dr^2/(1-r_s/r) + r^2 d\phi^2\). Embed this as a surface of revolution \(z(r)\) in flat 3-D space, where \(ds^2=dz^2+dr^2+r^2d\phi^2\):

\[\left(\frac{dz}{dr}\right)^2 = \frac{r_s}{r-r_s} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad z(r) = 2\sqrt{r_s(r-r_s)}, \qquad r\ge r_s.\]

The default run (--mode embed) draws Flamm's paraboloid for a ten solar-mass black hole, with Schwarzschild radius \(29.5\,\mathrm{km}\) and throat at \(r=r_s\).

python main.py --mode embed --M 10

Output: The embedding of the equatorial, constant-time Schwarzschild spatial slice as a surface of revolution in flat 3-D space: Flamm's paraboloid.

Where is the narrowest ring? What part of the picture has been mirrored?

Read the radial scale first. The left panel is a cross-section; the right revolves it into Flamm's paraboloid.

The derivative diverges as \(r\to r_s\): the throat has a vertical tangent, a real geometric feature, not a numerical artefact. The embedding is undefined for \(r \lt r_s\). The dashed lower profile only helps you see the shape: nothing here corresponds to a passage through to "another side," a picture that belongs to the different, larger maximally-extended (Kruskal) spacetime.

The equation this picture illustrates is \(z=2 \sqrt{r_s(r-r_s)}\) for the upper branch, with \(r_s=2GM/c^2\). Its height lives in an auxiliary drawing space. If you need a distance along the surface, use the CSV's proper_radial_distance column instead of reading the vertical axis as a physical altitude.

Beat 1 · Tides

Crossing a black hole's event horizon is not, by itself, locally violent: for a large enough hole nothing dramatic happens right at the horizon. What can kill you is the tidal force — the difference in gravitational acceleration between your head and your feet — and that difference depends on the size of the hole in a way most people find surprising. Because the Schwarzschild radius grows linearly with mass while the tidal acceleration at that radius falls as the inverse square of the mass, a bigger black hole is gentler at its horizon, not fiercer. This program lets a student verify that scaling directly rather than take it on faith.

Tidal acceleration:

Two nearby free-falling test particles separated by proper distance \(\delta\ell\) accelerate relative to each other according to the geodesic deviation equation, governed by the Riemann tensor. For Schwarzschild, in the local orthonormal frame of an observer at radius \(r\) (static or radially infalling — the radial components turn out to be the same for both):

\[a_{\rm radial} = \frac{2GM}{r^3}\,\delta \ell \quad(\text{stretching: the two ends pull apart}), \] \[a_{\rm tangential} = \frac{GM}{r^3}\,\delta\ell \quad(\text{compressing: the two ends push together, each transverse direction}).\]

Radial stretching and transverse compression are magnitudes for a small proper separation along the relevant local direction. At each mass's own \(r_s\propto M\), the radial magnitude scales as \(M^{-2}\).

The following compares tidal accelerations around a 10 solar mass black hole versus those around a supermassive black hole: 
python main.py --mode tidal --M 10 --compare_masses 10,4.31e6

Output: Radial (stretching) and tangential (compressing) tidal acceleration between the two ends of a test rod, vs. radius. In addition, compares the tidal acceleration at the horizon and a "survival radius" across the input list of black-hole masses. The console summary also prints linearization_ratio, the rod length divided by the smallest sampled radius; EXP-9 uses it.

A 10 solar mass hole gives a radial tidal acceleration of \(1.9\times10^7\,g\) at the horizon — solidly lethal — while the supermassive black hole at the centre of the Milky Way, Sgr A* at \(4.31\times10^6\,M_\odot\), manages only about \(10^{-4}\,g\) there: gentle enough that, by this criterion alone, you would not notice crossing its horizon.

Illustrative survival radius:
\[r_{\rm crit} = \left(\frac{2GM\,\delta\ell}{a_{\rm limit}}\right)^{1/3}, \qquad a_{\rm limit} = \texttt{limit\_g}\times g.\]

Solving \(a_{\rm radial}=a_{\rm limit}\) for \(r\) gives the radius at which a body of length \(\delta\ell\) first experiences a chosen illustrative tidal-acceleration threshold. Comparing \(r_{\rm crit}\) with \(r_s\) tells you whether that threshold is reached outside the horizon (the body is destroyed before crossing) or only inside it (the body crosses intact, by this criterion). \(\texttt{limit\_g}\) is an illustrative number (set to \(100\,g\) in the plot), not a precise claim of whether a real body survives.

Beat 2 · Infall

A particle falling into a Schwarzschild black hole experiences nothing pathological at the horizon: its proper time runs smoothly and it crosses in a finite, ordinary interval. A distant observer never actually sees the crossing happen.

Schwarzschild coordinate time \(t\) is normalized to the clock of a static observer at infinity. It diverges as the infaller approaches the horizon, while the infaller's own elapsed proper time stays finite. Signals emitted closer and closer to the horizon reach a distant observer progressively later and with lower frequency. Each outward signal emitted at a fixed radius outside the horizon can arrive in finite time; the limiting signal from the crossing does not escape.

This is the precise sense in which an object falling into a black hole is said to "freeze and fade" as seen from outside, even though nothing freezes from the particle's own point of view.

Radial geodesic equations:

For a particle released from rest at \(r_0\), the conserved specific energy is \(E=\sqrt{1-r_s/r_0}\) (\(E\to1\) as \(r_0\to\infty\)). The exact radial equations of motion are:

\[\left(\frac{dr}{d\tau}\right)^2 = c^2\left(\frac{r_s}{r}-\frac{r_s}{r_0}\right), \qquad \frac{dt}{d\tau} = \frac{E}{1-r_s/r}.\]

\(dr/d\tau\) is finite and smooth all the way through \(r=r_s\): nothing pathological happens to the particle there. \(dt/d\tau\) diverges as \(r\to r_s\): this is what makes the distant observer's coordinate-time coordinate run away.

Which clock gives the infaller's own elapsed time, and which coordinate time grows steeply as the endpoint approaches \(r_s\)?

python main.py --mode infall --M 10 --r0_rs 6

Output: Given a test particle released from rest at some radius and dropped radially inward: its radius, proper time, the distant observer's Schwarzschild coordinate time, its local speed relative to static observers along the way, and the redshift of light it sends outward.

The integration deliberately stops just outside the horizon, at \(r_{\rm stop}=\)--r_stop_rs\(\times r_s\) (default 1.0005), because the Schwarzschild \(t\) coordinate used for the distant observer's clock diverges there; the particle's own \(\tau\) does not, and nothing in the underlying physics is singular at the horizon itself — only this particular coordinate choice is.

Read the proper time \(\tau\), Schwarzschild time \(t\), local speed and outward light's received/emitted frequency ratio. The reason why the two times separate can be seen from the radial equations of motion, with \(E=\sqrt{1-r_s/r_0}\). The coordinate-time rate \(dt/d\tau\) diverges at the horizon in Schwarzschild coordinates. The particle's own time stays finite. “Local static observer” has meaning only outside the horizon.

Local speed and redshift:
\[\beta(r)\equiv\frac{v_{\rm local}(r)}{c} =\frac{1}{E}\sqrt{\frac{r_s}{r}-\frac{r_s}{r_0}} \longrightarrow 1\quad(r\to r_s^+),\] \[\frac{\nu_{\rm obs}}{\nu_{\rm emit}} =\sqrt{1-\frac{r_s}{r}} \sqrt{\frac{1-\beta}{1+\beta}}.\]

The redshift factor is the product of the ordinary gravitational redshift for a static source at \(r\) and the special-relativistic Doppler factor for that source's local motion relative to a static observer it is passing. For a particle released from rest at infinity (\(E=1\)) this collapses to the well-known closed form \(\nu_{\rm obs}/\nu_{\rm emit}=1-\sqrt{r_s/r}\).
Note: In the CSV, \(\beta(r)\) corresponds to the v_local_over_c column.

Beat 3 · Horizon

In an unchanging (static) black hole spacetime, two boundaries known as the apparent horizon and the event horizon coincide. Once the hole is allowed to change — by swallowing matter, or, in principle, by evaporating — the two boundaries diverge, and the distinction is one of the more important ideas in modern black-hole physics.

In this spherically symmetric model, the apparent horizon is the round surface where the outward light-ray expansion vanishes: outgoing radial light instantaneously has \(dr/dv=0.\) Here it lies at \(r_{\rm AH}(v)=2M(v),\) in geometrized units, and can be located without knowing the future mass history.

The event horizon is defined globally: it is the boundary of the set of events from which a light ray can never escape to arbitrarily large distance, no matter how long you wait. Answering "can it ever escape" requires knowing the entire future of the spacetime — which is exactly why the event horizon is called teleological, and why, in a dynamical spacetime, it can begin moving in response to infalling matter that has not arrived yet. This program makes that abstract statement into a picture: for a black hole that gains mass over some interval of time, the located event horizon is demonstrably larger than the apparent horizon throughout the accretion, and it starts rising perceptibly before the accretion begins.

The ingoing Vaidya line element: (as a solution of the Einstein field equations for infalling null dust)

In ingoing advanced coordinates, let the mass depend on \(v\):

\[ds^2 = -\left(1-\frac{2M(v)}{r}\right)dv^2 + 2\,dv\,dr + r^2d\Omega^2 \qquad (G=c=1).\]

This program uses a mass function that ramps linearly from \(M_0\) to \(M_1\) between advanced times \(v_1\) and \(v_2\), with \(v_2-v_1\) set by --duration_rs0 in units of \(r_{s0}\), and is constant before and after — a simple, explicit, student-chosen stand-in for an accretion episode, not a result of an accretion-physics calculation.

Apparent horizon:
\[r_{AH}(v) = 2M(v).\]
Outgoing radial null geodesics:
\[\frac{dr}{dv} = \frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{2M(v)}{r}\right).\]

The following models the hole doubling in mass. Note how the event horizon rises before accretion starts: it anticipates the future.

python main.py --mode horizons --M0 5 --M1 10

Output: The event horizon and the apparent horizon of a black hole that gains mass by swallowing an infalling null-dust shell (an ingoing Vaidya spacetime).

The dashed apparent horizon responds to the prescribed local mass, \(r_{\rm AH}(v)=2GM(v)/c^2\). The solid event horizon separates outgoing light that escapes from light that cannot, so its position uses the future mass history. Its early rise is a global property, not information moving backward in time.

Once accretion finishes at \(v_2\), the spacetime is static Schwarzschild with mass \(M_1\), so the event horizon is exactly \(r(v_2)=r_{s1}\). In geometrized units, the program integrates the radial null geodesics equation backward (for numerical stability) from \(r(v_2)=2M_1\) to obtain the earlier event horizon. The apparent horizon is read directly from the mass function. The figure compares these two horizons throughout the accretion episode.

Suggested Experiments

EXP-1 — Introductory
Read the Throat

Run python main.py --mode embed --M 10. Identify the throat in both panels. Why does the profile have a vertical tangent exactly at \(r=r_s\), and why is that not a numerical mistake? The dashed lower branch is a mirror image drawn for visual symmetry only. Do research on the internet to discover why Flamm's paraboloid is not to be confused with depictions of wormholes.

EXP-2 — Introductory
Falling In

Run python main.py --mode infall --M 10 --r0_rs 6. Record the proper time and the Schwarzschild coordinate time at which the integration stops, just outside the horizon. Which one is "how long it took," from the falling observer's own point of view? At roughly what value of \(r/r_s\) does the local infall speed first exceed half the speed of light?

EXP-3 — Introductory
Two Kinds of Horizon

Run python main.py --mode horizons --M0 5 --M1 10 --csvdir ./data. Recall the difference between the apparent horizon and the event horizon. From the CSV, find the first value of \(v/r_{s0}\) at which \(|r_{EH}-r_{AH}|/r_{s0} > 10^{-3}\) — a value at which one could say that the apparent horizon and event horizon become "measurably distinct" — and how does that compare with the beginning of the shaded accretion window? Separately, note that after \(v_2\) the two curves coincide exactly, by construction due to the integration method.

EXP-4 — introductory/intermediate
Coordinate Distance Is Not Proper Distance

Using --mode embed --csvdir ./data, compare the proper_radial_distance column \(\ell(r)\) with the naive coordinate difference \(r-r_s\) at a few radii out to 8 \(r_s\). Report the excess as a percentage of the coordinate difference, i.e. \(100\,\frac{\ell(r)-(r-r_s)}{r-r_s}\). What is that percentage at \(r=2r_s\)? At \(r=8r_s\)? (The default 400-point linear grid happens to land exactly on \(r=2r_s\) and \(r=8r_s\) for --r_max_rs 8; if you change --n_r or --r_max_rs, you will need to interpolate.) State why the two are not equal even though both are "distance from the horizon."

EXP-5 — intermediate
Compressing and Stretching Are Locked Together

Using the tidal CSV for any mass, verify numerically that a_tangential_g is exactly half of a_radial_g at every radius. Prove algebraically, from the two formulae in the equations section, that this ratio holds at every radius for every mass, not just as an approximation.

EXP-6 — intermediate
The Event Horizon's Memory

Run --mode horizons --M0 5 --M1 10 --v_start_margin_rs0 5, then repeat with 10, 15, 20 and 30. Each time, record the value on the printed summary line Event horizon at v_start: r/r_s0 = …; call it \(r_{\rm EH}(v_{\rm start})/r_{s0}\). Plot \(\ln\!\left(r_{\rm EH}(v_{\rm start})/r_{s0}-1\right)\) against --v_start_margin_rs0. The value \(r_{\rm EH}(v_{\rm start})/r_{s0}-1\) at each margin is the physical displacement of the true event horizon above the static radius \(r_{s0}\) at that specific, earlier moment in advanced time (the event horizon equals \(r_{s0}\) only in the strict \(v\to-\infty\) limit). Is the decay with increasing margin consistent with an exponential? This shows the event horizon's teleological character: the horizon "anticipates" accretion that has not happened yet, arbitrarily far in advance, at an exponentially shrinking but never literally zero displacement.

EXP-7 — intermediate
Checking the Infall Integration Against a Closed Form

The radial free-fall equation \((dr/d\tau)^2=c^2(r_s/r-r_s/r_0)\) happens to have an exact closed-form solution, the same "cycloid" parametrisation used for Newtonian radial (zero-angular-momentum) Kepler infall: with \(\eta\) running from 0 (at \(r=r_0\)) to \(\eta_{\rm stop}=\arccos(2r_{\rm stop}/r_0-1)\), \[r(\eta)=\tfrac{r_0}{2}(1+\cos\eta), \qquad \tau(\eta)=\sqrt{\tfrac{r_0^3}{8GM}}\,(\eta+\sin\eta).\] Run --mode infall --M 10 --r0_rs 6 (default --r_stop_rs 1.0005) and compare the printed proper time tau with \(\tau(\eta_{\rm stop})\) from this formula. You should find agreement to better than one part in \(10^5\) even at the coarsest allowed --step_frac 0.2. Now repeat with --step_frac at 0.1, 0.05, 0.02, 0.01 and 0.005, and plot the relative error on a log scale against --step_frac (also on a log scale). You should see strong convergence as --step_frac is refined — roughly third-to-fourth order, consistent with (though not an exact multiple of) RK4's fourth-order accuracy — down to about --step_frac 0.005, where the shrinking RK4 truncation error and the accumulated floating-point round-off from many more, individually tinier steps become comparable in size. Now push further, to --step_frac 0.001, 0.0001 and 0.00002 (near the smallest accepted value, just above \(10^{-5}\)):

As the step size decreases, truncation error initially falls. At sufficiently small steps, accumulated floating-point error can become dominant and the error can grow again. Use your results to identify these regimes. These tests illustrate the limits of the RK4 integrator.

Separately: confirm that the very first row of the infall CSV is exactly \(\tau=0\), \(t=0\), \(r=r_0\) — the actual release event.

Note: For precise comparison, it is recommended that you use the IAU nominal solar mass parameter and the defined speed of light: \[ (GM)^N_\odot = 1.3271244 \times 10^{20}\, \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}, \quad c = 299792458 \,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \qquad \text{(superscript "N" means "nominal")} \]

The exact closed form value of \(\tau\) for this exercise evaluates to \( \tau_{\rm exact} = 2.2048475035379664 \, \mathrm{ms} \)

EXP-8 — intermediate/advanced
Sudden vs. Gradual Accretion

Run --mode horizons --M0 5 --M1 10 --duration_rs0 0.5 (a near-instantaneous mass jump) and compare the event-horizon curve with the default --duration_rs0 10 run. Does the sudden case still rise smoothly before \(v_1\), or does its shape change qualitatively? --n_steps controls only the output sampling/plot grid, not the underlying geodesic integration (which uses its own adaptive step regardless), so raising it can make a plotted curve look smoother but cannot itself resolve a genuinely under-integrated feature. --n_steps changes the output sampling, not the integration accuracy. Using \(|r_{EH}-r_{AH}|/r_{s0}\gt10^{-3}\) as your quantitative criterion for "measurably distinct" (the same threshold used in EXP-3), what would you expect to happen to the anticipation lead-time (how far before \(v_1\) the event horizon first becomes distinct from the apparent horizon by that criterion) as the accretion becomes more sudden? Check your prediction against both runs.

EXP-9 — advanced
Newtonian Tides, Relativistic Reach — and Where "Exact" Stops Applying

By hand, derive the Newtonian tidal acceleration between two points separated by \(\delta r\) along the line to a point mass \(M\) at distance \(r\), starting from \(g(r)=GM/r^2\) and a first-order Taylor expansion. Compare your result term-for-term with the program's \(a_{\rm radial}\) formula: the coefficient \(2GM/r^3\) agrees exactly, term for term, with the corresponding relativistic tidal coefficient. Given that agreement, what precisely does general relativity add to this particular calculation? (Consider what it means that the coefficient is asserted to hold arbitrarily close to, and inside, \(r_s\) — a regime where the Newtonian derivation's own assumptions no longer straightforwardly apply, even though the coefficient survives.) The agreement concerns the local coefficient. To obtain an acceleration, the program multiplies that coefficient by a finite separation \(\delta\ell\), just as your Taylor expansion multiplies it by \(\delta r\). Applying the local coefficient over a finite separation is a leading-order approximation. Neither a first-order finite-difference derivation nor the infinitesimal-separation limit of the geodesic deviation equation establishes an exact result for a finite-length body "at any order"; both are leading-order approximations that happen to be excellent whenever the body is small compared with the local curvature scale. Using the program's linearization_ratio diagnostic (separation divided by the smallest sampled radius), find a mass/separation combination within the program's accepted ranges where that ratio is no longer small, and explain in your own words why the reported tidal acceleration should no longer be trusted as more than indicative there.

EXP-10 — advanced
Proper Distance Near the Throat and Far Away

Using the embed CSV out to a large --r_max_rs (try 50), fit the excess proper distance \(\Delta=\ell(r)-(r-r_s)\) as a function of \(r/r_s\) at large radius. In the embed CSV, r, z and proper_radial_distance (i.e. \(\ell(r)\)) are in meters; r_over_rs is dimensionless. These units are also stated in the first comment. Coarse radial sampling can make the first step of \(\Delta\) (near the horizon) look abrupt. Does \(\Delta\) approach a constant, grow, or shrink as \(r\to\infty\)? Separately, examine the near-horizon behaviour: does your fit, or a direct look at the embedding formula, suggest \(\Delta\) diverges, vanishes, or stays finite as \(r\to r_s^+\)? (Careful: it is the embedding's slope \(dz/dr\) that diverges at the throat, not the proper distance itself, which shrinks to zero there along with \(r-r_s\) — do not assume the answer before you compute it.) Support your answer with the closed form for \(z(r)\) and its relationship to proper distance along the embedded surface.

EXP-11 — advanced
Beyond This Program: Rotation

Every real astrophysical black hole is expected to rotate and is therefore better described by the Kerr metric than by Schwarzschild. Read in your textbook how the Kerr event horizon and ergosphere differ from their Schwarzschild counterparts, and identify how each of these program's four calculations would need to be revised. Justify your answer by performing research on the internet.

Domain of validity

embed is a spatial slice, tidal uses a linearized separation without material strength, and infall is radial motion of a point test particle. The horizons mode prescribes nondecreasing ingoing null-dust accretion rather than simulating a real accretion disk. None of the modes includes rotation.
